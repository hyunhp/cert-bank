AIB-C01 Study Guide: A Business Exam About AI, Where the Right Answer Is Usually the Right Order

The AWS Certified AI Business Strategist exam (AIB-C01) carries an AWS badge, but it is not a service exam: AWS's own exam page says it does not assess AWS services knowledge. Of the 445 AIB-C01 questions in this question bank, only 35 (about 8%) name an AWS service anywhere in the question or its answers. The rest describe a company, a goal and a proposed step, and ask which step a careful leader would take. This guide is about that judgement: what the correct answers have in common, where the tempting wrong answers come from, and the handful of AWS names you still need.

Study guide · AWS Certified AI Business Strategist (AIB-C01)

The exam

The exam guide gives 130 minutes and a minimum passing score of 700 on a scaled score of 100–1,000. The exam is in beta at the time of writing: the AWS exam page lists 85 questions (multiple choice and multiple response) in 170 minutes for the beta. Four domains, almost evenly weighted: AI Fundamentals and Literacy (24%), AI Strategy and Business Value Creation (28%), AI Governance and Responsible AI Leadership (24%), Business Readiness, Leadership, and AI Transformation (24%). The score is compensatory, but with no domain under 24%, skipping one gives away about a quarter of the exam.

The questions are short. In our bank the average AIB-C01 question is about 26 words long, against roughly 45 for the Solutions Architect – Professional exam (SAP-C03). Short does not mean easy: more than half of our questions (55%) are use-case scenarios, and the four answers are often all things a real company might do. The difference is when, and how much.

Three habits the right answers share

1. They do things in the right order

Many questions are really asking "what comes first?". The answer is nearly always the step that makes later steps possible or measurable:

  • Measure a baseline before deployment. Without today's numbers, nobody can prove later that the AI made the difference.
  • Settle consent and privacy before reusing customer data for a new purpose. Technical choices come after that gate.
  • Fix data quality before adding data or a bigger model. A model cannot be better than what it learned from.
  • Pilot, then expand to a limited group, then go enterprise-wide. Skipping the middle stage is the classic failure: scaling too early.
  • Check each jurisdiction's rules before launch, not after the first complaint.

2. They spend in proportion to the need

The cheapest option that actually solves the problem usually wins. For adapting a generative model the ladder is: prompt engineering first; retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) when the model needs current or company-specific facts; fine-tuning only when the need is a lasting change in style or vocabulary that the first two cannot deliver. The same logic applies to build or buy: a company with good data, a small team and a short deadline should use a managed service trained on its own data, not write algorithms from scratch.

3. They name an owner and a measure

A roadmap item without a target outcome, a success metric and a named owner is a wish. The exam's governance questions reward a single accountable person per decision (the "A" in a RACI chart), time-boxed reviews, and proof through audits and outcome metrics. Longer policy documents, bigger committees and launch announcements are recurring wrong answers.

Beginner tip: pick one real process from your own job, such as answering customer emails, and write a one-page AI business case for it: today's baseline (for example, average handling time), the KPI you expect to move, every cost line (licences or API usage, data clean-up, staff time, training, change management), the pilot group, and who owns the decision to scale or stop. Then calculate the ROI. Many Domain 2 and Domain 4 questions are variations of that page, and once you have filled it in yourself, the missing piece in a question stands out.

The arithmetic you must get right

ROI is (benefit − total cost) ÷ total cost. Two traps appear again and again in our explanations. First, subtract before you divide: a gain of 200,000 on an investment of 500,000 is (200,000 − 500,000) ÷ 500,000 = −60%, a loss, not "40% recovered". Second, total cost means total. Leaving out data preparation, staff time or change management makes the ROI look better than it is, and questions that ask you to critique a business case are often testing whether you spot the missing cost line.

Where the tempting wrong answers come from

When two answers both look reasonable, the bank's explanations keep pointing the same way: the staged, measured, risk-reducing option beats the fast, broad or unchecked one. Recognising the shape of a wrong answer is quicker than proving the right one.

Tempting answerWhy it loses
"Roll it out company-wide now; the pilot went well"A pilot proves feasibility, not production readiness. Production needs versioning, monitoring, rollback and a fallback.
"Launch more pilots to build maturity"Maturity grows by evaluating and scaling the pilots you have, not by starting new ones.
"Start from the latest model and look for uses"Opportunities come from business pain mapped with the people who feel it, then tested for AI fit.
"Do not launch until every risk is eliminated"Risk is managed, not removed: red-team the riskiest cases, then release with monitoring and rollback.
"Our edge is the model we use"Anyone can buy the same model. Durable advantage comes from proprietary data, unique processes and trained people.
"Fund every readiness gap equally"Readiness is limited by the weakest pillar (people, process, technology, governance). Fund that one first.

One caution: the "cautious answer" habit is a tie-breaker, not a rule. "Do nothing until the data is perfect" is also a wrong answer; waiting for perfection appears in our explanations as often as rushing does.

The AWS names you still need

Few questions name a service, but those that do expect you to match the name to its job. The exam guide's in-scope list is short: Amazon Bedrock (including pricing tiers, Guardrails and Knowledge Bases), Amazon SageMaker AI, Amazon Quick, the AWS Cloud Adoption Framework (AWS CAF), the shared responsibility model, AWS AI pricing structures, AWS Pricing Calculator, AWS Cost Explorer and AWS Marketplace. Amazon Bedrock is the service our AIB-C01 questions are tagged with most often, followed by Bedrock Guardrails, Amazon SageMaker AI and Bedrock Knowledge Bases. Know them at this level: Bedrock gives access to foundation models through one managed service; Guardrails filters harmful content, denied topics and personal data in prompts and answers; Knowledge Bases provides managed RAG over your documents; Amazon Quick provides AI-powered business assistants (its user guide now calls it AWS Quick; Amazon Q Business, which older material names for this job, is no longer open to new customers); SageMaker Clarify, which a few of our questions use, measures bias and explains individual predictions. Add the shared responsibility line for managed AI: AWS secures the service, you own access, data, safeguards and how outputs are used.

Going deeper: why AWS Artifact cannot prove that you are compliant

Governance questions often ask for "evidence of compliance", and two services sound right. AWS Artifact is a portal where you download AWS's own audit reports and agreements: proof that AWS runs its infrastructure according to standards such as ISO or SOC. It says nothing about how you configured your accounts. AWS Config records the configuration of your resources over time and evaluates it against rules; conformance packs bundle rules for a framework. So the split follows the shared responsibility model: evidence about AWS's side comes from Artifact, evidence that your own controls held comes from Config (with CloudTrail for who did what). A question about an auditor reviewing your AI workload's settings over the last quarter is a Config question. Neither service is on the exam guide's in-scope list, so treat this as background for the governance questions in our bank, not as a service to memorise.

A three-week plan

  1. Week 1: literacy and strategy. AI, ML and generative AI terms; predictive versus generative; the prompt–RAG–fine-tune ladder; use-case prioritisation by value, feasibility and risk; baselines, KPIs, ROI and total cost of ownership.
  2. Week 2: governance and readiness. The responsible AI dimensions the exam guide names (fairness, explainability, privacy, safety, transparency, robustness); governance committees and RACI; model cards and consent; readiness across people, process, technology and governance; maturity models from experimentation to enterprise scale; change management and the staged scaling path (envision, experiment, launch, scale).
  3. Week 3: mixed practice. Do full sets under time. For every miss, write down which habit you broke: order, proportion or ownership. Most misses fall into one of the three.
