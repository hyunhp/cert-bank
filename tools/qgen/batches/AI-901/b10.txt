@ AI-901 D1 T1.1 advanced use-case
S: A pharmacy chain is piloting a voice assistant that reminds older customers to take their medication and answers simple dosage questions. Early tests went well in a quiet lab, but the product owner worries about how the assistant will behave in busy homes and what it does when it is unsure of an answer. Which TWO practices MOST directly support the reliability and safety principle for this pilot? (Select TWO)
A: Test the assistant with background noise, unusual phrasing and unexpected questions before release, and track how often it fails in each case.
B: Send questions the assistant cannot answer confidently to a pharmacist, and monitor real conversations after release for new failure patterns.
C: Publish a plain-language notice that explains which voice recordings are kept, for how long, and how customers can ask for them to be deleted.
D: Compare the assistant's accuracy for male and female customers, and retrain it whenever one group receives noticeably worse dosage answers from it.
E: Name one product manager who approves every new release of the assistant and answers complaints from customers and regulators about its behavior.
K: A,B
G: ## Reliability and safety
Microsoft's Responsible AI Standard says AI systems should "operate reliably, safely, and consistently", "function as designed, respond safely to unexpected conditions, and resist harmful manipulation". Their behavior reflects the range of situations the developers anticipated during design and testing.
In practice the principle asks two questions:
- **Before release**: have we tested the system under the conditions it will really meet (noise, odd wording, rare inputs), and do we know where it fails? Learn's error analysis tooling exists for exactly this: understanding how failures are distributed.
- **When something unexpected happens**: does the system fail safely, for example by handing a doubtful case to a person, and do we keep monitoring it in production?
## Telling the principles apart
A medication assistant touches several principles at once, so ask which risk a practice reduces. Retention notices concern privacy and transparency, comparing groups concerns fairness, and a named owner concerns accountability.
W: A=Test under real-world conditions: testing with noise, unusual phrasing and unexpected questions, and measuring failures per condition, checks that the assistant behaves as designed across the situations it will meet.
B=Safe fallback and monitoring: routing low-confidence answers to a pharmacist makes the system fail safely, and production monitoring catches failure patterns the lab never showed.
X: C=Recording notice: telling customers what is kept and how to delete it supports privacy and security (and transparency about data use), not how reliably the assistant answers.
D=Accuracy by gender: comparing quality of service between groups is a fairness practice; it does not test behavior in noisy homes or define what happens when the assistant is unsure.
E=Named release approver: a named owner who approves releases and answers complaints is an accountability practice; it does not by itself make the assistant behave safely.
T: Reliability and safety means testing under the real range of conditions and designing safe fallbacks plus monitoring for when the system is unsure or fails.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.1 advanced troubleshooting
S: A lender retrains its loan-approval model after removing the gender and age columns, and its data science lead claims the model is now fair. An auditor points out that postcode and job title remain as inputs and asks for evidence before launch. The team has two weeks and cannot collect new data. Which action MOST directly provides the fairness evidence the auditor wants?
A: Compare approval and error rates across gender and age groups on held-out data, and mitigate any gaps that exceed agreed limits
B: Remove postcode and job title as well, because a model that never sees these inputs cannot treat any group of applicants differently
C: Publish a document that lists every input column and explains in plain language how the model weighs each one in its decisions
D: Encrypt the training data and restrict access to the data science team, so that no other staff can see applicants' personal details
K: A
G: ## Fairness is measured on outcomes, not on the input list
Learn describes fairness as AI systems treating everyone fairly and avoiding "affecting similarly situated groups of people in different ways"; loan decisions are its standard example. The Responsible AI dashboard's fairness assessment "helps assess model fairness across sensitive groups, such as gender, ethnicity, age".
## Why dropping columns is not enough
Other inputs can act as **proxies** for a removed attribute. Postcode can correlate with age or ethnicity; job title can correlate with gender. A model can therefore still give different results to different groups even though it never sees the sensitive column.
The evidence an auditor needs comes from **disaggregated evaluation**: keep the sensitive attributes in the test data (not as model inputs), compare metrics such as approval rate and false-decline rate per group, and mitigate gaps that exceed agreed limits.
## Example
Overall accuracy 91%, but false declines of 6% for applicants under 60 and 15% for applicants over 60: that gap is the finding, whatever the input list says.
W: Compare outcomes per group: measuring approval and error rates for each gender and age group on held-out data is the direct evidence of whether similar applicants are treated alike, and mitigation closes any gap found.
X: B=Remove more columns: removing inputs still produces no evidence, other features can carry the same signal, and removing useful features can hurt accuracy without proving fairness.
C=Explain the inputs: documenting inputs and their weights supports transparency, but it does not show whether outcomes differ between groups.
D=Encrypt and restrict data: protecting applicants' data supports privacy and security; it says nothing about whether decisions differ between groups.
T: Fairness is shown by comparing outcomes and errors across sensitive groups, because removing a sensitive column leaves proxies in place.

S: A regional news site plans to illustrate some articles with images generated by a GPT-image series model in Microsoft Foundry. Editors want readers, and other sites that republish the pictures, to be able to check that a picture was generated by AI, and the developers have no time to build a labeling system of their own. Which capability MOST directly meets this transparency need?
A: Content Credentials, a cryptographically signed C2PA manifest that Azure OpenAI attaches automatically to each image it generates
B: A guardrail in annotate mode, which adds a flag to the API response whenever a generated image contains potentially harmful content
C: A caption under each picture on the news site's own article pages stating that the picture was made with a generative AI tool
D: A system message telling the model to paint a visible "AI" watermark into one corner of every image that it generates for the site
K: A
G: ## Transparency about AI-generated content
Transparency means people can understand how an AI system works and when they are dealing with its output. For generated media, one key question is provenance: where did this picture come from?
## Content Credentials
Learn: "All AI-generated images from Azure OpenAI in Microsoft Foundry Models include Content Credentials, a tamper-evident way to disclose the origin and history of content." They follow the open C2PA specification:

- **No setup is needed**: credentials are applied automatically.
- Anyone can check them, for example with the Content Credentials Verify website, which shows that Microsoft issued them.

D=Watermark via system message: a prompt instruction is not a reliable control; the model may not follow it consistently, and a painted mark is easy to crop and cannot be verified.
T: Azure OpenAI image generation automatically attaches signed C2PA Content Credentials, so the AI origin of an image can be verified wherever it travels.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.1 advanced comparison
S: A city council's AI review board is checking a draft checklist for a new chatbot that helps residents apply for parking permits. Each line pairs a planned practice with the responsible AI principle it mainly supports. The board will approve only lines whose pairing matches Microsoft's definitions, because the checklist will be reused to train other teams. Which THREE lines are paired correctly? (Select THREE)
A: Transparency: tell residents what the chatbot is for, what it cannot do, and that they are talking to an AI system
B: Privacy and security: collect only the permit fields needed, and keep residents' data under access controls and retention rules
C: Accountability: name an owner who approves each release, and make sure a person can review and overturn every refused permit
D: Reliability and safety: add screen-reader support, captions and a plain-language mode so residents with disabilities can use the chatbot
E: Transparency: compare refusal rates across residents' age groups and districts, and correct any large gaps before the chatbot goes live
F: Fairness: encrypt the conversation logs at rest and in transit so that no unauthorized person can ever read the residents' messages
K: A,B,C
G: ## Microsoft's six principles in one line each
Microsoft's Responsible AI Standard rests on six principles:
- **Fairness**: similar people get similar outcomes; measured by comparing results across groups.
- **Reliability and safety**: the system works as designed and responds safely to unexpected conditions.
- **Privacy and security**: personal and business data is protected; people have appropriate control over how it is used.
- **Inclusiveness**: the system empowers and engages everyone, including people with disabilities.
- **Transparency**: people understand how the system works, what it is for, its limits, and that they are dealing with AI.
- **Accountability**: people who design and deploy the system are answerable for it; AI is not the final authority on decisions that affect people's lives.
## How to check a pairing
Ask which risk the practice reduces. Accessibility features reduce exclusion (inclusiveness), group comparisons reduce unequal treatment (fairness), encryption reduces data exposure (privacy and security).
W: A=Transparency: explaining purpose, limits and that the user is talking to AI is the core of transparency.
B=Privacy and security: data minimization, access control and retention rules protect residents' personal data.
C=Accountability: a named owner plus human review that can overturn refusals keeps people, not the AI, answerable for decisions.
X: D=Accessibility as reliability: screen readers, captions and plain language make the chatbot usable by more people, which is inclusiveness, not reliability and safety.
E=Group comparison as transparency: comparing refusal rates across groups and closing gaps is a fairness practice, not transparency.
F=Encryption as fairness: encrypting logs protects data from unauthorized access, which is privacy and security; it does nothing about equal treatment.
T: Map each practice to the risk it reduces: accessibility to inclusiveness, group comparisons to fairness, data protection to privacy, disclosure to transparency, ownership to accountability.

The first will label 3 million archived articles over the next week; each result can take up to a day, and cost matters most. The second is a fine-tuned model that the team only wants to evaluate for a few hours, at the lowest cost, before deciding whether to keep it. Which TWO deployment types fit? (Select TWO)

E: Regional Provisioned, which reserves capacity inside one Azure geography so the fine-tuned model is tested with guaranteed throughput

@ AI-901 D1 T1.2 advanced tradeoff
S: A marketing app sends two kinds of requests to the same chat model deployment: catchy slogan ideas, where variety is welcome, and legal disclaimer text, which must stay focused and consistent. A developer currently sets temperature to 1.5 and top_p to 0.2 on every request and gets unpredictable results for both kinds. Following Learn's guidance on these parameters, which change fits BEST?
A: Set temperature per request, higher for slogans and much lower for disclaimers, and leave top_p at its default rather than tuning both
B: Keep temperature at 1.5 for every request, and raise top_p to 1.0 for disclaimers so that the model considers more candidate tokens
C: Create a separate deployment for disclaimers, because the temperature of a model is fixed when it is deployed and cannot change per call
D: Set temperature to 2.0 for both kinds of request, and add a frequency penalty so that disclaimer wording repeats less between calls
K: A
G: ## Two ways to control randomness
A language model picks each next token from a probability distribution. Two request parameters shape that choice:
- **temperature** (0 to 2): higher values, such as 0.7 or more, make output "more random" and divergent; lower values, such as 0.2, make it "more focused and concrete". Learn's example: a fictional story suits a higher temperature, a legal document a much lower one.
- **top_p**: also controls randomness, but by limiting sampling to the most probable tokens whose probabilities add up to the given share.
Learn's recommendation: "alter one of these two parameters at a time, not both".
## Parameters are per request
Temperature and top_p are sent with each call, so one deployment can serve creative and precise tasks with different settings.
W: Per-request temperature, one knob at a time: a higher temperature gives slogans variety, a low one keeps disclaimers focused, and leaving top_p alone follows the guidance not to tune both together.
X: B=High temperature, top_p 1.0: a temperature of 1.5 keeps disclaimers random, and widening top_p to 1.0 adds even more candidate tokens instead of focusing the output.
C=Separate deployment: temperature is a request parameter, not a deployment setting, so a second deployment adds cost and management without being needed.
D=Temperature 2.0 plus penalty: the maximum temperature makes both outputs as random as possible; a frequency penalty reduces repetition inside a response, not inconsistency between calls.
T: Set temperature per request (high for creative, low for precise text) and change temperature or top_p, not both.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.2 advanced troubleshooting
S: A support tool sends a deployed chat model the full history of a long troubleshooting chat on every turn. The model's documented context window is 128,000 tokens. One request contains about 121,000 tokens of messages and asks for up to 16,000 output tokens, and it fails even though the input alone is under the limit. The team wants it to succeed without switching models. What is the cause, and which fix fits?
A: Messages plus requested output must fit in the context window, so trim or summarize older turns or lower the requested output limit
B: The service counts the requested output twice, so a request may ask for no more than half of the tokens left over after the input
C: Context windows are measured in words rather than tokens, so the history should be translated into a shorter language before sending
D: Requests fail whenever their input passes 90 percent of the window, so the app must send each earlier message in a separate request
K: A
G: ## The context window is shared
A model's context window (token limit) covers **everything in one request**: the input messages **and** the output it is asked to produce. Learn's chat guide: "The combined token count of your `messages` plus the requested output tokens must stay within the model's limit, or the request fails."
## Worked numbers
- Window: 128,000 tokens.
- Input: about 121,000 tokens, leaving about 7,000.
- Requested output: up to 16,000, so 121,000 + 16,000 = 137,000 > 128,000, and the request fails.
## Fixes that keep the same model
- Send less history: drop or summarize older turns (Learn: "only send the model a prompt that falls within the limit").
- Ask for less output, if 7,000 tokens or fewer is enough.
- With the Responses API, the service can manage truncation of conversation history for you.
W: Shared window: input and requested output together exceed 128,000 tokens; trimming or summarizing history, or lowering the output limit, brings the request back under the limit with the same model.
X: B=Output counted twice: there is no double counting; the rule is simply input plus requested output must fit in the window.
C=Words instead of tokens: limits are counted in tokens, and translating a support chat into another language would change its meaning without addressing the overflow.
D=90 percent rule and separate requests: there is no such threshold, and sending earlier messages separately would lose the conversation context the model needs.
T: A request fails when its input tokens plus the requested output tokens exceed the model's context window, so trim history or ask for less output.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.2 advanced troubleshooting
S: A company has 240,000 tokens-per-minute (TPM) of quota for a chat model with the Global Standard deployment type in one region. An existing deployment holds 200,000 TPM, but its peak use is only about 100,000 TPM. A new team needs a second deployment of the same model and type in the same region with 80,000 TPM and cannot wait for a quota request. What should the administrator do?
A: Lower the existing deployment's TPM allocation to 160,000 or less, and then create the new deployment with 80,000 TPM
B: Create the new deployment with 80,000 TPM anyway, because quota is enforced only on the tokens actually used each minute
C: Submit a quota increase request for that region, and wait for its approval before the second deployment can be created
D: Create the new deployment in a second Foundry resource in the same region, because quota is counted separately per resource
K: A
G: ## How Azure OpenAI quota works
Learn: quota is assigned "to your subscription on a per-region, per-model, per-deployment-type basis in units of Tokens-per-Minute (TPM)". Each deployment takes a slice of it when created:
- The TPM you assign becomes that deployment's rate limit.
- The sum across deployments of that model and type in that region, in any number of resources, cannot exceed the quota.
- When the quota is used up, you either **reduce TPM on other deployments** or request a quota increase.

- **`omitContent: true`**: "excludes the original content object from the response", so the result holds only the sub-analyzer's structured fields.
## Example
```
"config": {"enableSegment": false, "omitContent": true,
  "contentCategories": {"deliveryNote": {"description": "...", "analyzerId": "deliveryNotes"},
                        "customsDeclaration": {"description": "...", "analyzerId": "customsDecl"}}}
```
W: Whole-file classification with routing and omitContent: every file goes to one analyzer, is classified as a whole, is processed by the matching analyzer, and only the fields come back.
X: B=enableSegment with segmentPerPage: it splits each file into page segments, which is meant for mixed bundles and would treat each page of a one-document file separately.
C=Classify field plus manual calls: it classifies, but the app must then call the second analyzer itself, which breaks the single-analyzer routing requirement.
D=prebuilt-layout with returnDetails: layout extracts text and structure; it does not classify documents or route them to other analyzers.
T: Use contentCategories with analyzerId to route; enableSegment false classifies whole files, and omitContent returns only the routed fields.
