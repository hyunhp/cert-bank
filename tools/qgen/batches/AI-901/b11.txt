@ AI-901 D1 T1.1 expert troubleshooting
S: A bank audits its small-business loan model before renewing it. Overall accuracy is 92 percent, but applicants over 60 are wrongly declined three times as often as younger applicants with similar finances. Every declined applicant already receives the three main reasons for the decision, and applicant data is minimized, encrypted and deleted on schedule. The audit also finds that a data scientist pushed the current model version to production without any sign-off, and nobody can say who approved the two earlier versions or who handles appeals. Which TWO responsible AI principles does the audit show are not being met? (Select TWO)
A: Fairness, because applicants with similar finances face very different error rates depending on the age group they belong to
B: Accountability, because nobody owns model releases or appeals, and new versions reach production without any approval
C: Transparency, because declined applicants cannot find out which factors led the model to decline their loan applications
D: Privacy and security, because applicant data is processed by a model whose handling of personal data has never been reviewed
E: Inclusiveness, because the bank never tested its online application form with screen readers and other assistive technologies
K: A,B
G: ## Read the evidence, not the topic
Every principle can sound relevant to a loan model. An expert answer uses only what the audit actually shows:
- **Fairness**: similar people should get similar outcomes. A false-decline rate three times higher for one age group with similar finances is a fairness failure (high overall accuracy hides it).
- **Accountability**: people must be answerable for the system, and AI must not be the final authority on decisions that affect lives. Releases without sign-off, no record of approvers and no owner for appeals are accountability failures.
## Details that rule principles out
- Declined applicants already get their main reasons, so **transparency** (interpretability) is addressed.
- Data is minimized, encrypted and deleted on schedule, so the audit gives no evidence of a **privacy** gap.
- Nothing in the audit concerns accessibility, so **inclusiveness** is not shown to fail.
W: A=Fairness: a three-times-higher false-decline rate for over-60 applicants with similar finances is unequal treatment of similar people.
B=Accountability: unapproved releases, unknown approvers and no owner for appeals mean no person is answerable for the model's decisions.
X: C=Transparency: the stem states that every declined applicant already receives the main reasons, so this principle is being addressed.
D=Privacy and security: minimized, encrypted data deleted on schedule is evidence of good practice; the audit found no privacy gap.
E=Inclusiveness: assistive-technology testing is never mentioned in the audit, so there is no evidence either way.
T: Name a principle as failing only when the evidence shows it: unequal error rates mean fairness, missing ownership and approvals mean accountability.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.1 expert comparison
S: A bank's model risk team reviews a credit-limit model before release and writes down three questions. First, which data cohorts, such as customers with short credit histories, receive the most wrong predictions? Second, what would a declined customer have to change to be approved next time? Third, how can the findings be shared as a downloadable report with auditors and executives who will never open the data science tools? The team uses the Responsible AI dashboard in Azure Machine Learning. Which mapping of dashboard components to the three questions is correct?
A: Error analysis for the first, counterfactual what-if for the second, and the Responsible AI scorecard for the third
B: Fairness assessment for the first, causal inference for the second, and model interpretability charts for the third
C: Counterfactual what-if for the first, error analysis for the second, and the Responsible AI scorecard for the third
D: Error analysis for the first, model interpretability for the second, and an exported fairness metrics table for the third
K: A
G: ## Responsible AI dashboard components and the principles they serve
Learn's Responsible AI overview ties tools in Azure Machine Learning to the six principles:
- **Error analysis** (reliability and safety): "Get a deep understanding of how failure is distributed for a model" and find cohorts with higher error rates.
- **Fairness assessment** (fairness): compares model behavior across **sensitive groups** such as gender, ethnicity or age.
- **Model interpretability** (transparency): global and local explanations of which features drive predictions.
- **Counterfactual what-if** (transparency): model-driven insights that answer "What can I do to get a different outcome from your AI next time?"
- **Causal inference**: data-driven insights about treatment effects using historical data ("How would a medicine affect a patient's blood pressure?").
- **Responsible AI scorecard** (accountability): "a customizable PDF report" to share model health "with both technical and non-technical stakeholders".

"Data cohorts with the most wrong predictions" is error distribution, not a sensitive-group comparison. "What would the customer have to change" is a counterfactual. "Downloadable report for non-technical people" is the scorecard.
W: Error analysis, counterfactual what-if, scorecard: each component answers exactly its question: where failures concentrate, what change flips the outcome, and a shareable PDF for stakeholders.
X: B=Fairness, causal, interpretability: fairness assessment compares sensitive groups rather than error cohorts, causal inference estimates treatment effects from historical data, and charts in the tool are not a report for people who never open it.
C=Counterfactual first, error analysis second: the two are swapped; what-if answers what a customer should change, while error analysis finds where errors concentrate.
D=Interpretability for the second, fairness table for the third: feature importance explains the model but does not give a customer a concrete change, and a metrics table is not the stakeholder report the scorecard provides.
T: Error analysis finds where a model fails, counterfactual what-if shows what change alters an outcome, and the Responsible AI scorecard shares results with non-technical stakeholders.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.2 expert use-case
S: A German bank wants to fine-tune a chat model on 20,000 anonymized complaint letters in a Foundry resource in an EU region. Its data protection officer requires that the training data and the model weights stay in that region during training, and that inference also stays in the region. The team wants the cheapest option that still meets these rules. The model it chose supports the Standard, Global and Developer training types and the Standard, Data Zone Standard, Global Standard and Developer deployment types for fine-tuned models. Which combination fits?
A: Standard training, then a Standard deployment, which runs inference in the deployment's region with per-token and hosting charges
B: Global training, then a Developer deployment, which is the lowest-cost combination and avoids the hourly hosting fee for fine-tuned models
C: Standard training, then a Data Zone Standard deployment, which keeps inference within the EU data zone at per-token prices with no fee
D: Developer training, then a Standard deployment, which uses idle capacity to cut training cost before serving the model in the same region
K: A
G: ## Training types are separate from deployment types
Learn's fine-tuning page lists training types (where training runs):
- **Standard**: "Training occurs in the current Foundry resource's region and provides guarantees for data residency."
- **Global**: cheaper, using capacity beyond your region; "Data and weights are copied to the region where training occurs."
- **Developer**: idle capacity, preemptible, "no guarantees for data residency".
And deployment types for fine-tuned models (where inference runs):
- **Standard**: inference in the deployment's region, per-token billing plus fine-tuned model hosting charges.
- **Data Zone Standard**: inference within a data zone, but "**Only the US data zone is supported.**"
- **Global Standard**: custom model weights "might temporarily be stored outside the resource's geography".
- **Developer**: no hourly hosting fee, but no data-residency guarantee; evaluation only, 24 hours.
## The hidden detail
Data Zone Standard sounds like the EU answer, but for fine-tuned models only the US data zone is supported. The only combination that keeps both training and inference in the region is Standard plus Standard, so it is also the cheapest compliant one.
W: Standard training plus Standard deployment: training is guaranteed to stay in the resource's region, and Standard deployment runs inference in that region, so both rules are met; the hosting charge is the price of compliance.
X: B=Global training and Developer deployment: global training copies data and weights out of the region, and a Developer deployment has no residency guarantee.
C=Standard training and Data Zone Standard: for fine-tuned models Data Zone Standard supports only the US data zone, so EU inference is not available this way.
D=Developer training: it uses idle capacity with no data-residency guarantee, so the training data could leave the region.
T: For fine-tuning with strict regional residency use Standard training and a Standard (regional) deployment; Global and Developer options give up residency guarantees.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.2 expert tradeoff
S: A retailer's product-question assistant gives excellent answers with a large model, but at two million questions a month its cost and latency are too high. A smaller model answering zero-shot falls well short of the quality bar. The team has no labeled answers, only a large log of real customer questions, and policy says it may use only generally available features without signing up for any program. Management wants evidence that a cheaper model really matches quality before traffic moves. Which plan fits BEST?
A: Use Managed Training to fine-tune the smaller model with SFT on the large model's answers, then compare both on held-out questions
B: Use Programmed Training to write a custom distillation loop in Python, and switch traffic once the training job reports a low enough loss value
C: Use reinforcement fine-tuning on the smaller model with no grader, so that the customer questions on their own serve as the reward signal
D: Switch to the smaller model and add twenty few-shot examples to every prompt, then judge quality from customer complaints after the launch
K: A
G: ## Specializing a smaller model
Learn lists, among the reasons to fine-tune: "**Specialize a smaller model.** Teacher-generated examples can help a smaller model meet task requirements at lower cost and latency." This is **distillation**: a large "teacher" model answers real inputs, and those answers become supervised training data for a smaller "student".
## Choosing the training approach
- **Managed Training**: Foundry runs the job from your data; its methods include SFT, DPO, RFT and "distillation through teacher-generated SFT data".
- **Programmed Training**: you write the training loop in Python; it is **in preview and requires explicit access approval**.
- **RFT** needs "a grader that scores generated responses".
## Proving quality
"Benefits aren't guaranteed; held-out evaluation helps you compare the fine-tuned model with your baseline." Hold back a set of questions, answer them with both models, and compare before switching.
W: Managed Training SFT on teacher answers, then held-out comparison: the large model creates the training answers the team lacks, the method is a predefined GA workflow, and held-out evaluation gives the evidence management wants.
X: B=Programmed Training: it is a preview that needs access approval, which policy forbids, and a low training loss is not evidence of answer quality.
C=RFT with no grader: reinforcement fine-tuning needs a grader to score responses; questions alone provide no reward.
D=Few-shot and complaints: twenty examples per prompt add tokens to every call, and waiting for complaints after launch is the opposite of evidence before switching.
T: To cut cost with a smaller model, distill: fine-tune it with Managed Training on the large model's answers, then compare both on held-out data before switching.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.3 expert comparison
S: A bridge-maintenance company sends drones to photograph steel girders. Engineers need two numbers from each photo: the percentage of the girder surface covered by rust, which must follow the irregular edge of each rust patch, and the number of bolts still in place, with the location of each one so that a robot can torque it later. A third team already reads the serial plates printed on each girder with its own OCR system, and no whole-photo labels are needed. Which combination of computer vision techniques fits BEST?
A: Semantic segmentation for the rust, which labels each pixel so the covered area can be measured, plus object detection for the bolts
B: Object detection for the rust, which draws a rectangle around each patch to measure its area, plus image classification for the bolts
C: Image classification for the rust, which labels each photo as rusty or clean, plus semantic segmentation to count and place the bolts
D: Semantic segmentation for the rust, plus optical character recognition, which reads each bolt's markings and returns the bolt's position
K: A
G: ## Match the output to the measurement
- **Image classification**: one label for the whole image. Good for "is there rust?", useless for "how much?" or "how many?".
- **Object detection**: a class and a **bounding box** per object, so you can count objects and locate them. Rectangles do not follow irregular shapes, so areas computed from them are overestimates.
- **Semantic segmentation**: a class for **every pixel**, so the exact area of an irregular region can be measured. Pixels of one class are not separated into countable items.
- **OCR**: reads text and its position.
## Worked example
A girder photo has 2,000,000 girder pixels; segmentation labels 180,000 of them as rust: 9% coverage. Detection finds 46 bolts, each with a box for the robot.
## Hidden detail
The serial plates are already handled by another team, so OCR adds nothing here.
W: Segmentation plus detection: pixel-level labels measure the irregular rust area, and bounding boxes count each bolt and give its location.
X: B=Detection for rust, classification for bolts: rectangles overstate irregular rust areas, and a whole-image label cannot count or locate bolts.
C=Classification for rust, segmentation for bolts: a rusty/clean label gives no percentage, and class-per-pixel output does not separate individual bolts into a count with positions.
D=Segmentation plus OCR: bolts carry no text to read, and the only text in the photos is already handled by another team.
T: Use semantic segmentation to measure irregular areas and object detection to count and locate items; choose classification only for whole-image labels.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.3 expert troubleshooting

In testing with a shorter list of en-US, fr-FR, es-ES and de-DE, calls from Italian-speaking passengers come back transcribed as Spanish. The hotline runs on a Python service, and a short delay at the start of each call is acceptable. Which change addresses ALL of these facts?

X: B=At-start with two English locales: it repeats the duplicate-English mistake and drops two required languages to another phone line.
C=Continuous with en-US and en-GB: continuous LID allows enough candidates, but listing two locales of English breaks the one-locale-per-base-language rule.
D=Five or more at-start candidates: at-start LID allows at most four candidates; extra candidates are not silently ignored.
T: Supply one locale per language as LID candidates; at-start LID takes up to four, continuous LID up to ten, and a language missing from the list is reported as one of the candidates.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.3 expert use-case
S: A consumer magazine processes reader reviews of kitchen appliances. Its legal team requires that any summary shown under a review use only sentences that appear word for word in that review. Editors want to know, for each review, which product features are mentioned, such as the lid or the motor, and whether the reviewer is positive or negative about each feature. The data team also wants every brand, store and city in a review labeled with its type. Which THREE text analysis techniques fit these requirements? (Select THREE)
A: Extractive summarization, which selects the most important original sentences of each review to form its summary
B: Opinion mining, which links each product feature mentioned in a review to the sentiment expressed about that feature
C: Named entity recognition, which finds mentions such as organizations and locations and labels each with its category
D: Abstractive summarization, which writes new sentences that capture each review's main points in a shorter, fluent form
E: Document-level sentiment analysis, which gives each review a single positive, negative, neutral or mixed overall label
F: Key phrase extraction, which labels each brand, store and city in a review with its entity type and a confidence score
K: A,B,C
G: ## Similar techniques, different outputs
- **Extractive summarization** returns sentences **copied** from the source, ranked by importance. **Abstractive summarization** writes **new** sentences.
- **Sentiment analysis** labels a document (and its sentences) positive, negative, neutral or mixed. **Opinion mining**, an aspect-based extension, links **targets** (such as "lid" or "motor") to the **assessments** about them ("flimsy", "powerful") with their sentiment.
- **Named entity recognition** labels mentions with categories such as Organization, Location or Product. **Key phrase extraction** returns the main talking points without types.
## Mapping the three requirements
- "Word for word" rules out abstractive summaries: extractive.
- "Each feature and the feeling about it" needs aspect-level output: opinion mining.
- "Labeled with its type" needs categories: NER.
## Techniques versus products
These are general techniques. In Azure Language, NER is a core capability, while summarization and sentiment analysis with opinion mining are legacy features that retire on March 31, 2029; Learn points new projects to Foundry models for those tasks.
W: A=Extractive summarization: it uses only original sentences, which satisfies the legal word-for-word rule.
B=Opinion mining: it pairs each mentioned feature with the sentiment about it, exactly what editors want per feature.
C=Named entity recognition: it labels brands (organizations), stores and cities (locations) with their categories.
X: D=Abstractive summarization: it writes new sentences, which breaks the requirement to use only sentences from the review.
E=Document-level sentiment: one overall label cannot say how the reviewer feels about the lid versus the motor.
F=Key phrase extraction: it returns key phrases without entity types or confidence per type; typed labels come from NER.
T: Use extractive summarization for verbatim summaries, opinion mining for feature-level sentiment, and NER for typed mentions such as organizations and places.

@ AI-901 D2 T2.1 expert troubleshooting
S: A building-products company's chat assistant answers pricing questions by pasting the raw HTML of three product web pages into each prompt, about 40,000 tokens including scripts, menus and footers. Answers often quote the wrong price tier or mix up products, although the correct prices appear in the HTML. The pages change monthly, the team must keep using a non-reasoning chat model, and latency must drop as well. Product managers also want each answer to name the price tier it used. Following Learn's guidance on grounding context, which change fits BEST?
A: Extract the price tables ahead of time into clean text close to the final answer form, and ground prompts on that text
B: Keep the full HTML but raise max_tokens, so the model has room to reason through all the markup before it writes the final answer

- reduces the model's work (and errors such as mixing up tiers),
- cuts input tokens from about 40,000 to a few hundred, which also lowers latency and cost,
- keeps answers current because the extraction reruns when the pages change.
W: Pre-extract clean price tables: grounding text close to the final answer form leaves less room for error, and far fewer tokens means lower latency.
X: B=Raise max_tokens: the output limit does not help the model find prices in markup; more output tokens can only add latency.
C=Rely on training data: the company's prices are not public knowledge and change monthly; removing grounding invites invented prices.
D=Paste even more HTML: more irrelevant markup makes the model's job harder and increases tokens and latency further.
T: Ground prompts on clean data that is as close as possible to the final answer form; it reduces errors, tokens and latency.
