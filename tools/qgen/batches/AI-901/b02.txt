@ AI-901 D1 T1.2 applied definition
S: A developer sends the sentence "The results were unbelievable!" to a language model and sees that the request used more tokens than the sentence has words. What explains the difference?
A: The model counts every character of the input as its own token, so a five-word sentence becomes several dozen separate tokens.
B: The model splits text into tokens that can be whole words, parts of words such as "un", and punctuation, so one word can become several tokens.
C: The model adds one hidden token for each word to record its dictionary definition, which doubles the count compared with the words sent.
D: The model translates the sentence into English base forms first and counts each grammatical rule it applies as one extra token in the request.
K: B
G: ## What a token is
Language models do not read words the way people do. Before any text reaches the model it is broken into **tokens**, and each token gets a unique integer ID from the model's vocabulary.
- A token can be a **whole word** ("results"), a **sub-word** (the "un" in "unbelievable" and "unlikely"), **punctuation** ("!") or another common sequence of characters.
- Modern large language models have vocabularies of hundreds of thousands of tokens built from their training data.
- Common words are often a single token; rarer or longer words are split into several pieces.
## Why it matters
Everything about a model's limits is measured in tokens, not words: the **context window** (how much text one request can hold), the **maximum output** setting, and usage reporting. A prompt with 5 words can easily use 7 or 8 tokens once sub-words and punctuation are counted.
## Example
"The results were unbelievable!" might become `The` `results` `were` `un` `believ` `able` `!`: five words, seven tokens.
W: Sub-word tokenization: tokens include whole words, sub-words and punctuation, so a long word like "unbelievable" and the exclamation mark add tokens beyond the word count.
X: A=Every character a token: tokenizers group common character sequences into single tokens; counting each character separately would make vocabularies tiny and prompts enormous.
C=Hidden definition tokens: the model does not add dictionary entries to the input. Meaning is captured in the embedding vectors the model learned, not in extra tokens.
D=Grammar rule tokens: no translation into base forms happens before tokenization, and grammar rules are not counted; the text is split into vocabulary tokens as written.
T: Models measure text in tokens (words, sub-words, punctuation), so token counts are usually higher than word counts.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.2 applied use-case
S: An internal help site must match the question "I can't sign in anymore" to an article titled "Recover a forgotten password", even though the two share almost no words. Which capability makes this match possible?
A: A keyword index that counts the words the question and each article title have in common, then ranks articles by how many shared words they contain.
B: An image generation model that draws a picture for each article and compares the pictures with a picture drawn from the user's question.
C: Embeddings that turn the question and each article into vectors, so texts with similar meaning have similar vectors and a high cosine similarity.
D: A higher temperature setting on a chat model, which makes the model consider more unusual words and so find articles that use different terms.
K: C
G: ## Embeddings capture meaning
An **embedding** is a vector (an array of floating-point numbers) that represents the semantic meaning of a piece of text. Embedding models are trained so that the **distance between two vectors reflects how similar their meanings are**.
- "dog" and "puppy" end up with vectors pointing in roughly the same direction; "skateboard" points somewhere else.
- Two sentences that say the same thing in different words get similar vectors.
## Measuring similarity
Azure OpenAI embeddings commonly use **cosine similarity**: the cosine of the angle between two vectors. A small angle means a high score and similar meaning, even if one text is much longer than the other.
Learn notes that simply counting common words does not scale: longer documents share more words even when the topics differ.
## Example
The help site converts every article into an embedding once and stores the vectors (for example in Azure AI Search). When a user types "I can't sign in anymore", the app embeds the question and returns the article whose vector is closest: "Recover a forgotten password".
V: Azure OpenAI embeddings, Azure AI Search
W: Embeddings with cosine similarity: embedding vectors encode meaning, so a question and an article about the same problem score as similar even when they use different words.
X: A=Shared-word counting: the two texts share almost no words, so a keyword overlap count would rank the right article low; this approach matches spelling, not meaning.
B=Image generation: image models create pictures from text descriptions; drawing and comparing pictures is not a way to measure how similar two texts are.
D=Higher temperature: temperature changes how random a chat model's generated text is. It does not search or compare documents at all.
T: To match text by meaning rather than shared words, compare embedding vectors (for example with cosine similarity).

@ AI-901 D1 T1.2 advanced definition
S: While learning how a transformer model builds embeddings, a student reads the sentence "I heard a dog bark". What does the attention mechanism do when it processes the token "bark"?
A: It looks up the token "bark" in a fixed dictionary of meanings and copies the stored definition into the embedding without looking at any other token.
B: It weighs the surrounding tokens by how strongly they influence "bark", giving "heard" and "dog" more weight than "I" or "a", to compute its embedding.
C: It removes small and common tokens such as "I" and "a" from the sentence so that only nouns and verbs are passed on to the next layer of the model.
D: It sorts the tokens of the sentence alphabetically so that the position of each word stops affecting the vector values the model calculates.
K: B
G: ## The transformer in brief
Modern generative language models are based on the **transformer** architecture. In the simplified picture used on Microsoft Learn:
- An **encoder** block turns tokens into **embeddings**, vectors that carry linguistic and semantic meaning learned from context.
- A **decoder** block uses those embeddings to predict the **next most probable token** in a sequence that a prompt started.
## Attention
**Attention** is the technique that makes the embeddings context-aware. For each token, an attention layer considers the tokens around it and assigns **weights** that reflect how much each one influences the current token. Those weights are used to calculate the token's vector values.
- **Multi-head attention** evaluates different elements of the vectors in parallel to make this efficient.
- **Positional encoding** is fed in with each token because word order matters to meaning.
- In the decoder, **masked attention** only lets the model look at tokens that come before the one it is predicting.
## Example
In "I heard a dog bark", the tokens "heard" and "dog" are strong clues about what "bark" means (a sound a dog makes, not tree bark), so attention gives them more weight than "I" or "a".
W: Weighted context: attention assigns each neighbouring token a weight that reflects its influence on the current token, which is how "bark" gets an embedding that reflects a dog's sound.
X: A=Fixed dictionary lookup: embeddings are learned from the contexts tokens appear in during training, not copied from a stored definition, and attention specifically uses the other tokens.
C=Dropping small tokens: attention does not delete tokens; it gives less influential tokens lower weights while still considering every token in the sequence.
D=Alphabetical sorting: transformers add positional encoding precisely because word order matters, so sorting the tokens would destroy information the model needs.
T: Attention computes each token's embedding by weighting the surrounding tokens according to how much they influence it.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.2 applied use-case
S: A support team's chat app sometimes answers questions about the company's return policy with confident but invented details. Which TWO changes are most likely to reduce these fabricated answers? (Select TWO)
A: Retrieve the relevant sections of the current policy documents and include them in the prompt as grounding data for the model to answer from.
B: Raise the temperature so the model explores a wider range of possible wordings before it settles on the answer it returns to the customer.
C: Increase the maximum number of output tokens so the model has enough room to explain the policy fully instead of guessing at the details.
D: Instruct the model in the system message to answer only from the provided content and to cite the source section for each statement it makes.
E: Remove the system message entirely so that the model relies only on what it learned during training rather than on extra instructions.
K: A,D
G: ## Why models invent answers
A generative model produces text by repeatedly predicting the **most probable next token**. It has no built-in check that a sentence is true. When the answer is not in its training data (for example, a company's private return policy), it can still produce fluent, confident text that is wrong. This is called a **hallucination** or fabricated answer.
## Grounding
**Grounding** means giving the model the facts it should use. The retrieval augmented generation (RAG) pattern does this in three steps:
- **Retrieve** relevant content from your data, often through a search index.
- **Augment** the prompt with that content (the grounding data) plus the user's question.
- **Generate** a response based on the retrieved content.
## Instructions and citations
Learn notes that models can still hallucinate even with grounding, so pair it with clear instructions: tell the model to stick to the retrieved content and to **cite its sources**. Asking for citations, especially inline ones, makes unsupported statements less likely, because the model would have to invent both the claim and its source.
## Example
The app searches the policy index for "return electronics after 30 days", adds the two matching paragraphs to the prompt, and the system message says: "Answer only from the provided policy text and cite the section number. If the answer is not there, say you don't know."
W: A=Grounding data: putting the current policy text into the prompt gives the model real facts to base its answer on instead of guessing from training data that never contained the policy.
D=Stay-on-source instructions with citations: telling the model to use only the provided content and to cite it makes unsupported statements less likely and easier to spot.
X: B=Higher temperature: a higher temperature makes output more random and divergent, which tends to increase invented details rather than reduce them.
C=More output tokens: the maximum output length limits how long an answer can be; extra room does not give the model any facts it lacks.
E=No system message: removing instructions leaves the model relying on training data that never contained the company's policy, which is exactly what causes fabrication.
T: Reduce hallucinations by grounding the prompt in retrieved data and instructing the model to answer only from it with citations.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.2 applied comparison
S: Employees chat with a deployed model every day, and a manager assumes the model is learning from these conversations. How do training and inference differ for a deployed generative AI model?
A: Each chat request is inference: the model uses fixed learned weights to predict tokens, and its weights change only through a separate training or fine-tuning job.
B: Each chat request is a small training step: the model updates its weights after every answer, so it gradually learns the company's facts from employees.
C: Inference happens only once when the model is deployed, and every chat request after that is training that permanently adds the conversation to the model.
D: Training and inference are two names for the same process, and the deployment type chosen in Foundry decides whether the conversations are remembered.
K: A
G: ## Two phases in a model's life
- **Training** is where a model learns. A large language model is trained on huge volumes of text; during training the model compares its predicted next token with the real one and adjusts its internal **weights** to reduce the error. This is slow and compute-heavy.
- **Inference** is where a trained model is used. You send a prompt, and the model uses its learned weights to predict output tokens. Inference does **not** change the weights.
## Changing what a model knows or does
- **Fine-tuning** is additional training on task-specific data. It adjusts the model's weights to improve accuracy on a task, align style and format, or specialize a smaller model. It is a separate job, not something that happens during chat.
- **Grounding (RAG)** adds current or private information to each prompt at inference time without changing the weights.
## A note on memory
A model does not remember earlier requests by itself: the app resends the earlier turns with each new question, or uses an API that stores conversation state for it, such as the Responses API. Either way, nothing from those conversations is written back into the model's weights.
## Example
A team wants the model to always answer in the company's support style. Chatting with it for a month changes nothing; a fine-tuning job on example conversations, or a well-written system message, does.
W: Inference with fixed weights: a chat request uses the trained model as it is; only a separate training or fine-tuning job adjusts the weights.
X: B=Training on every answer: deployed models do not update their weights from chat traffic, so employees' facts are not learned this way; the app must supply them in each prompt.
C=Inference only at deployment: it is the other way round. Training happens before deployment, and every chat request afterwards is inference that leaves the model unchanged.
D=Same process: training changes weights and inference uses them, and the deployment type controls where data is processed and how you pay, not whether the model learns.
T: Chat requests are inference with fixed weights; only training or fine-tuning changes what the model has learned.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.2 applied definition
S: A chat model answers the prompt "Write a short welcome message for new staff" with a full paragraph. At a basic level, how does a generative language model produce that paragraph?
A: It finds the closest matching paragraph in a stored library of human-written messages and returns that text with a few words changed.
B: It predicts a probable next token from the prompt and the text so far, adds it to the sequence, and repeats until the response ends.
C: It writes every word of the paragraph at the same moment from a fixed template and then sorts the words into grammatical order.
D: It sends the prompt to a web search engine, copies the top result into the reply and then trims it to fit the maximum output length.
K: B
G: ## Generation is next-token prediction
A generative language model does not look up finished answers. It builds the response **one token at a time**:
- The prompt is split into tokens and turned into embeddings that carry context.
- The **decoder** part of the transformer uses attention over the tokens so far to work out the **most probable next token**.
- That token is **added to the sequence**, and the whole process repeats to predict the next one, until the model predicts that the sequence has ended (or a limit such as the maximum output tokens is reached).
## Masked attention
During training, the decoder uses **masked attention**: when it predicts a token it may only look at the tokens that come **before** it. It compares its prediction with the real next token and adjusts its weights to reduce the error. At inference time the same model simply keeps extending the text it has started.
## Example
Given "When my dog was a ...", the model weighs the earlier tokens and predicts "puppy" as far more likely than "cat" or "skateboard". It then predicts the token after "puppy", and so on.
W: Iterative next-token prediction: the model repeatedly predicts a likely next token from everything generated so far and appends it, which is how a whole paragraph is built.
X: A=Stored library lookup: the model does not keep a database of finished messages; it generates new text token by token from patterns learned in training.
C=All words at once from a template: generation is sequential, each new token depends on the tokens before it, and no template or sorting step exists.
D=Copying a web search result: a model produces text from its learned weights and the prompt; searching the web is a separate tool an app may add, not how generation works.
T: A language model writes a response by predicting the next token, appending it and repeating until the response ends.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.2 applied tradeoff
S: An insurer wants a chat assistant to answer questions from its product terms, which legal staff update every week. Answers must reflect the latest version and point to the source section. Which approach fits best?
A: Fine-tune a language model on the product terms each quarter so the terms become part of the model's weights and need no further changes.
B: Use retrieval augmented generation to search an index of the current terms and give the model the matching sections to cite.
C: Train a new large language model from scratch on the product terms so that it knows nothing except the insurer's own documents.
D: Write all of the product terms into the system message once and keep that system message unchanged for every conversation from then on.
K: B
G: ## Adding knowledge versus changing behaviour
Large language models are trained on public data available at training time. Private documents and frequently changing information are not in that data. Microsoft Learn gives a simple rule for choosing an approach in Foundry:
- **Retrieval augmented generation (RAG)**: use it when you need answers grounded in **private or frequently changing data**. Content is retrieved at request time, so updates show up as soon as the index is refreshed, and responses can include **citations** back to the source.
- **Fine-tuning**: use it to change the model's **behaviour, style or task performance**, not to add fresh knowledge. It adds training and hosting costs and does not replace retrieval for current information.
## How RAG fits this case
The terms are chunked and indexed (Azure AI Search is a recommended index store for RAG). For each question the app retrieves the matching sections, adds them to the prompt, and asks the model to answer from them and cite them.
## Example
On Monday legal changes the flood-cover exclusion. The index is refreshed that afternoon, and from then on every answer quotes the new wording and names the section it came from.
W: Retrieval augmented generation: retrieving the current terms at request time keeps answers up to date with weekly changes and lets the response cite the exact source section.
X: A=Quarterly fine-tuning: fine-tuning changes behaviour and style rather than reliably adding fresh facts, and a quarterly cycle would leave answers weeks behind the weekly updates.
C=Training from scratch: building a new large language model needs vast data and compute, and a model trained only on policy documents would lack general language ability.
D=Terms in a fixed system message: an unchanged system message goes stale after the first weekly update, and stuffing all terms into every request wastes context window space.
T: Use RAG for private or frequently changing knowledge with citations; use fine-tuning to change behaviour or style.

@ AI-901 D1 T1.2 advanced use-case
S: A legal firm is building a retrieval augmented generation (RAG) app: it must find contract passages by meaning and then write answers grounded in those passages. Which TWO model types does the solution need? (Select TWO)
A: An embedding model that converts contract passages and user questions into vectors so the app can retrieve passages by semantic similarity.

G: ## Two jobs, two models
A RAG solution retrieves first and generates second, and each step needs a different kind of model.
- **Retrieve**: passages and questions are turned into **embeddings** by an embedding model (for example text-embedding-3-large or a Cohere embedding model). The index (Azure AI Search is a recommended store) finds passages whose vectors are closest to the question's vector, so matches are based on meaning rather than exact keywords.
- **Augment**: the app puts the retrieved passages into the prompt as grounding data.
- **Generate**: a **chat completion model** reads the question and the passages and writes the answer, ideally with citations.
## Why not one model?
An embedding model only outputs vectors; it cannot write an answer. A chat model can write but is not designed to produce search vectors, and on its own it does not know the firm's contracts.
## Example
