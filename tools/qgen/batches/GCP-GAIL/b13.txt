@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.3 applied tradeoff
S: A bank's board asks why the company should spend time on reviews, documentation and testing for a new customer-facing gen AI assistant when competitors are shipping quickly. Which argument best reflects Google Cloud's view of responsible AI?
A: Responsible AI practices mainly satisfy regulators, so they should be added after launch, once the product has proven its commercial value
B: Evaluating how the system behaves, including when it fails, makes products safer and more accountable and earns the customer trust that drives adoption
C: Responsible AI mostly concerns the research team's model choices, so business leaders can delegate it entirely and review the results annually
D: Responsible AI guarantees that the assistant will never produce an incorrect answer, which removes the need for human oversight in production
K: B
G: ## Why responsible AI is a business topic
Responsible AI means designing, building and operating AI so that it is safe, fair, private, transparent and accountable. Google Cloud presents it as values-based AI that is good for business, not only a compliance exercise. Its stated benefits are:
- **Safer and more accountable products**: evaluating the system both when it performs as intended and when it does not.
- **Customer trust**: lack of trust is a growing barrier to enterprise AI adoption, and organizations increasingly choose products based on a provider's AI commitments and practices.
- **A culture of responsible innovation**: giving decision-makers and developers room to weigh ethical considerations early.
Google applies its AI Principles through review bodies that assess risks and opportunities for products. A customer of Google Cloud can borrow the same idea: review before launch, not after an incident.
## Reading the scenario
The board frames responsible AI as a cost that slows delivery. The strongest answer reframes it as what makes adoption and trust possible.
W: Evaluation, safety and trust: responsible AI builds in testing of both success and failure cases, which makes the product more accountable and earns the customer trust that enterprise adoption depends on.
X: A=Add it after launch: retrofitting reviews after customers are already affected is the costly path, and treating the topic as only regulatory ignores the trust and product-quality benefits.
C=Delegate to research: accountability for AI outcomes sits with business and product leaders as well, so it cannot be handed to the model team and checked once a year.
D=Guarantee of no errors: no practice can promise that a gen AI system never errs, and human oversight remains part of responsible deployment.
T: Responsible AI is a business enabler: it makes products safer, more accountable and more trusted, and it starts before launch.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.3 applied use-case
S: A health insurer will offer a gen AI claims assistant to members. Compliance leaders want members, auditors and partner organizations to understand what the underlying model is intended for, how it was evaluated and where its known limitations lie. Which practice best delivers this transparency?
A: Publishing structured documentation for the model, such as a model card, that describes its intended use, evaluation results and limitations
B: Hiding the model's limitations from members to avoid confusion, and releasing only marketing material describing its best-case accuracy
C: Giving every member direct access to the raw training data so each person can verify the model's behavior independently
D: Retraining the model each quarter and keeping the training configuration confidential, so that its behavior stays hard to predict
K: A
G: ## What transparency means
Transparency is being open about what an AI system is, what it is for, how it was built and tested, and where it can go wrong. It lets users and reviewers set the right level of trust.
Google Cloud points to resources that give model transparency in a structured, accessible way: **Model Cards** (short documents on intended use, performance and limitations), **Explainable AI** (insight into why a model produced a result) and open-source toolkits for inspecting models.
## Transparency is not disclosure of everything
Being transparent does not require exposing raw training data, which may contain private information. It requires clear, honest documentation and communication about purpose, evaluation and limits.
W: Model cards: structured documentation of intended use, evaluation results and limitations is exactly what members, auditors and partners need to understand the system without exposing sensitive internals.
X: B=Marketing-only material: omitting limitations misleads members; transparency requires honest reporting of limits, not best-case claims.
C=Raw training data for members: this would likely breach privacy and does not explain behavior to a non-technical reader; it is disclosure, not useful transparency.
D=Confidential configuration: keeping behavior hard to predict is the opposite of transparency, and retraining does not document anything.
T: Transparency means honest, structured documentation (such as model cards) of purpose, evaluation and limits.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.3 applied use-case
S: A retailer's support agents have begun pasting full customer emails, including names, home addresses and order histories, into a gen AI tool to get drafted replies. A privacy officer is worried. Which statement best identifies the core privacy risk?
A: The drafted replies will be too long, which raises latency and makes the assistant less pleasant for agents to use
B: Customers can no longer be contacted by email, because gen AI tools normally block messages that contain addresses
C: Personal data may be exposed, retained or used beyond its intended purpose, so the company needs data handling rules before it is submitted
D: The model will always refuse to process personal data, so the agents will simply receive empty responses and stop using the tool
K: C
G: ## Privacy risks in gen AI
Prompts, retrieved documents and training data can all contain **personally identifiable information (PII)** or other sensitive data. Typical privacy risks are:
- Sending personal data to a system or provider without a clear legal basis or customer expectation.
- Data being kept, logged or reused for purposes the person never agreed to.
- Sensitive details appearing in outputs shown to people who should not see them.
- Training or tuning on personal data, so that the model may reproduce it.
## Controls a leader should expect
- Clear policy on what may be entered into which tool.
- Data minimization: send only what the task needs.
- De-identifying data before use (see anonymization and pseudonymization), for example with Sensitive Data Protection.
- Access controls and agreements that state how the provider handles your data.
W: Personal data exposure and misuse: submitting full emails with names and addresses creates a risk that data is exposed or used beyond its purpose, so handling rules and minimization must come first.
X: A=Long replies: reply length is a usability matter, not a privacy risk.
B=Blocked contact: gen AI tools do not stop customers being emailed; this invents a restriction unrelated to data protection.
D=Refusal of personal data: models do not reliably refuse such input, which is exactly why policy and controls are needed rather than hoping for refusals.
T: The privacy risk is personal data being exposed or used beyond its purpose, so set handling rules and minimize what is submitted.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.3 applied comparison
S: A lender wants its analytics team to build customer-behavior reports from loan records. Customer IDs must be replaced with tokens, the same customer must receive the same token across tables so records can still be joined, and an authorized fraud team must be able to recover the original ID when legally required. Which approach fits?
A: Pseudonymization with reversible tokens, where keys held by a restricted team allow authorized re-identification
B: Anonymization that irreversibly removes the identifiers, so that nobody, including the fraud team, can ever link records back to a customer
C: Deleting the ID column entirely, since joins across tables are not needed when analysts only build reports
D: Encrypting the whole data warehouse at rest, which leaves IDs visible to every analyst who has query access
K: A
G: ## Anonymization versus pseudonymization
Both are **de-identification** techniques that reduce the privacy exposure of data, but they differ in reversibility.
- **Anonymization** removes or transforms identifying information so that individuals cannot reasonably be identified again. It is meant to be irreversible. Data that is truly anonymous is generally outside many privacy rules, but heavy anonymization can reduce analytic value, and combining datasets may still enable re-identification.
- **Pseudonymization** replaces identifying values with tokens or surrogates. In Sensitive Data Protection it uses cryptographic keys: **two-way** tokens (deterministic encryption or format-preserving encryption) can be reversed with the key, and **one-way** tokens (cryptographic hashing) cannot. The same input and key give the same token, which preserves **referential integrity** for joins across tables.
## Applying it
The scenario needs consistent tokens for joins and a controlled way back for the fraud team. That is reversible pseudonymization, with the key kept by a restricted group (for example in Cloud KMS).
W: Reversible pseudonymization: tokens are consistent, so tables can be joined, and a restricted key lets authorized staff re-identify records when legally required.
X: B=Irreversible anonymization: removing any link back to a customer satisfies the analysts but breaks the requirement that the fraud team can recover the original ID.
C=Deleting the ID column: with no identifier, records cannot be joined across tables or traced by the fraud team, so two of the three needs fail.
D=Encryption at rest: this protects stored disks but does not replace the visible ID for analysts, so it does not de-identify the data being analyzed.
T: Pseudonymization swaps identifiers for consistent, reversible-by-key tokens; anonymization is meant to be irreversible.
V: Sensitive Data Protection, Cloud KMS

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.3 applied definition
S: A research hospital wants to release a patient dataset to outside academics for public-health studies. The hospital does not need to follow up with any patient and wants to make it as hard as reasonably possible for anyone to identify individuals. Which description best matches the technique the hospital should aim for?
A: Pseudonymization that replaces names with tokens while the hospital keeps the key so that it can re-identify patients later
B: Anonymization that removes or generalizes identifying details, so individuals cannot reasonably be identified from the released data
C: Encryption of the released file with a key shared with every academic, so that the file is protected during transfer only
D: Data augmentation that adds synthetic patients to the dataset while leaving the real patients' names and dates of birth unchanged
K: B
G: ## Choosing the degree of de-identification
When data leaves your organization, the question is whether recipients should ever be able to link records to real people.
- If nobody needs to re-identify, aim for **anonymization**: remove direct identifiers (names, IDs) and generalize or suppress quasi-identifiers such as exact birth dates or small-area locations.
- If your own organization needs a path back, **pseudonymization** with a protected key is more suitable, but the data is still linkable by whoever holds the key, so it stays personal data in many privacy regimes.
## Caution
Removing names alone is not enough: rare combinations of attributes can still single out a person, so anonymization needs a check for re-identification risk. Sensitive Data Protection can inspect data for sensitive elements and apply de-identification transformations.
W: Anonymization: removing and generalizing identifying details so individuals cannot reasonably be identified matches a release where no follow-up is needed and exposure must be minimal.
X: A=Reversible pseudonymization: the retained key keeps patients linkable, which is useful for follow-up but is unnecessary here and leaves residual identification risk.
C=Shared-key encryption: encryption protects the file in transit, but every recipient who decrypts it sees the original identities, so no de-identification occurs.
D=Synthetic additions: adding fake rows does not hide the real patients, whose names and birth dates remain exposed.
T: When no one needs to re-identify individuals, aim for anonymization; keep pseudonymization for cases that need a controlled way back.
V: Sensitive Data Protection

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.3 applied use-case
S: A lender is fine-tuning a model to pre-screen small-business loan applications. Historical approvals came mostly from urban applicants, and rural businesses are barely represented. Which TWO actions would best support fairness? (Select TWO)
A: Measure model performance and approval rates separately for urban and rural applicant groups before the model is used
B: Remove all application records from the training set that mention a rural location and retrain on the remaining data
C: Broaden and rebalance the training data so rural applicants are adequately represented, then re-evaluate the results
D: Publish only the model's overall accuracy, since a single high number shows that every group is treated equally
E: Increase the model's temperature so that its decisions vary more between applicants from the same group
K: A,C
G: ## Data quality and fairness
A model learns patterns from its data. If some groups are under-represented or historical decisions were skewed, the model can produce worse or less favorable outcomes for those groups. Representativeness is therefore a data quality dimension with direct fairness consequences.
## Good practice
- **Evaluate by subgroup**: an overall metric can hide poor results for a minority group. Compare error rates and outcomes group by group.
- **Improve the data**: collect or rebalance data so that relevant groups are adequately covered, and review labels for historical bias.
- **Keep humans involved** for high-impact decisions such as lending, and re-evaluate after changes.
## Things that do not help
Deleting a group from the data worsens its representation; sampling randomness does not create fairness.
W: A=Subgroup evaluation: comparing performance and approval rates across urban and rural groups reveals disparities that an overall number would hide.
C=Rebalanced data: adding and rebalancing rural examples fixes the under-representation at its source, and re-evaluating confirms the effect.
X: B=Dropping rural records: removing the under-represented group makes representation worse, so the model would know even less about rural applicants.
D=Overall accuracy only: a single aggregate can look high while a smaller group is treated poorly, so it does not demonstrate equal treatment.
E=Higher temperature: temperature adds randomness to generated text; it does not remove bias and would make lending decisions inconsistent.
T: Improve representation in the data and measure results per group; an overall metric can hide unfair outcomes.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.3 applied definition
S: A hiring platform's leadership asks what "fairness" means for its candidate-ranking model. Which description is most accurate?
A: The model is fair when it runs on the fastest hardware, since equal response time gives all candidates the same experience
B: The model is fair when it never sees any candidate data at all, because only a model without inputs can be free of bias
C: The model is fair when it produces identical scores for every candidate, regardless of their qualifications or experience
D: The model avoids creating or reinforcing unjust outcomes for people, particularly across sensitive attributes such as gender or ethnicity
K: D
G: ## Bias and fairness
**Bias** is a systematic skew in data, model or process that leads to results favoring or disadvantaging certain people. It can come from the data (unrepresentative samples, historical discrimination), from labels, from how the problem is framed or from how outputs are used.
**Fairness** is the goal of avoiding unjust impact on people, especially related to sensitive characteristics such as race, gender, age or disability. Google's AI Principles call for avoiding creating or reinforcing unfair bias.
## What fairness is not
- It does not mean ignoring qualifications: a ranking should still differentiate by relevant criteria.
- Fairness is a property of outcomes and process, not of speed or hardware.
- It cannot be achieved by removing the data entirely; it must be measured and monitored for the actual use.
W: Avoiding unjust outcomes: fairness means the system does not create or reinforce unfair impact on people, particularly across sensitive attributes, and that is measured on real outcomes.
X: A=Fast hardware: infrastructure speed affects performance, not whether groups of candidates are treated justly.
B=No candidate data: a ranking model needs inputs to work, and the goal is fair use of data, not the absence of data.
C=Identical scores: giving everyone the same score removes the model's purpose and ignores legitimate, job-relevant differences.
T: Fairness means avoiding unjust or unfairly biased outcomes for people, especially across sensitive attributes, while still using relevant criteria.

S: A retailer's gen AI assistant recommends discounts. After a customer complaint about an inappropriate offer, executives ask who is answerable for the system's behavior. Which approach best establishes accountability?
A: Assign clear ownership of the system's outcomes to named people and teams, with review processes, escalation paths and human oversight for high-impact decisions
B: Tell customers that the AI made the decision independently, so that no employee or team is responsible for the outcome
C: Leave accountability with the model provider, because the retailer only supplies prompts and cannot influence how the application behaves
D: Wait for regulators to define responsibilities, and avoid recording who approved the assistant's launch until rules are published

G: ## Accountability
**Accountability** means that people and organizations, not models, answer for what an AI system does. Practically it involves:
- Named owners for each AI system and its outcomes.
- Governance: review boards or approval steps before launch, as Google does with its review bodies that assess products against its AI Principles.
- Documentation and logging, so decisions can be traced and audited.
- Escalation and human oversight, especially for decisions that affect people.
- A way for affected users to appeal or report problems.
## Shared responsibility
A provider is responsible for its platform and models, but the company deploying an application decides the use case, prompts, data and guardrails, so it stays answerable for the application's behavior.
W: Clear ownership and oversight: named owners, review, escalation and human oversight make it possible to say who answers for outcomes and to correct problems.

D=Wait for regulators: accountability is an internal governance practice that should start now; undocumented approvals also make later audits impossible.
T: Accountability means named human owners, governance and oversight answer for AI outcomes; models cannot be held responsible.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.3 applied use-case
S: A bank uses a machine learning model to decide credit-limit increases. Regulators and customers ask which factors drove each individual decision, such as income or payment history. Which Google Cloud capability addresses this need?
A: Generating a longer prompt so the model describes its own reasoning, which is then treated as proof of the real causes
B: Explainable AI feature-based explanations, which show how much each input feature contributed to a model's prediction
C: Increasing the training data volume, which lets the bank skip documenting how individual decisions were made
D: Autoscaling the model endpoint, which provides more capacity but does not reveal what influenced any single prediction
K: B
G: ## Explainability
**Explainability** is the ability to describe why a model produced a particular output in terms people can understand. It supports trust, debugging, regulatory review and the right of people to question decisions.
Google Cloud's Explainable AI (documented as Vertex Explainable AI; Vertex AI is now part of Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform) offers two kinds of explanations for ML models:
- **Feature-based explanations** (feature attributions) show how much each input feature contributed to a prediction, for example how income and payment history influenced a credit decision.
- **Example-based explanations** use nearest-neighbor search to return similar examples from the training data, helping you see why the model behaved as it did.
## Caution with gen AI
A text rationale written by a language model is not guaranteed to reflect the real cause of an output, so it should not be treated as an audit trail.
W: Feature-based explanations: attributions quantify each feature's contribution to a specific prediction, which answers the question of which factors drove the decision.
X: A=Self-described reasoning: a model's narrative may sound plausible without reflecting what actually influenced the result, so it is not reliable evidence.
C=More training data: more data may improve accuracy but does not explain any individual decision; documentation still matters.
D=Autoscaling: this adjusts serving capacity and has no relationship to the factors behind a prediction.
T: Explainability shows why a model produced a result; Explainable AI feature attributions show each input's contribution.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.3 applied comparison
S: A data science lead at a manufacturer says that an image-defect model sometimes classifies good parts as faulty with high confidence. The team wants to see which training images the model considers most similar to a misclassified part, so it can decide whether to gather more data. Which type of explanation fits best?
A: Feature-based explanations, because they rank the importance of every input column in a spreadsheet of tabular business data
B: A transparency report written for customers, because it covers intended use but is not generated per misclassified image
C: Example-based explanations, which return the training examples most similar to the input to reveal gaps in the data or model
D: Pseudonymization of the images, since replacing identifiers with tokens reveals why the model misjudged a part
K: C
G: ## Two styles of explanation
- **Feature-based explanations** attribute a prediction to input features (for example, which pixels or columns mattered most).
- **Example-based explanations** use nearest-neighbor search to return the examples, usually from the training set, most similar to an input. If the neighbors of a misclassified part are all dark parts from the wrong class, the training data probably needs more examples of good parts; if neighbors are from the right class, the model itself may be too simple.
Google Cloud documents example-based explanations as helping to improve data or models, interpret new data, detect outliers and decide which cases deserve human labeling.
## Matching question to method
"Which training examples look like this one?" points to example-based explanations. "Which input contributed how much?" points to feature-based ones.
W: Example-based explanations: returning the most similar training examples for a misclassified part exposes data gaps and tells the team whether to gather more data.
X: A=Feature importance for spreadsheet columns: attributions answer a different question, which input mattered, and the scenario asks which training examples resemble the part.
B=Customer transparency report: a general document does not analyze an individual misclassification.
D=Pseudonymization: replacing identifiers is a privacy technique and says nothing about why a model made an error.
T: Example-based explanations show similar training examples to diagnose data or model gaps; feature-based ones show input contributions.
V: Explainable AI

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.3 applied use-case
S: A university plans a gen AI tutoring assistant that will use student records, including grades and counseling notes. The privacy office wants to lower privacy risk without making the assistant useless. Which TWO measures should the project team adopt? (Select TWO)
A: Include all available student fields in every prompt so the assistant never lacks context, and rely on the model to ignore sensitive details
B: Minimize the data used to what the tutoring task needs and de-identify or mask sensitive fields, such as with Sensitive Data Protection
C: Restrict who can access the assistant and the underlying records using least-privilege access controls, and review how data is handled
D: Store counseling notes in public documents to speed up retrieval, since the assistant will filter what to show to each student
E: Disable all logging and documentation so that no record of data handling exists, which means no record can be exposed
K: B,C
G: ## Reducing privacy risk in practice
Privacy by design applies at every step of an AI solution:
- **Data minimization**: collect and send only what the purpose requires.
- **De-identification**: mask, tokenize or remove sensitive values before they reach prompts, tuning data or logs. Sensitive Data Protection can discover and de-identify sensitive elements.
- **Access control**: least-privilege permissions on the assistant and on the source data, so users only see data they are entitled to.
- **Governance**: documented purpose, retention rules and periodic review.
## Anti-patterns
Relying on a model to "ignore" sensitive input, exposing data broadly and relying on filtering later, or removing logs entirely (which removes the ability to audit and respond).
W: B=Minimization and de-identification: limiting fields to what tutoring needs and masking sensitive values lowers exposure while keeping the assistant useful.
C=Least-privilege access and review: restricting who reaches the assistant and the records keeps students from seeing data they should not, and review keeps handling accountable.
X: A=All fields in every prompt: sending everything maximizes exposure, and a model cannot be relied on to disregard sensitive details.
D=Public documents: storing sensitive notes in publicly reachable documents is a breach risk, and downstream filtering is not an access control.
E=No logging: removing records eliminates audit and incident response, but does not protect the data that is still processed.
T: Lower privacy risk with data minimization, de-identification, least-privilege access and reviewed handling.
