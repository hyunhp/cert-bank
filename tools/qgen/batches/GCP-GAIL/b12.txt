@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.1 applied use-case
S: A regional insurer wants its claims staff to spend less time writing meeting summaries and drafting routine emails. The company has no data science team, needs results this quarter, and the task does not depend on any proprietary data. Which approach to the gen AI solution best fits?
A: Adopt a ready-made assistant built into the productivity tools the staff already use, and roll it out to the claims team
B: Train a new foundation model on the insurer's historical emails so that the drafts match the company's writing style
C: Build a custom agent on a developer platform with its own retrieval pipeline, evaluation harness and deployment process
D: Fine-tune an open model on rented accelerators and host it in the insurer's own project behind a private endpoint
K: A
G: ## Types of gen AI solutions
Gen AI solutions sit on a spectrum of effort and control:
- **Ready-made applications**: assistants such as Gemini for Google Workspace or Gemini Enterprise that employees use directly. Little or no engineering is needed.
- **Configured or low-code solutions**: pre-built products you point at your own data or processes, for example search or conversational agents.
- **Custom solutions**: applications and agents built on a platform such as Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform (formerly Vertex AI), where the team chooses models, grounding and evaluation.
- **Model-level work**: fine-tuning or training models, which needs the most data, skills and time.
## Matching effort to need
Google Cloud's guidance is to start with managed services and pre-trained models and add complexity only when the need justifies it. Summaries and routine emails are common tasks that ready-made assistants already handle.
W: Ready-made assistant: the task is generic, no proprietary data is needed, there is no data science team and the deadline is short, so an assistant in the tools staff already use delivers value fastest at the lowest effort.
X: B=Train a new foundation model: training from scratch needs very large data, compute and expertise, and it is far out of proportion to writing summaries and emails.
C=Custom agent with its own pipeline: this adds engineering and operating work for a generic need, which a team without data scientists cannot deliver this quarter.
D=Fine-tune and self-host an open model: it requires ML skills, accelerators and hosting operations, and nothing in the scenario calls for custom model behaviour.
T: Pick the lightest solution type that meets the need, and build custom only when ready-made products cannot.
V: Gemini for Google Workspace, Gemini Enterprise

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.1 applied comparison
S: A project team is listing what must be true for a customer-service gen AI assistant before it is built. Select the TWO items that are technical constraints rather than business requirements. (Select TWO)
A: The assistant must reduce the average time to resolve a customer inquiry compared with today
B: The knowledge articles that answers depend on are stored in several formats and are not yet searchable
C: Customer satisfaction scores must not fall during the first six months after the launch
D: The assistant must answer within a few seconds and fit into the existing contact center's systems
E: The service desk wants the assistant to ease the load on human agents during seasonal peaks
K: B,D
G: ## Business requirements vs technical constraints
Choosing a gen AI solution starts with two lists:
- **Business requirements** say what outcome the organization wants and how success is judged: faster resolution, higher satisfaction, lower workload, a launch date or budget.
- **Technical constraints** describe the conditions the solution must live within: the state and format of data, how quickly it must respond, which systems it must integrate with, and security or residency rules.
## Why separate them
Requirements decide whether a solution is worth building, and constraints decide which solutions are feasible. A team that mixes them may pick a tool that serves the goal on paper but cannot reach the data or fit the environment.
W: B=Data format and access: knowledge that sits in many formats and cannot be searched is a limit on what the solution can use, which is a data readiness constraint.
D=Latency and integration: response time and fit with existing contact center systems describe how the solution must work technically, so they limit which products and architectures are feasible.
X: A=Faster resolution: this is the outcome the business wants and the measure of success, so it is a business requirement.
C=Satisfaction must not fall: it states a business result to protect and is judged by customers, not by system design.
E=Ease the peak load: it names the business reason for the project, and a solution is selected to meet it.
T: Business requirements state the outcome; technical constraints are the data, performance and integration limits the solution must fit.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.1 applied use-case
S: A manufacturer wants employees to ask natural-language questions about thousands of internal maintenance manuals and incident reports stored in Google Cloud. Answers must cite the source document, and the company wants to avoid training or tuning its own model. Which solution best matches this business need?
A: A search and question-answering solution grounded in the company's own documents, so answers come with links to their sources
B: A larger foundation model with a higher temperature setting so that it produces more varied answers from its general knowledge
C: A model fine-tuned once on the manuals so the content becomes part of the weights and no source documents are needed later
D: An image-generation model that turns the maintenance manuals into diagrams for the engineers to review
K: A
G: ## Choosing the right solution for the need
Once the need is clear, match its shape to a pattern:
- Questions over the company's own documents with citations point to **grounding or RAG**, for example search and Q&A products such as Agent Search or a custom RAG application.
- Content creation (text, images, video) points to a generative model.
- Handling many customer conversations points to conversational agents.
## Why not retrain
Fine-tuning changes how a model behaves but is a poor way to add documents that change often and must be cited. Grounding supplies the current documents at request time and lets the answer point to them, which supports auditability.
W: Grounded search and Q&A: it answers from the manuals and incident reports, keeps content current as documents change, shows the source, and needs no model training.
X: B=Larger model, higher temperature: general knowledge does not contain the company's internal documents, and more randomness raises the chance of invented answers.
C=One-time fine-tuning: the content would go stale as documents change, and a tuned model cannot reliably point to the specific document behind a statement.
D=Image generation: the need is to find answers in text, not to produce pictures, so the modality does not match.
T: Questions over enterprise documents with citations call for grounded search or RAG, not retraining.
V: Agent Search, Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.1 applied tradeoff
S: A retailer's executive sponsor asks the team to start the gen AI program by buying licenses for every available tool and rolling them out company-wide at once. The team has not yet picked a first problem. According to Google Cloud's recommended approach, what should happen first?
A: Define the business problem and the outcomes to be achieved, break them into measurable KPIs, then choose the solution
B: Select the largest available model and decide afterward which departments will find a use for it
C: Collect every dataset in the company into one location and delay any use case until the collection is finished
D: Launch to all employees right away and let the usage statistics reveal which problems the tools should solve
K: A
G: ## Start from the problem, not the technology
Google Cloud's AI and ML guidance says that before starting any AI project you need a clear understanding of the business problems to be solved and the required outcomes. Outline the business objectives and break them down into measurable KPIs.
## Typical order of steps
- Identify a business problem or opportunity and the outcomes that matter.
- Define KPIs, including how the baseline is measured today.
- Check data readiness and technical constraints.
- Choose the solution type, and pilot with a limited group.
- Integrate into the organization, train users and measure against the KPIs.
- Expand what works.
Skipping the first steps risks tools without adoption and results nobody can judge.
W: Define the problem and KPIs first: the problem and its measurable outcomes drive every later choice, from solution type to data and from pilot design to success criteria.
X: B=Pick the biggest model first: starting with a technology and hoping to find uses reverses the order, and a bigger model is not automatically a better or cheaper fit.
C=Centralize all data first: collecting everything delays value and ignores that the needed data depends on the chosen use case.
D=Launch to everyone: without a defined problem and baseline, usage statistics cannot show whether business outcomes improved, and a wide launch raises risk.
T: Business problem and KPIs first, then data and technical fit, then solution, pilot and scale.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.1 applied tradeoff
S: A bank has identified three possible gen AI projects. One would draft replies for a small group of internal analysts, using documents already stored in a governed repository. The other two need new data pipelines, affect customers directly and have unclear success measures. How should the bank proceed to build confidence and learn quickly?
A: Start with the internal analyst project as a pilot, measure it against agreed KPIs and use the lessons to scale to the others
B: Start all three projects at the same time so that the bank can compare them and keep the one that happens to perform best
C: Begin with the customer-facing projects, because their visibility to customers makes executive support easy to secure
D: Delay all three until a single enterprise-wide platform exists that covers every future use case and data source
K: A
G: ## Pilot first, then scale
Google Cloud's guidance on cost and operations favours an incremental approach: begin with a manageable use case, learn, then increase complexity. Good pilot candidates have:
- Clear, measurable outcomes.
- Data that is ready and governed.
- Limited and lower-risk users, such as an internal team.
## What a pilot gives you
- Real measurements against the baseline.
- Feedback from users to improve prompts, grounding and workflows.
- Evidence for the next investment decision, and lessons about risks before customers are exposed.
W: Internal pilot with KPIs: it uses ready, governed data, has a limited audience and clear measures, so the bank learns quickly with low risk and can apply the results to the larger projects.
X: B=All three at once: this splits attention and resources, and two of the projects lack clear success measures, so the comparison would not be meaningful.
C=Start customer-facing for visibility: errors would reach customers before the bank has learned how the solution behaves, and the success measures are unclear.
D=Wait for the perfect platform: waiting delays learning, and an all-purpose platform designed without real use cases is likely to miss real needs.
T: Start with a contained, measurable use case on ready data, learn from it, then scale.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.1 applied use-case
S: After a successful pilot of a gen AI writing assistant, a company plans the move to wider use. Select the TWO activities that most help integrate the solution into the organization. (Select TWO)
A: Train employees on how to use the tool and give them a channel to report problems and share useful prompts
B: Hide the tool's limitations from users so that early impressions of quality stay high during the rollout
C: Involve the business owners, security, legal and IT teams so that processes and policies are set before the rollout widens
D: Retire the pilot's KPIs and baseline so that the wider rollout is judged only on how many employees log in
E: Give the tool to every team without guidance so that each team invents its own rules for acceptable use
K: A,C
G: ## Integrating gen AI into the organization
A tool that works in a pilot still has to fit into how people work. Common elements of a successful rollout:
- **People**: training, clear guidance on what the tool is for, a way to give feedback and champions in each team.
- **Governance**: agreed policies on data use, review of outputs and responsibilities, with business, security, legal and IT teams involved early.
- **Process**: changes to workflows so the tool is part of the work and not an extra step.
- **Measurement**: the same KPIs and baseline carried from the pilot, so the effect of scaling can be seen.
## Common failure
Technology is delivered but users are not prepared, policies are missing or nobody tracks outcomes. Adoption then stays low or risks grow unnoticed.
W: A=Training and feedback channel: informed users get better results, and shared feedback and prompts improve the solution and increase adoption.
C=Cross-functional involvement: business, security, legal and IT input sets policies and process changes before the rollout widens, so risks are managed and the tool fits real workflows.
X: B=Hide limitations: users who do not know that outputs can be wrong are more likely to trust errors, and trust built on hidden facts collapses when problems appear.
D=Retire the KPIs: logins say nothing about whether the business outcomes improved, and the baseline is needed to prove impact.
E=No guidance: every team would set its own, possibly unsafe rules, which creates inconsistent practice and governance gaps.
T: Successful integration combines user training and feedback with cross-functional governance and continued measurement.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.1 applied use-case
S: A telecom provider introduced a gen AI assistant for its contact center. Leadership wants a measure that shows the effect on business operations rather than on the model itself. Which metric fits that purpose best?
A: The share of customer chats that the assistant resolves without handing over to a human agent
B: The percentage of tokens in the model's answers that match a reference answer word for word
C: The accelerator utilization of the serving infrastructure during the busiest hour of the week
D: The number of different prompts that the engineering team has tested during development
K: A
G: ## Three families of gen AI KPIs
Google Cloud describes three families of measures:
- **Model quality**: how good the output is, measured with computed metrics against references or with judge models and human raters for qualities such as coherence, groundedness, safety and summarization quality.
- **System quality**: how well the platform runs, such as latency, error rate, uptime, throughput and accelerator utilization.
- **Business operational**: the effect on the work and outcomes of the business. For customer service this includes containment rate, average handle time, customer satisfaction and churn.
## Using them together
A leader judging business impact needs the third family, while the first two help the team find the reasons behind the result.
W: Containment rate: the share of chats resolved without a human agent shows the effect on workload and cost, which is a business operational KPI.
X: B=Word-for-word match: this is a model quality measure for bounded output, and it says nothing about business operations; free-form answers can be right with different words.
C=Accelerator utilization: it is a system measure of infrastructure efficiency, not of what the assistant changes for the business.
D=Number of prompts tested: it describes engineering activity, not an outcome for customers or operations.
T: Model quality, system quality and business operational KPIs answer different questions; business impact needs operational ones.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.1 applied troubleshooting
S: A pilot of a gen AI search tool scored well on every model quality test, and the platform shows low latency and no errors. Three months in, only a small share of the target employees use it, and the sponsor sees no change in the business results that justified the project. What should the team do next?
A: Measure adoption and user satisfaction, gather feedback on why employees avoid it, and track the business KPIs against the baseline
B: Conclude that the project succeeded because the model quality and system measures are good, and report that result as the business outcome
C: Replace the foundation model with a different one, because low usage always means that the model is the problem
D: Stop collecting measurements, because additional numbers will not change how employees feel about the tool
K: A
G: ## Good model, no impact
Model quality and system quality KPIs show that the technology works. They do not show that anyone uses it or benefits from it. Google Cloud notes that many organizations measure only computation-based model quality and miss adoption and business outcomes.
## What to look at
- **Adoption and engagement**: how many of the target users use the tool, how often and for which tasks.
- **User feedback**: why people do not trust or need the tool, where it does not fit their workflow.
- **Business KPIs against the baseline**: whether time saved, cost or satisfaction actually changed.
The findings then guide training, workflow changes, grounding in better data or a change of use case.
W: Measure adoption, feedback and business KPIs: the technical numbers are already good, so the missing information is why people do not use the tool and whether the business outcome moved.
X: B=Declare success from technical scores: the project was justified by business results, and technical quality is only a precondition for those.
C=Swap the model: nothing shows that the model is the cause, and low usage often comes from workflow fit, trust or training, not model choice.
D=Stop measuring: without measurement, the team cannot find the cause or show value, and the project loses its basis for investment.
T: Technical KPIs do not prove impact; add adoption, feedback and business KPIs measured against a baseline.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.1 applied tradeoff
S: An online retailer's team launches a gen AI shopping assistant. Average cart size rises, but the time customers need to add a first item to the cart also rises, and the team had treated short time-to-cart as important. How should the team interpret this?
A: One KPI can improve while another moves the other way, so the team should weigh the KPIs together against the business goal
B: The result proves the assistant failed, because a single KPI moving the wrong way always cancels any improvement elsewhere
C: The time-to-cart KPI should be ignored from now on, because it should never be tracked next to cart size
D: The shopper-assistant pilot must be repeated until every KPI improves at once, and no launch should happen before that
K: A
G: ## KPIs can pull against each other
Google Cloud's gen AI KPI guidance points out that, when you change an AI system, there is not always one direction of movement. Its example comes from retail: a more engaging chatbot can raise cart size while time-to-cart rises, even though a short time-to-cart was previously important.
## What to do
- Choose a small set of KPIs that links to the business goal, such as revenue per visit.
- Decide in advance which KPIs matter most and what trade-offs are acceptable.
- Review the KPIs together and look at the combined effect, not each in isolation.
W: Weigh the KPIs together: engagement can raise basket size and also lengthen browsing, so the decision depends on the combined effect on the goal, such as revenue per visit.
X: B=One bad KPI cancels all gains: a single metric moving the wrong way does not by itself outweigh other gains, and the net effect on the goal decides.
C=Drop time-to-cart: removing a metric because it moved hides information; the correct step is to decide how much it matters to the goal.
D=Require every KPI to improve: real systems rarely improve everything at once, and a demand like that blocks useful launches indefinitely.
T: KPIs can conflict, so pick them to match the business goal and judge them together.

Impact means change compared with a baseline, expressed in business terms. For intelligent document processing, Google Cloud lists measures such as processing time and extraction accuracy. S: A board member asks what Google's Secure AI Framework (SAIF) is and why the company should care. Which description is most accurate?
A: A conceptual framework from Google for securing AI systems, helping organizations address AI security challenges across the AI lifecycle
B: A single product that scans every model and blocks all attacks without further security work by the company
C: A certification that Google Cloud grants to AI applications once they pass a fixed audit, after which security reviews stop
D: A set of model-quality benchmarks that rank foundation models by accuracy, speed and the cost of each answer

G: ## What SAIF is
Google's Secure AI Framework (SAIF) is a conceptual framework for secure AI systems. Google Cloud presents it as a practical approach to AI security challenges and offers companion resources: an introduction to the framework, a risk self-assessment that shows which risks inherent to AI development may affect you, and guidance on applying SAIF through the AI development lifecycle.
## Why it matters
- AI adds new risks, such as data poisoning, prompt injection and model theft, to ordinary software security.
- A shared framework gives security, data and business teams the same vocabulary and checklist.
- It is guidance for people and processes, which tools such as Security Command Center and Model Armor then help carry out.
W: Conceptual framework for secure AI: SAIF guides organizations in thinking through AI-specific security risks and controls across the lifecycle, and it supplies resources such as a risk self-assessment.
X: B=A product that blocks all attacks: SAIF is a framework and not a scanner, and no single tool replaces ongoing security practice.
C=A certification: it is not an audit or label, and security work continues for as long as the system runs.
D=Model-quality benchmarks: SAIF is about security risk, not about ranking models by accuracy or cost.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.2 applied tradeoff
S: A company plans a customer-facing gen AI application. The project lead proposes building and launching it first and adding security reviews after launch, to save time. What does Google Cloud's AI security guidance say about this plan?
A: Security should be built in from the start and be part of every stage from design through operation, because adding it later is harder
B: Security reviews are best left to the end, because threats to AI systems cannot be known until real users are online
C: Only the training stage needs security attention, since a model that has already been trained cannot be attacked afterwards by anyone
D: Security applies mainly to the cloud infrastructure, so the data, models and prompts need no separate controls
K: A
G: ## Security across the AI lifecycle
Google Cloud's AI and ML security guidance asks for a holistic approach that starts at the initial concept of a solution and extends through development, deployment and ongoing operations. It states that it is easier to include security and compliance controls early in design and development than to add them after development.
## What each stage needs
- **Plan**: define security goals and identify risks such as data poisoning, model inversion and adversarial attacks.
- **Data**: protect training data, limit access, minimize and de-identify sensitive data.
- **Build**: secure pipelines and artifacts, use least-privilege identities.
- **Deploy and run**: screen prompts and responses, monitor and audit, and be ready to respond.
This is sometimes called shifting security left.
W: Build security in from the start: controls added early cost less and cover data, pipelines, deployment and operation, so risks are reduced before real users or data are exposed.
X: B=Review only at the end: many AI risks, like poisoned training data or excessive permissions, can be found and avoided long before launch, and the guidance names continuing assessment as the system changes.
C=Only the training stage: deployed models can still face prompt injection, data leakage and misuse, so protection must continue in production.
D=Only infrastructure: data, models, prompts and outputs are assets that need their own controls in addition to a secure platform.
T: Treat security as part of every stage of the AI lifecycle, beginning at design.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.2 applied use-case
S: A health-tech company discovers that an internal contractor could modify the data set used to train its diagnostic model. Leaders worry that changed examples could quietly make the model behave wrongly. Which risk is this, and which control addresses it first?
A: Data poisoning, addressed by controlling who can access and change training data and by logging and reviewing changes
B: Prompt injection, addressed by filtering the prompts that users type into the application after it has launched for customers
C: Model drift, addressed by retraining the model more often on whatever new data is available
D: Over-provisioning, addressed by buying additional compute capacity for the training environment
K: A
G: ## Data poisoning
Training data shapes model behavior. If someone adds or alters examples, the model can learn wrong or harmful patterns. Google Cloud's AI security guidance lists data poisoning, model inversion and adversarial attacks as typical attack vectors to plan for, and says that training data poses the largest risks for introducing bias and data leakage.
## Controls for training data
- Least-privilege access, so only needed identities can read or write the data.
- Governance and logging of data access and changes, for audit and detection.
- Integrity checks and lineage, which show where data came from and what changed.
## Other risks in this list
Prompt injection targets a running application through user input. Drift is natural change in data over time. Neither is the same as tampering with training data.
W: Data poisoning with access control and logging: restricting who can change training data and recording changes prevents and reveals tampering at the point where the risk is introduced.
X: B=Prompt injection filtering: it protects the running application from hostile inputs, but the risk here is altered training examples, which filtering user prompts cannot prevent.
C=Retrain more often: retraining on data that may be tampered with could spread the problem, and drift is a different issue.
D=More compute: capacity has no effect on who can change data, so it leaves the risk untouched.
T: Protect training data with least-privilege access and logging to defend against data poisoning.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.2 applied use-case
S: A company runs a model-training pipeline on Google Cloud. A developer proposes giving the pipeline's identity the broad basic Owner-type role so that it never fails because of missing permissions. What does Google Cloud's IAM guidance recommend instead?
A: Create a dedicated service account for the pipeline and grant it only the predefined roles on the resources it needs
B: Reuse the personal account of the engineer who built the pipeline, so access follows that person's permissions
C: Share one powerful service account among all pipelines and applications, which makes access management easier
D: Grant the basic role at the organization level so that every project inherits the access the pipeline might need
K: A
G: ## Least privilege with IAM
Identity and Access Management (IAM) controls who can do what on which resource. Google Cloud's guidance for using IAM securely says:
- Do not grant basic roles in production unless there is no alternative. They contain thousands of permissions across all services. Use the most limited predefined or custom roles instead.
- Treat each application component as its own trust boundary: create a separate service account for each service and grant only what it needs.
- Grant roles at the smallest scope that works, because allow policies are inherited from parent resources.
## For AI pipelines
Google Cloud's AI security guidance gives an example: a training pipeline's service account may read from one specific Cloud Storage bucket and write model artifacts to the model registry, and nothing else. This limits the damage from mistakes or compromise.
W: Dedicated service account with narrow roles: separate identities with only the needed predefined roles on specific resources follow least privilege and limit the damage if the pipeline is misused or compromised.
X: B=Engineer's personal account: automation tied to a person breaks when they leave or change roles, and it ties the pipeline to that person's wider access.
C=One shared powerful account: shared accounts mix trust boundaries, so a problem in one workload exposes everything the account can reach.

G: ## Protecting the conversation itself
Some AI risks occur in the content of the interaction:
- **Prompt injection and jailbreaks**: inputs that try to override instructions or push the model into unintended actions.

- **Harmful content**: toxic or unsafe output.
Model Armor is a Google Cloud service that screens prompts and responses against these risks. It inspects the incoming prompt, passes the prompt (or a sanitized version) to the model, then inspects the response before it returns to the user. IAM controls who may call the service, SCC reports posture and threats across the environment, and audit logs provide records. Each is needed, but none inspects the text of a conversation as it happens.
W: Model Armor: it checks both what users send and what the model returns, so tricked instructions and leaking confidential data are caught at the point where they occur.
X: B=Security Command Center posture reports: they find misconfigurations and risks in the environment, and do not filter individual chat content.
C=IAM allow policies: they limit which identities may use the service, but an authorized user can still send a hostile prompt.
D=Cloud Audit Logs: logs support investigation after an event and do not stop a request in progress.

@ GCP-GAIL D4 T4.2 applied definition
S: A CIO asks what "secure by design" means when Google Cloud describes the security of its infrastructure and services. Which statement is accurate?
A: Security is built into the whole development lifecycle, so threats are anticipated and mitigated by design choices from the start
B: Security features are added after a product is released, once customer incidents show which threats actually matter

D: Only the physical data centers are protected by Google, while the software and the services that run in them are left entirely to the customer

G: ## Secure by design and secure by default
Google Cloud's security architecture guidance distinguishes two related ideas:
- **Secure by design**: proactively incorporate security through the whole development lifecycle. It means anticipating threats and vulnerabilities early and making design choices that reduce risk, using secure coding practices, layered defenses and hardened components.
- **Secure by default**: system settings start in a secure mode, so users and administrators need to do less to protect the system and every user gets a baseline of security.
Google Cloud's recommendations include a layered approach, hardened and attested infrastructure and encryption of data at rest and in transit.
## Why it matters for AI
Gen AI workloads run on this foundation. The same design principle applies to your own AI applications: decide on risks and controls during design, not after an incident.
W: Security built into the whole lifecycle: secure by design means anticipating threats early and choosing designs, components and layers that mitigate them from the beginning.
X: B=Add security after release: reacting to incidents is the opposite of designing for security from the start.
C=Open by default: that contradicts secure by default, where the initial configuration is safe and needs little customer action.
D=Physical protection only: Google's approach covers hardware, software, services and operations, with shared responsibilities where customers configure their own resources.
T: Secure by design means anticipating threats and building controls in from the start, across the whole lifecycle.

Securing a workload does not end at deployment. Google Cloud's AI security guidance asks teams to monitor, evaluate and prepare to respond:

G: ## Protecting data and artifacts
Training data and model files are intellectual property and often contain sensitive information. Google Cloud's AI security guidance combines several layers:
