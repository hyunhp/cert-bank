@ GCP-GAIL D1 T1.2 applied use-case
S: A pharmacy chain is building an assistant that answers staff questions about its drug formulary. The data lake holds formulary documents, but also store security-camera footage, social media posts and years of unrelated marketing spreadsheets. Which data principle should guide what the team feeds into the solution?
A: Relevance: use the data that matches the assistant's purpose, rather than everything that happens to be available
B: Volume: the more data of any kind the team connects, the better the formulary answers will be
C: Format: only video files should be used, because video is the richest data type and covers every other kind
D: Cost: the cheapest data to store should be chosen first, whatever its connection to formulary questions
K: A
G: ## Data relevance
Data quality for gen AI is not only about errors. Teams judge data on several qualities: **completeness**, **consistency**, **relevance**, **availability**, **cost** and **format**. Relevance asks whether the data actually relates to the task. A formulary assistant needs current formulary and pharmacy policy documents; camera footage and old marketing sheets add noise, cost and privacy exposure without improving answers.
## Business implication
Curating a smaller, relevant data set usually gives better answers and a lower risk than connecting every source. Choosing data is a business decision made with the people who own the content.
W: Relevance: the assistant should be built from the data that relates to formulary questions, because unrelated content adds cost and noise and does not improve answers.
X: B=Volume: more data helps only when it is relevant to the task; unrelated footage and spreadsheets dilute the useful content.
C=Format: no single format is best for every task, and the formulary lives mainly in documents, so insisting on video does not match the need.
D=Cost: storage cost is one factor, but choosing data because it is cheap ignores whether it answers the questions staff ask.
T: Choose data for its relevance to the use case, not for its volume or its convenience.

@ GCP-GAIL D1 T1.2 applied comparison
S: A retail analytics lead explains why a foundation model needs no purchased labels during its first training, while a team that fine-tunes the model to recognize "damaged item" complaints does need them. Which statement describes the difference between labeled and unlabeled data?
A: Labeled data is stored in databases, while unlabeled data is stored only as images and video
B: Labeled data comes from customers, while unlabeled data is created by the company's own staff
C: Labeled data carries the right answer for each example; unlabeled data has no such tag
D: Labeled data has been encrypted for security, while unlabeled data has been left in plain text
K: C
G: ## Labeled and unlabeled data
- **Labeled data**: each example is paired with the correct answer or category, such as a complaint tagged "damaged item". People or processes usually create these labels, which costs time and money.
- **Unlabeled data**: raw examples with no tag, such as large volumes of text or images.
Foundation models learn broad patterns from very large amounts of unlabeled data. When a company adapts a model to a narrow, specific task, a smaller labeled data set shows the model what the right output looks like.
## Business implication
Labeling is a real cost. Leaders should ask whether prompting or grounding can solve the problem before paying for large labeled sets.
W: Labeled versus unlabeled: labeled examples carry the correct answer or category, which supervised tuning needs, while unlabeled examples have no such tag.
X: A=Storage type: labels are about the presence of an answer tag, not about whether data sits in a database or is an image or video.
B=Data source: labels do not depend on who produced the data; customer-written and staff-written examples can be either labeled or unlabeled.
D=Encryption: protecting data is a security matter and is unrelated to whether examples have answer tags.
T: Labeled data pairs each example with the correct answer; unlabeled data has none, and labels cost effort.

Ask what the item actually is:

Knowing the layer tells a leader who runs the component, how much customization is possible, and which team in the company will own it.

B=Model, infrastructure, platform: this swaps the first two layers, and a ready-to-use browser tool is an application rather than a platform for builders.
D=Agent, platform, infrastructure: compute capacity is not an agent, and a tool dispatchers open directly is the top layer, not the bottom one.
T: Compute is infrastructure, a trained system is a model, a finished tool people use is an application.

@ GCP-GAIL D1 T1.3 advanced tradeoff
S: An insurer wants a claims-triage experience that uses its own policy rules and data, behaves differently from any off-the-shelf product, and can later be extended with new steps. Which approach best fits that goal across the layers of the gen AI landscape?
A: Buy a ready-made application only, because applications can be reshaped into any company process without development work
B: Buy additional infrastructure only, because more compute automatically produces a claims-triage product
C: Use a model through its public chat interface with no connection to company data or processes
D: Build on a platform with a foundation model, grounded on company data, and add agent behavior for the triage steps
K: D
G: ## Buying higher or building lower
Moving down the stack gives more control and more work:
- **Applications** are quick to adopt but follow the vendor's design.
- **Platforms, models and agents** let a company use its own data, rules and workflow, at the price of design and operating effort.
- **Infrastructure** alone is raw capacity; it produces nothing without models and software on top.
A distinctive, extensible process such as claims triage points to building on a platform, grounding a model in company data, and adding agent behavior to carry out the steps.
## Business implication
Choose the highest layer that meets the differentiation need; go lower only where unique requirements justify the effort.
W: Build on a platform with a grounded model plus agent behavior: this uses the insurer's data and rules, allows a custom workflow, and can be extended with further steps.
X: A=Ready-made application only: applications follow the vendor's design and are not meant to be reshaped into a distinct claims process without building.
B=More infrastructure only: compute capacity is a prerequisite, not a solution, and it provides no triage logic, models or data connection.
C=Public chat interface: a model used without company data or process integration cannot apply the insurer's policy rules or carry out triage steps.
T: Distinct, extensible company processes call for building on platforms and models with agents; common needs are better met by applications.

@ GCP-GAIL D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: A logistics company wants a system that improves its warehouse-robot routes by trying actions in a simulator and receiving a reward for faster deliveries and a penalty for collisions. Which machine learning approach is this?
A: Supervised learning, because every simulator run is paired with a human-written label

G: ## Three ML approaches
- **Supervised learning** learns from examples paired with correct answers (labels).
- **Unsupervised learning** finds structure in unlabeled data, such as groups or patterns.
- **Reinforcement learning** learns by acting in an environment and receiving rewards or penalties, gradually favoring actions that earn more reward.

## Business implication
Matching the approach to the problem is a first step in any ML project: labeled answers, hidden structure, or sequential decisions with feedback.
W: Reinforcement learning: the system tries actions, is rewarded for speed and penalized for collisions, and improves through that feedback.

B=Unsupervised learning: it finds groups in unlabeled data and has no reward signal or goal to optimize.
D=Prompt engineering: it shapes a model's output by changing instructions and does not train a system through rewards.
T: Rewards and penalties from trial and error mean reinforcement learning.

@ GCP-GAIL D3 T3.1 applied tradeoff
S: A health insurer wants a model to draft explanations of coverage decisions for members. A wrong explanation could affect a member's care. Select TWO measures that best reduce the risk of an incorrect explanation reaching a member. (Select TWO)

Foundation models can produce fluent but wrong statements. Google-recommended practices combine several layers of protection:

- **Human in the loop** puts a person in the review path where errors are costly.
- Monitoring and evaluation continue after launch.
The higher the cost of an error, the more of these controls a solution needs.

For decisions that affect health, money or rights, plan for human review as a standing part of the process, not a temporary pilot step.

@ GCP-GAIL D3 T3.1 applied use-case
S: A law firm uses a model to draft client letters. The letters must always follow the firm's own house style and standard clauses, and this style rarely changes. Prompts with long instructions work, but they are repetitive and sometimes ignored. Which Google-recommended practice best addresses this?
A: Fine-tune the model on approved examples of the firm's letters so that the style becomes the default
B: Ground the model with a live web search so that the style adapts to the latest internet content

G: ## Choosing the right remedy
Different problems call for different techniques:

- **Fine-tuning**: adapts the model on example data, suitable for a consistent style, format or specialized task that does not change often.
- **Human in the loop**: reviews output but does not change the model's behavior.
Here the problem is style and format, not missing facts, so tuning on approved examples fits.
## Business implication
Tuning needs good example data and some effort, but it can shorten prompts and make results more consistent at scale.
W: Fine-tuning on approved letters: it teaches the model the firm's recurring style and standard clauses, so the behavior is consistent without repeating long instructions.
X: B=Live web search: the style is the firm's own and does not come from the internet, so web content would not make the model follow it.
C=Reviewer only: review catches problems but leaves the model's behavior unchanged, so staff would keep correcting the same issues.
D=Waiting for an upgrade: a new model is not trained on the firm's house style, so it cannot be assumed to match it.
T: A stable style or format need points to fine-tuning; missing or changing facts point to grounding.

@ GCP-GAIL D3 T3.1 applied troubleshooting

- Review outputs for balance before publishing.
- Write more specific prompts that describe the audience the company actually serves.
- Keep a human in the loop for public-facing content.
- Evaluate results across groups.

W: Data dependency: the training data probably over-represents some groups, so the team should review pictures and guide the model with more specific descriptions.

D=Faulty hardware: bad accelerators would cause slow or failed runs, not systematically skewed content.

S: A procurement team asks a model to compare three supplier offers. Answers come back as long paragraphs of different shapes each time, which makes comparison slow. The team wants the same layout each time with columns for price, delivery time and risk. Which prompt improvement is most direct?
A: State the required output format and constraints explicitly, such as a table with those three columns
B: Ask the same question several times and pick whichever answer happens to look best
C: Give the model no instructions about layout, since formatting is always decided by the user's software
D: Add more background about the company's history so the model can guess the layout
K: A
G: ## Be explicit about the output
Prompt engineering is the craft of designing inputs that lead to useful outputs. Strong prompts usually give a clear task, relevant context, constraints, and the desired **output format** (a table, a list, a length limit, a tone). Models follow explicit format instructions far more reliably than they guess a layout.
## Business implication
Specifying format and constraints is the cheapest improvement available. It needs no training or new infrastructure and makes results comparable and ready to use.
W: Explicit format and constraints: telling the model to return a table with price, delivery time and risk columns directly shapes the layout of every answer.
X: B=Repeat and choose: this wastes effort and still gives inconsistent layouts, because nothing in the prompt asks for a fixed structure.
C=No layout instructions: the model decides the structure when nothing is specified, which is exactly why the shapes vary.
D=Company history: background about the company does not tell the model which columns are wanted.
T: To get a consistent layout, say what format and constraints you want in the prompt.

@ GCP-GAIL D3 T3.2 applied tradeoff
S: A localization team asks a model to translate short product notices from English to Spanish, a common task the model handles well. An analyst proposes adding twenty carefully written examples to every request. What is the best advice?
A: Always add more examples, because every task must use many-shot prompting to be accurate
B: Start with a clear zero-shot instruction, and add examples only if results fall short
C: Use chain-of-thought prompting, since translation always needs step-by-step arithmetic
D: Use ReAct, since translation requires searching the web in between each sentence
K: B
G: ## Zero-, one- and few-shot prompting
- **Zero-shot**: only an instruction, no examples. Works well for common tasks.
- **One-shot**: a single example.
- **Few-shot**: several examples that show format, tone or edge cases.
Examples help when the task is unusual, the format is specific or the model misreads the instruction. For a common task, they add length, effort and maintenance with little gain.
## Business implication
Start simple and add complexity only when results justify it; this saves time and keeps prompts easy to maintain.
W: Zero-shot first: a clear instruction is enough for a familiar task like translation, and examples can be added later if testing shows a gap.
X: A=Always many examples: examples are optional and help mainly with unusual formats or tasks; they do not have to be added to every prompt.
C=Chain-of-thought: step-by-step reasoning helps with multi-step logic or arithmetic, not with straightforward translation.
D=ReAct: it combines reasoning with tool use, such as searches, which a translation of short notices does not need.
T: Start with zero-shot for common tasks and add examples only when results need them.

@ GCP-GAIL D3 T3.2 applied tradeoff
S: A team writes few-shot examples so the model can extract the vendor name, invoice date and amount from emails. Select TWO practices that make the examples work well. (Select TWO)
A: Show examples in one consistent format so the model can recognize the pattern
B: Make each example use a different structure, so the model learns to cope with any layout
C: Include examples that cover typical cases and a few tricky ones, such as a missing date
D: Use only examples that look identical, so the model never sees variation
E: Provide examples without the expected outputs, so the model has to invent the format
K: A,C
G: ## Writing good few-shot examples
Few-shot examples teach by showing. The model infers the pattern from the examples, so they must show it clearly:
- Keep the **format consistent** (the same fields, order and labels in every example).
- Include **representative variety** so the model learns what to do in normal and tricky situations.
- Show both the input and the expected output.
- Use a small number of strong examples rather than many weak ones.
## Business implication
A short time spent curating examples often improves quality more than a long period of re-wording instructions.
W: A=Consistent format: when each example follows the same layout, the model can recognize and repeat the pattern in its answer.
C=Typical plus tricky cases: examples that include an edge case, such as a missing date, show the model what to do when the input is imperfect.
X: B=Different structures: varying the format in each example hides the pattern, so the output format becomes unpredictable.
D=Identical examples: with no variation the model sees only one situation and may fail on cases that differ slightly.
E=No expected outputs: the examples teach by pairing inputs with the correct outputs; without outputs there is nothing to imitate.
T: Good few-shot examples share one format and cover typical and tricky cases, each with its expected output.

@ GCP-GAIL D3 T3.3 applied comparison
S: A company's product policies change every few weeks. An executive asks why the team plans to use retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) with the policy library instead of re-training the model each time a policy changes. What is the best answer?
A: RAG retrieves current documents at question time, so library updates change answers without retraining
B: RAG permanently rewrites the model's weights each night, which is quicker than any other approach
C: RAG removes the need to keep policy documents, because the model memorizes every version
D: RAG makes the model stop using prompts, since retrieval replaces the user's question
K: A
G: ## Why RAG suits changing knowledge
In **retrieval-augmented generation**, the system searches a knowledge source for passages relevant to the user's question, adds them to the prompt, and the model answers from that material. The model is not retrained: new or revised documents become available as soon as the library is updated.
## Comparison
- **Tuning or retraining** bakes information into the model, which is slow to repeat and fixed until the next update.
- **RAG** keeps knowledge outside the model, so it is current, auditable and replaceable.
## Business implication
For fast-changing content, RAG is typically cheaper, faster to update and easier to govern than repeated training.
W: Retrieval at question time: RAG pulls the current documents when a question is asked, so keeping the library up to date keeps answers up to date with no retraining.
X: B=Nightly weight rewriting: RAG does not change the model's weights; it supplies retrieved text alongside the question.
C=Model memorizes versions: the model does not memorize documents for later; the library remains the source and must be maintained.
D=Replacing prompts: retrieval adds context to the user's question and does not replace it.
T: RAG keeps knowledge outside the model, so updating documents updates answers without retraining.

@ GCP-GAIL D3 T3.3 applied troubleshooting
S: A team lowers the temperature setting of its assistant to the minimum, hoping that answers will stop containing invented facts. The assistant still states a nonexistent policy with confidence. Why did the change not solve the problem?
A: Temperature controls only the length of answers, so a lower value simply makes the replies shorter and shorter
B: A low temperature turns off safety settings, which then lets the model state false facts
C: Temperature controls randomness in wording, not truth; grounding in real sources addresses that
D: A low temperature makes the model forget its training data until it is reset
K: C
G: ## What sampling settings do
Parameters such as **temperature** and **top-p** shape how the model picks the next word. A low temperature makes output more deterministic and repeatable; a high one makes it more varied. None of them adds knowledge. A model with a gap in its knowledge can state a falsehood just as confidently with low randomness.
## What fixes invented facts
**Grounding** supplies verified sources the answer must follow, and evaluation and human review check the result.
## Business implication
Use low temperature for consistency, for example in policy explanations, but do not treat it as a factual-accuracy control.
W: Randomness versus truth: temperature affects how varied the wording is, not whether a statement is correct, so grounding in real sources is the remedy for invented facts.
X: A=Length: output length is controlled by a separate output-length setting, not by temperature.
B=Safety settings: temperature and safety settings are separate controls, and changing one does not switch off the other.
D=Forgetting: sampling settings do not alter what the model has learned; they apply only when it chooses output words.
T: Temperature changes variability, not factual accuracy; use grounding for facts.

@ GCP-GAIL D3 T3.3 applied tradeoff
S: A leadership team reviews statements about grounding an assistant in company documents. Select TWO statements that are correct. (Select TWO)
A: The quality and currency of the grounding sources limit how good the answers can be
B: Grounding permanently retrains the model so that review of its output is no longer needed
C: Grounding lets answers point to the sources they used, which helps users verify claims
D: Grounding works only with public internet pages and cannot use company documents
E: Grounding guarantees that every answer is correct regardless of the source content
K: A,C
G: ## What grounding does and does not do
Grounding connects a model to verifiable sources such as enterprise data, third-party data or world knowledge (for example through Google Search). The model answers using retrieved content and can show where it came from.
## Limits
- Output depends on the source: outdated or wrong documents lead to outdated or wrong answers.
- Grounding reduces hallucination but does not guarantee correctness, and it does not change the model's weights.
## Business implication
Grounding needs content owners who keep sources accurate, and it works best with review for important decisions.
W: A=Source dependence: if documents are wrong or stale, grounded answers inherit those problems, so source quality sets the ceiling.
C=Verifiability: because answers draw on specific sources, they can reference them and users can check claims.
X: B=Permanent retraining: grounding supplies information at answer time without changing the model, and review remains advisable.
D=Public pages only: grounding can use enterprise data and third-party data as well as world data.
E=Guaranteed correctness: grounding lowers the chance of invented facts, but errors in sources or misreadings can still occur.
T: Grounding links answers to sources and depends on their quality; it reduces, but does not eliminate, errors.

@ GCP-GAIL D3 T3.3 applied use-case

A: Ground only in the subscriber reports, because public news can never be combined with enterprise data
B: Ground only with Google Search, because subscriber reports are available on the public web to everyone
C: Use no grounding and rely on training, since training data always contains the morning's news
D: Ground in the firm's reports for private content and use Grounding with Google Search for current news
K: D
G: ## Combining grounding sources
Grounding can draw on different kinds of data:
- **Enterprise data**: the company's own documents and databases, retrieved through a RAG approach such as Agent Search.
- **Third-party data**: licensed sources from partners.
- **World data**: current public information, for example through Grounding with Google Search.
Each source covers a different gap, and an assistant can use more than one. Subscriber reports are private and paid, so only enterprise grounding can reach them; this morning's news is outside any training data, so web-based grounding is needed.
## Business implication
Matching sources to question types gives answers that are both specific to the firm and current.
W: Both sources: private subscriber reports come from enterprise grounding, and current public news comes from Grounding with Google Search, so the assistant covers both parts of the question.
X: A=Reports only: the assistant would lack current public information, and grounding sources can be combined rather than excluded from each other.
B=Search only: paid subscriber reports are private and not available on the public web, so search could not ground answers in them.
C=No grounding: a model's training ends at a cutoff, so it cannot know today's news, and it has no access to private reports.
T: Combine enterprise grounding for private content with Grounding with Google Search for current public information.
