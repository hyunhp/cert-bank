@ SAA-C03 D3 T3.1 expert troubleshooting
S: A nightly risk job on an m7i.2xlarge instance reads a 3 TiB dataset sequentially from one gp3 volume provisioned with 6,000 IOPS and 250 MiB/s. The run takes about three and a half hours and must finish in under one hour, so the team targets a sustained 1,000 MiB/s. CloudWatch shows VolumeReadBytes divided by VolumeReadOps averaging 256 KiB, about 1,000 read operations per second, and throughput flat at 250 MiB/s for the whole run. The EBS-optimized table lists the m7i.2xlarge with a baseline EBS throughput of 312.5 MB/s and a maximum of 1,250 MB/s, marked as sustainable for 30 minutes once every 24 hours. Which TWO changes are required to sustain the target rate for the full run? (Select TWO)
A: Use Elastic Volumes to raise the gp3 volume's provisioned throughput to 1,000 MiB/s while keeping its current 6,000 provisioned IOPS
B: Use Elastic Volumes to raise the gp3 volume's provisioned IOPS to 16,000 while keeping its current 250 MiB/s provisioned throughput
C: Move the job to an m7i.8xlarge, whose EBS bandwidth of 1,250 MB/s is a sustained rate rather than a 30-minute burst
D: Keep the m7i.2xlarge, because its listed maximum of 1,250 MB/s of EBS throughput already exceeds the planned 1,000 MiB/s
E: Replace the volume with a 4 TiB st1 volume, because Throughput Optimized HDD is designed for large sequential reads of this kind
K: A,C
G: ## Two ceilings: the volume and the instance
An EC2 instance gets the **lower** of two limits: what its attached volumes can deliver and what the instance type's EBS bandwidth allows.
- **Volume side (gp3)**: 3,000 IOPS and 125 MiB/s are included; you can provision up to 80,000 IOPS and up to 2,000 MiB/s, at up to 0.25 MiB/s per provisioned IOPS. So 1,000 MiB/s needs at least 4,000 provisioned IOPS (6,000 is enough).
- **Instance side**: the EBS-optimized table lists a baseline and a maximum. Sizes marked with footnote 1 (such as `m7i.2xlarge`) can hold the maximum for only **30 minutes once every 24 hours**, then fall back to the baseline (312.5 MB/s here). Larger sizes such as `m7i.8xlarge` (1,250 MB/s) sustain their rate indefinitely.
## Reading the evidence
`VolumeReadBytes / VolumeReadOps` gives the average I/O size: 256 KiB, the largest SSD I/O size. 1,000 ops x 256 KiB = 250 MiB/s, exactly the provisioned throughput. The volume is **throughput-bound, not IOPS-bound**: it uses 1,000 of its 6,000 IOPS.
## Working the numbers
- 3 TiB at 250 MiB/s is about 3.5 hours; at 1,000 MiB/s it is about 52 minutes.
- On an `m7i.2xlarge`, after the first 30 minutes the instance drops to 312.5 MB/s, so the run would still take hours.
W: A=gp3 throughput to 1,000 MiB/s: the volume is capped by its 250 MiB/s throughput setting, and 6,000 IOPS already covers the 4,000 IOPS that 1,000 MiB/s requires.
C=m7i.8xlarge: its 1,250 MB/s EBS bandwidth is sustained (footnote 2), so the instance can carry 1,000 MiB/s for the whole run instead of only for 30 minutes.
X: B=More IOPS: with 256 KiB I/O the volume reaches its 250 MiB/s throughput limit at 1,000 IOPS, so extra IOPS stay unused and the read rate does not change.
D=Keep the m7i.2xlarge: its 1,250 MB/s maximum is a burst available for 30 minutes once a day; after that it falls to the 312.5 MB/s baseline, far below the target.
E=st1 volume: st1 throughput is capped at 500 MiB/s per volume (burst of 250 MiB/s per TiB), so a 4 TiB st1 volume tops out at half the target.
T: Check both ceilings: a volume's throughput (IOPS x I/O size) and the instance's sustained, not burst, EBS bandwidth.
V: Amazon EBS, Amazon EC2, Amazon CloudWatch

@ SAA-C03 D3 T3.1 expert troubleshooting
S: A genomics lab uploads each sequencing run as one object of about 600 GiB to an S3 bucket in its home Region. The upload tool uses multipart upload with a fixed 50 MiB part size and 20 parallel connections over a link that drops briefly a few times an hour, so the team keeps parts below 100 MiB to make every retry cheap. Small runs upload fine, but every 600 GiB upload now fails near the end with an error about the part number, after more than 480 GiB has been sent. The downstream pipeline expects a single object per run. Which change fixes the failures while respecting the team's retry rule?
A: Raise the part size to 64 MiB, so a 600 GiB object needs about 9,600 parts and stays within the multipart limit of 10,000 parts per upload
B: Raise the part size to 5 GiB, the largest part S3 accepts, so a 600 GiB object needs only about 120 parts and the upload finishes sooner
C: Enable S3 Transfer Acceleration on the bucket and keep 50 MiB parts, so each part travels over the AWS edge network and fewer parts time out
D: Raise the tool's parallel connections from 20 to 100 and keep 50 MiB parts, so the upload completes before the part-number counter runs out
K: A
G: ## Multipart upload limits
A multipart upload splits one object into parts that S3 later assembles. The documented limits are:
- at most **10,000 parts** per upload (part numbers 1 to 10,000);
- each part **5 MiB to 5 GiB** (the last part may be smaller);
- maximum object size **48.8 TiB**.
The number of parts is object size divided by part size, so large objects need larger parts.
## Working the numbers
- 600 GiB = 614,400 MiB. At 50 MiB per part: 614,400 / 50 = **12,288 parts**, more than 10,000.
- Part 10,001 starts at 10,000 x 50 MiB = about **488 GiB**, which is exactly where the uploads fail.
- Minimum part size for 600 GiB: 614,400 / 10,000 = 61.44 MiB, so 64 MiB gives 9,600 parts.
## Why the retry rule matters
On an unstable link, a failed part is resent in full. Small parts keep each retry cheap, which is why the team set a 100 MiB ceiling.
W: A=64 MiB parts: 9,600 parts fit under the 10,000-part limit, and each part stays under the team's 100 MiB retry ceiling.
X: B=5 GiB parts: 120 parts would work for S3, but every dropped connection would resend up to 5 GiB, breaking the rule that parts stay below 100 MiB.
C=Transfer Acceleration: it changes the network path, not the part count; 600 GiB in 50 MiB parts still needs 12,288 parts and still fails at part 10,001.
D=100 parallel connections: concurrency changes speed only; the part count is set by object size and part size, so the 10,000-part limit is still exceeded.
T: For multipart uploads, part size must be at least object size divided by 10,000; size parts from the largest object you will upload.
V: Amazon S3

@ SAA-C03 D3 T3.1 expert troubleshooting
S: A media company in eu-central-1 stores objects in one S3 bucket whose default encryption is SSE-KMS with a customer managed key, which compliance requires. Ingest workers upload about 16,000 objects per second spread across 200 prefixes, and readers download about 6,000 objects per second, nearly all written within the last hour. Requests intermittently fail with throttling errors, and Service Quotas shows usage of the KMS Cryptographic operations (symmetric) request rate pinned at its 20,000-per-second quota. The key policy allows the workers' role to call kms:GenerateDataKey and kms:Decrypt only when kms:EncryptionContext:aws:s3:arn matches arn:aws:s3:::media-raw/*. Finance also wants KMS request charges to fall. Which TWO actions should the solutions architect take? (Select TWO)
A: Enable an S3 Bucket Key in the bucket's default encryption settings, so S3 uses a short-lived bucket-level key to generate data keys for new objects
B: Change the key policy condition to match the bucket ARN arn:aws:s3:::media-raw, because objects encrypted under a Bucket Key use the bucket ARN as context
C: Request a Service Quotas increase of the symmetric cryptographic request rate to 30,000 per second and keep requesting one data key per object
D: Spread the uploads across 400 prefixes instead of 200, so that S3 partitions the request load further and stops returning the throttling errors
E: Change the bucket's default encryption to SSE-S3, so that uploads and downloads no longer call AWS KMS at all and the quota stops applying
K: A,B
G: ## How SSE-KMS uses KMS
With SSE-KMS, S3 calls KMS on your behalf: `GenerateDataKey` for every upload and `Decrypt` for every download. These calls count toward the account's **Cryptographic operations (symmetric) request rate**, which is 20,000 per second (shared) in Regions such as eu-central-1.
## Working the numbers
16,000 uploads + 6,000 downloads = **22,000 KMS requests per second**, above the 20,000 quota. The S3 side is fine: 16,000 / 200 prefixes = 80 PUTs per prefix, far below 3,500.
## S3 Bucket Keys
A Bucket Key is a short-lived bucket-level key that S3 gets from KMS and then uses to create data keys for new objects, cutting KMS request traffic (and cost) by up to 99%. Points to know:
- Only objects written **after** enabling it use it; existing objects keep their own keys (here almost all reads are of new objects).
- The encryption context becomes the **bucket ARN** instead of the object ARN, so policies that match `.../media-raw/*` stop matching and must be updated.
W: A=Enable Bucket Key: S3 asks KMS for a bucket-level key only occasionally instead of once per object, which brings KMS traffic far below the quota and cuts request charges.
B=Condition on the bucket ARN: with a Bucket Key the encryption context is `arn:aws:s3:::media-raw`, which does not match the old `media-raw/*` pattern, so without this change the role's KMS calls would be denied.
X: C=Quota increase: it may stop the throttling, but every object still costs a KMS request, so the finance requirement to lower KMS charges is not met.
D=400 prefixes: S3 is not the bottleneck at 80 PUTs per prefix; the throttling comes from the KMS request quota, which prefixes do not change.
E=SSE-S3: it removes KMS calls, but compliance requires the customer managed key, so switching to S3 managed keys breaks a stated constraint.
T: When SSE-KMS traffic hits the KMS request quota, enable S3 Bucket Keys and update any policy that keys on the object ARN encryption context.
V: Amazon S3, AWS KMS, Service Quotas

@ SAA-C03 D3 T3.3 expert troubleshooting
S: An order service writes to a DynamoDB table in provisioned mode with auto scaling turned off. The table has 2,400 WCUs, and at peak the service writes 600 new orders per second, each item about 2.5 KB. A global secondary index on customerId projects ALL attributes and was created with 600 WCUs. During peaks, PutItem calls fail with ProvisionedThroughputExceededException even though the table's consumed write capacity stays near 1,800 WCUs. A support dashboard queries the index and must keep returning every order attribute without a second read against the table. Which change should the solutions architect make?
A: Raise the index's provisioned write capacity to at least 1,800 WCUs, because each 2.5 KB order write also consumes three WCUs on the index
B: Raise the table's provisioned write capacity to 3,600 WCUs, so that peak order writes have twice the capacity they consume on the table today
C: Recreate the index with a KEYS_ONLY projection, so each index entry stays under 1 KB and consumes a single WCU for every order written
D: Place a DAX cluster in front of the table, so that order writes and the dashboard's index queries are absorbed by the write-through cache
K: A
G: ## Writes to a table also write to its indexes
When an item is added, DynamoDB also writes the matching entry to each global secondary index, and that write consumes the **index's own** provisioned WCUs. A table write succeeds only if the table **and all its GSIs** have enough write capacity; if an index runs short, writes to the **base table** are throttled.
## Working the numbers
- One WCU covers a write of up to 1 KB, rounded up: a 2.5 KB item costs **3 WCUs**.
- Table: 600 writes/s x 3 = 1,800 WCUs (it has 2,400, so the table is fine).
- Index with ALL projected: each index entry is also about 2.5 KB, so 600 x 3 = **1,800 WCUs needed**, but only 600 are provisioned.
The evidence fits: table consumption is well below its provisioned capacity, yet PutItem is throttled, so the index is the bottleneck. AWS recommends that a GSI's write capacity be equal to or greater than the table's.
W: A=Index WCUs to 1,800+: the index needs 3 WCUs per order at 600 orders per second, and giving it that capacity removes the back-pressure that throttles table writes.
X: B=Table to 3,600 WCUs: the table already has 600 spare WCUs; the throttling comes from the index, whose 600 WCUs are unchanged by this.
C=KEYS_ONLY projection: one WCU per entry would fit the 600 WCUs, but the dashboard would then need a second read on the table for every order attribute, which the stem rules out.
D=DAX: DAX is a write-through cache that writes every item to DynamoDB synchronously, so the index still receives 1,800 WCUs of writes per second.
T: A GSI with too little write capacity throttles writes to its base table; size GSI WCUs from projected entry size times write rate.
V: Amazon DynamoDB

@ SAA-C03 D3 T3.3 expert troubleshooting
S: A retail catalog serves about 20,000 reads per second through a three-node DAX cluster in front of DynamoDB, and roughly half of those reads are category listings. All writes go through the DAX client, and the cluster uses the default parameter group. After a price update, the product page, which calls GetItem by productId, shows the new price immediately, but the category listing, which runs a Query on a categoryId index, keeps showing the old price for up to five minutes. The business now requires price changes to appear in listings within 30 seconds, and consumed DynamoDB read capacity must not rise by more than about 10%. Which change meets these requirements?
A: Attach a custom parameter group that sets query-ttl-millis to 30,000 and leaves record-ttl-millis at 300,000, because writes never refresh cached query results
B: Attach a custom parameter group that sets record-ttl-millis to 30,000 and leaves query-ttl-millis at 300,000, because listings are assembled from cached items
C: Set ConsistentRead to true on the listing's Query calls, so that DAX returns the latest committed values of every item in the category
D: Switch the price update from PutItem to UpdateItem through the DAX client, so that DAX invalidates every cached query result that contains the item
K: A
G: ## DAX has two independent caches
- **Item cache**: results of `GetItem` and `BatchGetItem`. DAX is **write-through**: a write through the DAX client updates the item cache right away.
- **Query cache**: result sets of `Query` and `Scan`, keyed by their parameters. Updates to the item cache or the table **do not invalidate or modify** cached query results; a result set changes only when its TTL expires.
Both TTLs default to **5 minutes**. They are set in a parameter group (`record-ttl-millis`, `query-ttl-millis`); the default group cannot be changed, so you use a custom one, which can be modified on a running cluster.
## Reading the evidence
The product page is fresh because the write refreshed the item cache. The listing is stale for up to five minutes because it is a Query served from the query cache.
## Strongly consistent reads
DAX passes `ConsistentRead=true` requests to DynamoDB and does **not** cache the results.
W: A=Query TTL 30 s: listings are Query results, so a 30-second query TTL bounds staleness at 30 seconds; each distinct listing is re-read from DynamoDB at most twice a minute, a small rise in consumed capacity.
X: B=Item TTL 30 s: the item cache already shows the new price; the stale data sits in the query cache, whose five-minute TTL is unchanged.
C=ConsistentRead true: DAX would pass every listing query to DynamoDB uncached, about 10,000 extra reads per second, far beyond the 10% limit.
D=UpdateItem through DAX: writes already go through DAX; no write operation invalidates query cache entries, so listings would stay stale until the TTL expires.
T: In DAX, writes refresh only the item cache; control Query/Scan staleness with the query cache TTL.
V: Amazon DynamoDB Accelerator (DAX), Amazon DynamoDB

@ SAA-C03 D3 T3.3 expert troubleshooting
S: A multi-tenant SaaS API runs on Lambda and connects to Aurora PostgreSQL through RDS Proxy. Each invocation opens a connection, runs SET statement_timeout = '5s', which is identical for every tenant, then runs SET search_path TO the caller's tenant schema and two short queries. Under load, the proxy's DatabaseConnectionsCurrentlySessionPinned metric is almost equal to its total client connections, the writer reaches max_connections, and clients see connection timeouts while database CPU stays below 30%. The team wants transaction-level connection reuse back without changing the engine or adding instances. Which TWO changes should the solutions architect make? (Select TWO)
A: Move SET statement_timeout into the proxy's initialization query and remove that statement from the function code
B: Replace SET search_path with schema-qualified table names, such as tenant_schema.orders, in the function's queries
C: Add the EXCLUDE_VARIABLE_SETS session pinning filter to the proxy, so that the SET statements no longer pin sessions
D: Move both SET statements, including search_path for the caller's tenant, into the proxy's initialization query
E: Raise the proxy's MaxConnectionsPercent to 100, so that the proxy can open more database connections for pinned sessions
K: A,B
G: ## Multiplexing and pinning
RDS Proxy normally reuses a database connection at the end of each transaction (**multiplexing**). When a client changes session state that later transactions might rely on, the proxy **pins** that client to one database connection until the client disconnects; no one else can use it.
For PostgreSQL, **setting a variable leads to session pinning**. Watch `DatabaseConnectionsCurrentlySessionPinned` to see how often it happens.
## The documented remedies
- **Initialization query**: if every connection runs the *same* `SET` statements, put them in the proxy's initialization query; the proxy applies them to each new database connection, and you remove them from the code.
- **Avoid the state change**: per-caller settings cannot go in a shared initialization query, so remove the need for them (here, schema-qualified names instead of `search_path`).
- **Session pinning filters** (`EXCLUDE_VARIABLE_SETS`) exist only for the MySQL engine family; the setting is not supported for PostgreSQL.
## Reading the evidence
Nearly all sessions are pinned, connections run out while CPU is low: a pinning problem, not a capacity problem.
W: A=Initialization query for statement_timeout: the value is identical for all tenants, so the proxy can set it on every database connection and the client no longer issues a pinning SET.
B=Schema-qualified names: the tenant schema differs per caller, so the only way to avoid that pinning SET is to stop changing search_path and name the schema in each query.
X: C=EXCLUDE_VARIABLE_SETS: session pinning filters are not supported for PostgreSQL; the option exists only for MySQL-family proxies.
D=Both in the initialization query: the initialization query is one fixed string for all connections, so it cannot set a different search_path for each caller's tenant.
E=MaxConnectionsPercent 100: more database connections for pinned sessions still means one connection per client, so max_connections is still exhausted and reuse is not restored.
T: For RDS Proxy with PostgreSQL, move identical SET statements to the initialization query and remove per-caller session state from the code.
V: Amazon RDS Proxy, Amazon Aurora, AWS Lambda

@ SAA-C03 D3 T3.4 expert use-case
S: Each night a backup appliance copies a 1.8 TB database export between two network-optimized r6in.16xlarge EC2 instances in the same Availability Zone. The instances are not in a placement group and run current ENA drivers. The vendor's copy tool always uses a single TCP connection and cannot be changed. The copy now takes about 48 minutes and must finish within 15 minutes. An earlier test that placed both instances in a cluster placement group cut the copy to about 24 minutes, which is still too slow. Which change meets the requirement?
A: Enable ENA Express on the network interface attachments of both instances, so the single TCP flow can use SRD and reach up to 25 Gbps
B: Enable ENA Express on the sending instance's network interface attachment only, because the side that transmits the data negotiates SRD
C: Put both instances in a cluster placement group and raise the MTU to 9001 bytes, so each packet of the single flow carries more of the export
D: Move both instances to a larger r6in size with twice the network bandwidth, so the higher instance limit raises the rate of each flow
K: A
G: ## Single-flow limits on EC2
A flow is one 5-tuple TCP or UDP connection. Outside a cluster placement group, one flow is limited to **5 Gbps**, no matter how large the instance is. Ways to raise it:
- cluster placement group: up to **10 Gbps** per flow;
- **ENA Express** (built on SRD): up to **25 Gbps** per flow, up to the instance's aggregate limit.
## ENA Express requirements
Both the sending and the receiving instance must be a supported type and must have ENA Express **enabled on their network interface attachments**. SRD detects whether it runs on both sides; if not, traffic falls back to standard ENA transmission.
## Working the numbers
1.8 TB = 14.4 terabits.
- 5 Gbps: 2,880 s = **48 min** (today).
- 10 Gbps: 1,440 s = **24 min** (the placement group test).
- 25 Gbps: 576 s = **about 9.6 min**, inside the 15-minute window.
W: A=ENA Express on both: with both attachments enabled, the single flow uses SRD and can reach 25 Gbps, finishing in under 10 minutes.
X: B=Sender only: SRD is used only when ENA Express runs on both instances; otherwise the flow falls back to standard ENA and stays at 5 Gbps.
C=Placement group with jumbo frames: the test already showed the 10 Gbps per-flow ceiling of a cluster placement group (24 minutes); a larger MTU does not lift that ceiling.
D=Larger instance size: more aggregate bandwidth helps many flows, but a single flow outside a placement group is still limited to 5 Gbps.
T: When one TCP flow is the bottleneck, remember the 5/10/25 Gbps single-flow limits and enable ENA Express on both ends.
V: Amazon EC2, ENA Express

@ SAA-C03 D3 T3.4 expert troubleshooting
S: A market-data service runs behind a Network Load Balancer created with default settings and enabled in two Availability Zones. After a capacity change, the target group holds 2 instances in AZ-a and 8 in AZ-b, and DNS spreads clients evenly across the two load balancer nodes. The 2 instances in AZ-a run at 95% CPU and drop connections, while the AZ-b instances sit near 25%. Each instance can safely handle about 12% of total traffic. The team wants every target to carry an even share, does not want to add EC2 data transfer charges between zones, and must keep both zones serving clients. Which change should the solutions architect make?
A: Rebalance the Auto Scaling group to 5 instances in each Availability Zone and keep cross-zone load balancing turned off on the target group
B: Turn on cross-zone load balancing on the target group, so each of the 10 registered targets receives about 10% of the client traffic
C: Change the target group to least outstanding requests routing, so the two busy targets in AZ-a receive fewer new client connections
D: Disable AZ-a on the Network Load Balancer, so the eight healthier instances in AZ-b receive all of the client traffic from both nodes
K: A
G: ## Cross-zone load balancing on an NLB
Each load balancer node sits in one Availability Zone and receives a share of clients through DNS. With **cross-zone load balancing off** (the default for Network Load Balancers), a node sends traffic only to targets **in its own zone**. With it on, every node spreads traffic across targets in all enabled zones, and for an NLB **EC2 data transfer charges apply** to that cross-zone traffic.
## Working the numbers (cross-zone off)
- Each node gets 50% of the traffic.
- AZ-a: 50% / 2 targets = **25% each**, twice what an instance can handle.
- AZ-b: 50% / 8 targets = **6.25% each**.
## Two ways to even it out
- Turn cross-zone on: 100% / 10 = 10% each, but with inter-zone data transfer charges.
- Keep it off and **balance the targets**: 5 per zone gives 50% / 5 = **10% each**, with no inter-zone traffic.
W: A=5 instances per zone: equal target counts per zone give each target 10% of traffic, below the 12% limit, while traffic stays inside each zone and both zones keep serving.
X: B=Cross-zone on: it does give 10% per target, but for an NLB the cross-zone traffic incurs EC2 data transfer charges, which the team wants to avoid.

Data transfer over a VPC peering connection that **stays within an Availability Zone is free**; traffic that **crosses Availability Zones** is charged at standard in-Region data transfer rates.
## AZ names versus AZ IDs
For accounts created before November 2025, in AWS's oldest Regions, AWS maps the physical Availability Zones to the **names** (`us-east-1a`, `us-east-1b`, ...) **independently for each account**. `us-east-1a` in one account can be a different physical location from `us-east-1a` in another.
The **AZ ID** (for example `use1-az2`) names the same physical zone in every account. To co-locate resources across accounts, compare AZ IDs, shown for each subnet in the VPC console and through the CLI.

G: ## Interface endpoint billing
Interface endpoints are billed **for each hour they are provisioned in each Availability Zone**, plus per GB processed. Per-VPC endpoints multiply quickly:
- today: 30 spokes x 5 services x 3 AZs = **450 endpoint-AZ hours per hour**;
- centralized: 5 services x 3 AZs = **15**, a 30-fold cut, still in three zones.
## Centralized endpoints and DNS
When private DNS is enabled, AWS creates a **managed private hosted zone that works only inside the endpoint's own VPC**, so spokes would not resolve the service names to the central endpoint. The documented pattern:
1. create the endpoints centrally with **private DNS disabled**;
2. create a private hosted zone per service name with an **alias A record** to the endpoint;
3. **associate** those zones with every spoke VPC; traffic flows over the transit gateway.
The trade-off is one shared endpoint policy per service, which security has accepted.
## Gateway endpoints
Only **Amazon S3 and DynamoDB** support gateway endpoints.
W: A=Central endpoints, private DNS off: 15 endpoint-AZ hours replace 450, three zones keep access through an AZ loss, and turning private DNS off lets you build zones that spokes can share.
B=Private hosted zones with alias records: they map each default regional service name to the central endpoint, so workloads keep calling the standard names.
C=Associate zones and delete spoke endpoints: associated spokes resolve the names to the central endpoints over the transit gateway, and deleting the spoke endpoints removes their hourly charges.
X: D=Private DNS on: the managed private hosted zone answers only inside the shared-services VPC, so spoke workloads would not reach the central endpoints by the default names.
E=Gateway endpoints: gateway endpoints exist only for Amazon S3 and DynamoDB; SSM, KMS, Secrets Manager and CloudWatch Logs need interface endpoints.
F=One zone per spoke: 150 hours is still ten times the central design, and a single-zone endpoint fails when that Availability Zone is lost.
T: Centralize interface endpoints in a shared VPC with private DNS off and share Route 53 private hosted zones with the spokes to cut per-AZ hourly charges.
