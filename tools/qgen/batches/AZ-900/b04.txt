# AZ-900 D1 Describe cloud concepts (22 questions: T1.1 cloud computing x14, T1.2 high availability, scalability and SLAs x8)

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied definition
S: A startup's developers create test servers in Azure within minutes when they need them, delete them when the test ends, and the bill shows only the hours each server existed. Which statement best describes the cloud computing model they are using?
A: Computing services such as servers and storage are delivered over the internet, provisioned on demand and paid for as they are used.
B: Computing hardware is bought in advance for the expected peak, installed in the company's own building and written off over several years.
C: Computing capacity is leased as a fixed block of servers for a contract term, with the same monthly fee whether the servers run or not.
D: Computing services are supplied by one hosting partner that installs dedicated hardware for each new project after a purchase order.
K: A
G: ## What cloud computing is
Microsoft defines cloud computing as **the delivery of computing services over the internet**. Those services include common IT infrastructure such as virtual machines, storage, databases and networking, and also newer services such as IoT, machine learning and AI.
## Why it feels different from a datacenter
Because the services come over the internet, they are not limited by physical infrastructure the way a traditional datacenter is:
- **On demand**: you create resources when you need them instead of waiting for hardware to be ordered, delivered and installed. Planning moves from long procurement cycles to on-demand provisioning.
- **Metered**: cloud computing is consumption-based, so you pay for the resources you use and release them when you are done.
## Example
A retail team expecting heavy traffic for a seasonal launch deploys extra compute in the cloud for the launch period and scales down afterward, instead of buying physical servers months ahead.
W: Delivered over the internet, on demand, metered: this matches Microsoft's definition of cloud computing and the consumption-based model, which is exactly what the developers experience when they create, delete and pay only for the hours used.
X: B=Hardware bought for the peak: this is the traditional datacenter model with capital expenditure, the opposite of creating and deleting servers in minutes.
C=Fixed block of leased servers: a fixed fee whether servers run or not is not metered usage; the startup's bill follows the hours each server existed.
D=Hosting partner with purchase orders: waiting for a partner to install dedicated hardware per project is not on-demand self-service and would take far longer than minutes.
T: Cloud computing = computing services delivered over the internet, provisioned on demand and paid for by consumption.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied troubleshooting
S: A company uses a SaaS customer-support app. An employee's personal phone, which the company let sign in to the app without any device checks, was stolen and used to read customer tickets. Under the shared responsibility model, who was responsible for controlling which devices may connect?
A: The SaaS provider, because in SaaS the provider runs the whole application, so it decides which phones and laptops may connect to it.
B: Microsoft as the platform owner, because the device connected over the internet to servers inside a Microsoft-operated datacenter.
C: The company, because the devices allowed to connect to its cloud services are always the customer's responsibility in every model.
D: Responsibility is split evenly, because in SaaS the provider and the customer share every duty except the physical datacenter.
K: C
G: ## Three duties that never move to the provider
Whatever the service type (IaaS, PaaS or SaaS), the customer is **always** responsible for:
- the **information and data** stored in the cloud;
- the **devices** that are allowed to connect to the cloud, such as phones and computers;
- the **accounts and identities** of the people, services and devices in its environment.
The provider is always responsible for the physical datacenter, the physical network and the physical hosts.
## What SaaS changes and what it does not
In SaaS the provider manages almost the whole application stack: infrastructure, platform and application maintenance. Microsoft's description of SaaS says the customer still manages its **data, identity and access settings, and device access posture**.
## Example
A company that requires phones to be enrolled and compliant before they can open the support app would have stopped an unmanaged personal phone from signing in at all.
W: The company: device access is in the group of duties that always stays with the customer, so deciding which phones may connect, and under which conditions, was the company's job even though the app is SaaS.
X: A=The SaaS provider: the provider runs the application, but it does not decide which of the customer's devices are trusted; that is the customer's device access posture.
B=Microsoft as platform owner: running the physical datacenter does not make the provider responsible for the customer's devices that connect from outside.
D=Split evenly: SaaS does not split every duty evenly; data, devices and accounts stay fully with the customer in every model.
T: Data, devices and accounts/identities are always the customer's job, even in SaaS.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied definition
S: A trainer sorts cloud security duties into three groups for new staff: always the customer's, always the cloud provider's, and those whose owner depends on the service type (IaaS, PaaS or SaaS). Which duty belongs in the third group?
A: Protecting the information and data that the company stores in its cloud services and applications.
B: Maintaining the operating systems that run the workloads, including installing their security updates.
C: Running the physical hosts and the physical network inside the datacenters that hold the hardware.
D: Deciding which phones and computers are allowed to connect to the company's cloud services at all.
K: B
G: ## Three groups in the shared responsibility model
Microsoft sorts cloud duties into three groups:
- **Always the customer's**: the information and data stored in the cloud; the devices allowed to connect (phones, computers); the accounts and identities of the people, services and devices in the environment.
- **Always the provider's**: the physical datacenter, the physical network and the physical hosts.
- **Depends on the service type**: operating systems, network controls, applications, and identity and access.
## How the third group shifts
IaaS places the most responsibility on the customer, SaaS the least, and PaaS sits in the middle. The operating system shows the shift clearly:
- On Azure virtual machines (IaaS) the customer installs, configures and patches the OS.
- On a PaaS service or a SaaS application the provider maintains the OS underneath.
Identity and access is another example: in PaaS and SaaS it is shared, because you manage your own users, roles and policies while the provider runs the authentication platform, such as Microsoft Entra ID.
## Example
A company with one app on Azure VMs and another on a SaaS product patches the OS only for the VMs; for the SaaS product it still protects its data, devices and accounts.
W: Operating systems: Microsoft lists operating systems among the duties whose owner depends on the service type; the customer patches them in IaaS, while the provider maintains them in PaaS and SaaS.
X: A=Information and data: the data stored in the cloud is always the customer's responsibility, whatever the service type.
C=Physical hosts and network: the physical datacenter, network and hosts are always the provider's responsibility, even in IaaS.
D=Devices allowed to connect: deciding which devices may connect to the company's cloud always stays with the customer, in every service type.
T: Data, devices and accounts always stay with the customer; the physical layer always sits with the provider; OS, network controls, applications and identity and access depend on the service type.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied comparison
S: A company moves its file servers from its own server room to Azure virtual machines. Which responsibility does the company stop carrying, because it moves to Microsoft as soon as the workload runs on IaaS?
A: Deciding which employees may open which shared folders, and removing access when an employee leaves the company.
B: Applying security updates to the Windows Server operating system that runs the file-sharing role on each server.
C: Classifying the stored documents and deciding how long each type of business record must be kept before deletion.
D: Replacing a failed server, disk or network switch in the physical hardware that the file servers run on.
K: D
G: ## On-premises: you own everything
In your own server room the IT team maintains the physical space, the security, the servers, the network equipment, the operating systems, the applications and the data. If a disk fails, someone from the team replaces it.
## The first step into the cloud: IaaS
With the shared responsibility model, the moment a workload moves to IaaS the **physical infrastructure** becomes the cloud provider's job:
- the physical datacenter (space, power, cooling, physical security);
- the physical network;
- the physical hosts (the servers and their hardware).
Everything above the hardware stays with the customer in IaaS: the operating system, network configuration, applications, data, devices, and accounts and identities.
## Example
When a host fails under an Azure VM, Microsoft handles the hardware; the company never orders a replacement disk again. It still patches Windows Server on its VMs and still manages folder permissions.
W: Physical hardware replacement: Microsoft is always responsible for the physical hosts and physical network, so failed servers, disks and switches stop being the company's problem as soon as it runs on Azure VMs.
X: A=Folder access for employees: accounts, identities and access decisions always remain with the customer.
B=Windows Server updates: in IaaS the customer still installs, configures and patches the guest operating system.
C=Classifying and retaining documents: the information and data stored in the cloud is always the customer's responsibility.
T: Moving to IaaS hands the physical datacenter, network and hosts to Microsoft; the OS and everything above it stay with the customer.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied comparison
S: A board member asks what the company gains by running new workloads in a public cloud such as Azure instead of in a private cloud. Which TWO characteristics belong to the public cloud model? (Select TWO)
A: There are no capital expenditures to scale up, because the provider buys and maintains the hardware.
B: The company has complete control over every hardware resource and over all physical security settings.
C: Applications can be provisioned and deprovisioned quickly, and the company pays only for what it uses.
D: The company's data is never located on hardware that other organizations' resources also use.
E: The company must purchase hardware for startup and is responsible for its maintenance and updates.
K: A,C
G: ## Public cloud
A public cloud is **built, controlled and maintained by a third-party cloud provider**. Anyone who wants to buy cloud services can use it; this general public availability is the key difference from a private cloud. Microsoft lists these characteristics:
- no capital expenditures to scale up;
- applications can be quickly provisioned and deprovisioned;
- you pay only for what you use;
- you do not have complete control over resources and security.
## Private cloud
A private cloud is used by a single entity. Its characteristics:
- complete control over resources and security;
- data is not collocated with other tenants' data;
- hardware must be purchased for startup and maintenance;
- the organization is responsible for hardware maintenance and updates.
## Hybrid cloud
A hybrid cloud combines both in an interconnected environment and gives the most flexibility: you decide where each application runs.
W: A=No CapEx to scale up: the provider owns the hardware, so the company adds capacity as an operating cost instead of buying servers.
C=Quick provisioning, pay for use: resources are created and removed quickly and billed by consumption, a core public cloud trait.
X: B=Complete control over hardware: complete control over resources and security is a private cloud characteristic; in a public cloud the provider controls the physical layer.
D=Data never on shared hardware: not being collocated with other tenants' data is listed as a private cloud characteristic.
E=Buy and maintain hardware: purchasing hardware for startup and maintaining it is the private cloud model's burden, which the public cloud removes.
T: Public cloud = no CapEx to scale, fast provisioning, pay for use, less control; private cloud = full control, dedicated hardware, you buy and maintain it.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: A company that started on another public cloud provider is moving its applications to Azure over the next 18 months. During that time some applications run on the old provider and some on Azure, and the company has no datacenter of its own. Which cloud model describes this environment?
A: Hybrid cloud, because the company's applications are split across two different environments that are connected to each other.
B: Private cloud, because each of the company's applications runs in an environment that is reserved for that one company.
C: Multicloud, because the company uses two public cloud providers and manages resources and security in both of them.
D: Public cloud only, because every application runs with a public provider, so the use of a second provider is not significant.
K: C
G: ## Four cloud models
- **Private cloud**: used by a single entity, on-site or in a dedicated offsite datacenter.
- **Public cloud**: built and run by a third-party provider and available to anyone who buys services.
- **Hybrid cloud**: public and private clouds used together in an interconnected environment.
- **Multicloud**: two or more **public** cloud providers. Microsoft names two typical reasons: using different features from different providers, or being in the middle of a migration from one provider to another.
## The operational cost of multicloud
In a multicloud environment you deal with two or more providers and manage resources and security in each. Azure Arc can help: it is a set of technologies that helps manage your cloud environment whether it is solely on Azure, a private cloud in your datacenter, a hybrid configuration or a multicloud environment.
## Example
An online shop runs its catalog on Azure while its checkout service still runs on another public cloud until the migration finishes: that is multicloud, not hybrid, because no private cloud is involved.
W: Multicloud: the company uses two public cloud providers at the same time during a migration, which is one of the scenarios Microsoft gives for multicloud, and it must manage resources and security in both.
X: A=Hybrid cloud: hybrid means public plus private cloud; the company has no datacenter or private cloud, so being split across two environments is not enough.
B=Private cloud: neither environment is dedicated to one company; both are public clouds that anyone can buy.
D=Public cloud only: using two public providers is specifically what Microsoft calls multicloud, and it changes how resources and security are managed.
T: Two or more public cloud providers (for features or during a migration) = multicloud; public plus private = hybrid.

- migrate workloads from on-premises VMware environments;
- deploy new VMs with the same VMware tools;

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied definition
S: A consultant lists four IT setups used by different clients. Which setup is an example of a hybrid cloud?
A: A bank runs core systems on two public cloud providers, using one for analytics features and the other for its customer-facing apps.
B: A manufacturer keeps its factory control systems in its private cloud and connects them to Azure services for reporting and backup.
C: A hospital rents a datacenter that a hosting company runs only for the hospital and uses it with cloud-style self-service.
D: A retailer runs all of its applications on Azure in two regions, so that each region can serve customers on its own continent.
K: B
G: ## The hybrid cloud
A **hybrid cloud** uses **both public and private clouds in an interconnected environment**. Microsoft gives two typical uses:
- let a private cloud surge into the public cloud for increased, temporary demand;
- add an extra layer of security or control by choosing which services stay in the private cloud and which run in the public cloud.
Hybrid offers the most flexibility, because the organization decides where each application runs.
## How to tell the models apart
Ask two questions: *Is there a private cloud (resources for one organization only)?* and *Is there a public cloud?*
- Private only: private cloud, even when a third party hosts the dedicated datacenter.
- Public only, one provider: public cloud, however many regions are used.
- Public only, two or more providers: multicloud.
- Private and public, connected: hybrid.
## Example
A factory's machine-control servers must stay on-site, but production reports and backups run in Azure and talk to the on-site systems.
W: Private cloud connected to Azure: the manufacturer combines its own private cloud with public cloud services in one interconnected environment, which is the definition of hybrid cloud.
X: A=Two public providers: two public clouds without a private cloud is multicloud, not hybrid.
C=Dedicated hosted datacenter: resources used by a single organization form a private cloud, even when a hosting company operates them.
D=Azure in two regions: running everything with one public provider is public cloud; using several regions does not add a private cloud.
T: Hybrid = private cloud + public cloud, interconnected; two public providers = multicloud.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: Three years ago a company bought 40 servers for its own datacenter, sized for growth it expected. Growth was slower than planned, and today half of the servers sit mostly idle while still costing power and maintenance. Which consumption-based model benefit would have avoided this?
A: Higher performance per server, because cloud hardware is always faster than the servers a company can buy for its datacenter.
B: Guaranteed uptime for every server, because each cloud resource comes with an SLA that covers it from the day it is deployed.
C: Fixed long-term pricing, because the provider sets one price for the planned capacity that does not change for several years.
D: No need to buy capacity that may go unused, because resources are added when demand grows and released when they are not needed.
K: D
G: ## Capacity planning in a traditional datacenter
With your own datacenter you must **estimate future needs in advance**:
- **Overestimate** and you overspend on infrastructure that sits idle.
- **Underestimate** and applications suffer degraded performance; fixing that means ordering, installing and provisioning more hardware, power and cooling.
## The consumption-based model
Cloud computing operates on a consumption-based model: you pay for the IT resources you use, and nothing more. Microsoft lists these benefits:
- no up-front costs for hardware or datacenter infrastructure;
- no need to purchase and manage capacity that may go underutilized;
- the ability to add resources when demand increases;
- the ability to release resources when demand decreases.
## Example
Instead of 40 servers bought on day one, the company would have started with the 20 VMs it needed and added more only as growth arrived.
W: No unused capacity to buy: the consumption-based model lets the company match resources to actual demand, so it never pays up front for servers that end up idle.
X: A=Faster hardware: the cost problem here is idle capacity, not speed; the consumption model is about paying for what you use, not about faster machines.
B=Guaranteed uptime per server: SLAs describe availability commitments; they do nothing about having bought twice as many servers as needed.
C=Fixed long-term pricing: a fixed price for planned capacity would repeat the same mistake of paying for capacity sized in advance.
T: Consumption-based model = pay for what you use, add and release resources with demand, no overbuying capacity up front.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied definition
S: A company's finance team is sorting this year's IT costs into capital expenditure (CapEx) and operational expenditure (OpEx). Which cost is CapEx?
A: The monthly charges for Azure virtual machines, billed for each hour that the virtual machines ran during that month.
B: The purchase of a new storage array that is installed in the company's own datacenter and used for the next five years.
C: The monthly per-user subscription fee for a SaaS email and productivity service used by every employee in the company.
D: The monthly charges for Azure Blob Storage, billed for the amount of data stored and the transactions made in that month.
K: B
G: ## Two kinds of spending
- **Capital expenditure (CapEx)**: up-front spending on **physical infrastructure** such as servers, network hardware and datacenter space. The asset is bought once and used for years.
- **Operational expenditure (OpEx)**: **ongoing spending on services over time**, such as rent, subscriptions or usage-based charges.
## Where the cloud fits
Because you pay for cloud services as you consume them, Microsoft classifies cloud computing as an **operational expense**. This includes IaaS (VM hours), PaaS and storage charges, and SaaS subscriptions.
## Example
- Buying a rack of servers for the company's own server room: CapEx.
- Paying each month for the Azure VMs that replaced them: OpEx.
W: Storage array bought for the datacenter: it is an up-front purchase of physical infrastructure that the company owns and uses for years, which is the definition of CapEx.
X: A=Azure VM hours: VM charges follow consumption month by month, so they are ongoing operational spending.
C=SaaS per-user fee: a recurring subscription for a service is spending over time, which is OpEx.
D=Blob Storage charges: paying for stored data and transactions as they occur is consumption-based OpEx.
T: Buying physical infrastructure up front = CapEx; paying for cloud services as you consume them = OpEx.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 advanced tradeoff
S: A company wants a 3-year commitment discount on its compute. Its VMs change size families often, some workloads will move to another region next year, and part of the spend is on Azure App Service. Which option fits best?
A: Azure Reservations, committing to a specific VM size and region for three years so that the discount applies to that matching usage.
B: Azure Spot Virtual Machines, running every workload on spare Azure capacity at a lower price that changes with demand and region.
C: Pay-as-you-go pricing, paying the standard rate for each resource with no commitment so that changes never waste a discount.
D: An Azure savings plan for compute, committing to a fixed hourly spend that applies across eligible compute services and regions.
K: D
G: ## Two ways to commit for a discount
Azure offers two commitment-based discounts, each for one or three years:
- **Azure Reservations**: commit to a **specific** compute instance type or family in a **specific region**. Best for workloads that run continuously and are highly stable, with no expected change of instance type, family or region. When fully used, reservations give the greatest savings.
- **Savings plan for compute**: commit to a **fixed amount of spend per hour**. The discount applies automatically each hour to eligible compute usage in all regions, such as Azure Virtual Machines, Azure App Service, Azure Container Instances and the Azure Functions Premium plan. Microsoft recommends it when workloads are **dynamic or evolving**, use various instance families or compute services, or run in or shift between regions.
Both are billing discounts only: they do not change how resources run. Usage above the hourly commitment is billed at pay-as-you-go rates, and unused commitment for an hour does not roll over.
## Example
A company commits to a set hourly amount; this month it is spent on D-series VMs in one region, next year on other VM sizes in another region plus App Service, and the discount follows the usage.
W: Savings plan for compute: the company wants a 3-year commitment but its usage changes size families, regions and services, which is exactly the flexible case Microsoft recommends savings plans for.
X: A=Reservations: a reservation is tied to a specific instance type and region, so frequent size changes and a region move would leave it underused.
B=Spot VMs: Spot capacity can be evicted when Azure needs it back and has no SLA, so it is not a commitment discount for production compute.
C=Pay-as-you-go: it gives full flexibility but no commitment discount, which the company explicitly wants.
T: Changing sizes, services or regions + commitment = savings plan; stable specific size and region = reservation.
V: Azure savings plan for compute, Azure Reservations

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: A marketing agency needs four Azure VMs for an eight-week campaign website. The site must not be interrupted while it runs, and the campaign may be cut short or extended at a few days' notice. Which pricing option fits best?
A: Pay-as-you-go pricing, which bills each VM at the standard rate only while it exists, with no term to commit to.
B: A one-year Azure savings plan for compute, which commits to a fixed hourly spend that can't be canceled or refunded.
C: Azure Spot Virtual Machines, which run on spare Azure capacity at a deep discount but can be evicted at any time.
D: A one-year Azure reservation, which lowers the rate for one VM size and region in return for a one-year commitment.
K: A
G: ## Pay-as-you-go
Cloud providers use a **pay-as-you-go** pricing model: you pay for the services you consume and release resources when you are done. There is no up-front payment and no term to commit to, so it suits:
- short or time-limited projects;
- workloads whose size, region or lifetime may change at short notice;
- new workloads whose usage is not yet known.
The trade-off is price: you pay the standard rate, without a commitment discount.
## When the other options fit
- **Reservations** (1 or 3 years): a lower price for a specific VM size and region, best for stable workloads that run continuously.
- **Savings plan for compute** (1 or 3 years): a fixed hourly spend commitment that follows dynamic usage across eligible compute services and regions. Savings plan purchases can't be canceled or refunded.
- **Spot VMs**: deep discounts on unused capacity, but Azure can evict them with about 30 seconds' notice and they have no SLA, so they suit interruptible work such as batch jobs.
## Example
The agency creates four VMs at pay-as-you-go rates for the campaign and deletes them when it ends, after six weeks or ten; the charges stop with the VMs.
W: Pay-as-you-go: an eight-week project that may end early or run longer is where no-commitment pricing fits; the agency pays only while the VMs exist and never risks an unused commitment.
X: B=One-year savings plan: it commits to an hourly spend for a whole year that can't be canceled or refunded, far longer than an eight-week campaign.
C=Spot VMs: Azure can evict Spot VMs whenever it needs the capacity back, which breaks the requirement that the site is not interrupted.
D=One-year reservation: a reservation is a one- or three-year commitment to a specific size and region; most of the term would go unused after the campaign.
T: Short, uncertain or changing workloads = pay-as-you-go; commit (reservation or savings plan) only for long, predictable usage; Spot only for interruptible work.
V: Azure Virtual Machines

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied definition
S: A team lead explains to new developers what serverless computing means in Azure, for example with Azure Functions. Which TWO statements describe serverless correctly? (Select TWO)
A: The team does not provision, patch or manage the servers that run the code; the cloud provider handles them.
B: The team picks a fixed number of always-on instances, and billing stays the same whether the code runs or not.
C: Code runs in response to events such as a file upload, a queue message, an HTTP request or a schedule.
D: The team installs the operating system and runtime on each server, then deploys its code onto those servers.
E: Servers are completely absent, because the code runs directly on the developers' devices instead of in Azure.
K: A,C
G: ## What serverless means
**Serverless** does not mean there are no servers. It means the cloud provider deploys, maintains and scales the servers, so you focus only on your code.
Azure Functions, a serverless solution, shows the typical traits:
- **No server management**: instead of deploying and maintaining servers, you use cloud infrastructure that provides the resources your app needs.
- **Event-driven**: triggers start your code when something happens, such as a file uploaded to blob storage, a message in a queue, an HTTP request, a database change or a timer.
- **Pay for what runs**: serverless hosting plans scale with demand and use pay-as-you-go billing for the compute your code uses. (The Flex Consumption plan is the recommended serverless plan for Azure Functions.)
## Serverless versus other compute
- **IaaS VMs**: you manage the OS and pay while the VM runs.
- **Serverless**: no OS to manage and no charge for idle time on a consumption-style plan.
## Example
A function that sends a welcome email runs a few hundred times a day when new users sign up; between sign-ups nothing runs and nothing is billed for execution.
W: A=No server management: in serverless computing the provider runs, patches and scales the servers so the team writes only code.
C=Event-driven: serverless code is started by triggers such as uploads, queue messages, HTTP requests or schedules.
X: B=Fixed always-on instances: paying the same whether code runs or not describes dedicated hosting; serverless billing follows executions.
D=Install OS and runtime: managing the OS and runtime on each server is IaaS work, the opposite of serverless.
E=No servers at all: servers still exist in Azure datacenters; serverless means you do not manage them, not that the code runs on developers' devices.

G: ## Instance limits in autoscale
Every autoscale profile sets three instance counts: **minimum**, **maximum** and **default**. Autoscale always scales **between** the minimum and the maximum, which are inclusive:
- Under heavy load it scales out, but never beyond the maximum, which caps cost.
- Under light load it scales in, but never below the minimum, which keeps enough instances running to serve users.
If someone manually sets a count outside the range, autoscale moves it back to the minimum or maximum on its next run.

Microsoft recommends always combining a scale-out rule with a scale-in rule: scale out at high usage to keep the app available, and scale in at low usage to save cost. With only one of them, autoscale moves in one direction until it reaches a limit.

Azure Monitor autoscale changes the number of instances. It does not resize instances; that is vertical scaling, which autoscale does not support.

D=Smaller VM sizes: autoscale scales horizontally by changing the number of instances; it does not support vertical scaling, so it never resizes the VMs.

G: ## What an SLA is
A **service-level agreement (SLA)** is a **contractual commitment** between a service provider and a customer about a service's level of service, most often its uptime or availability, with defined consequences when the target is not met. Azure states the availability commitment of each service in its SLA.
## What happens when the target is missed
Microsoft's guidance on how to read an SLA (written for any provider, not only Azure) describes the usual pattern:
- The usual compensation is **service credits**, typically a percentage of the monthly fee for the affected service.
- Credits are generally **not applied automatically**: the customer detects the breach, documents it and files a claim within the deadline in the SLA.
- Credits do not cover lost revenue, lost customers or reputational damage.
## What an SLA is not
An SLA does not promise zero downtime and is not the same as your own reliability target. Uptime is usually measured over a billing period, so a short outage can still fall within the SLA.

B=Zero-downtime guarantee: an SLA is a commitment to a stated uptime level over a period, not a promise that downtime never happens.
C=No consequence: an SLA is a contract with defined consequences; an internal reliability goal is what Microsoft calls a service-level objective (SLO).
T: Missing an SLA target entitles the customer to claim service credits, not to a full refund or compensation for business losses.
