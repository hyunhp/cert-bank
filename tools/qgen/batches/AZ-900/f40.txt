@ AZ-900 D1 T1.2 applied definition
S: An Azure service a company relies on had an outage, and its measured uptime for the month fell below the percentage in the service's SLA. What does the SLA give the company in this situation?
A: No remedy, because an SLA describes Microsoft's internal reliability goals and carries no consequence when the target is missed.
B: A refund of the full monthly charge plus compensation for the revenue the company lost during the outage, added to the next invoice.
C: Eligibility for service credits, usually a percentage of the service's monthly fee, which the company claims within the SLA's deadline.
D: The right to treat the outage as a breach of a zero-downtime promise and to cancel its Azure contract without paying any fees.
K: C
G: ## What an SLA is
A **service-level agreement (SLA)** is a **contractual commitment** between a service provider and a customer about a service's level of service, most often its uptime or availability, with defined consequences when the target is not met. Azure states the availability commitment of each service in its SLA.
## What happens when the target is missed
Microsoft's guidance on how to read an SLA (written for any provider, not only Azure) describes the usual pattern:
- The usual compensation is **service credits**, typically a percentage of the monthly fee for the affected service.
- Credits are generally **not applied automatically**: the customer detects the breach, documents it and files a claim within the deadline in the SLA.
- Credits do not cover lost revenue, lost customers or reputational damage.
## What an SLA is not
An SLA does not promise zero downtime and is not the same as your own reliability target. Uptime is usually measured over a billing period, so a short outage can still fall within the SLA.
## Example
A web app's database service misses its monthly uptime commitment. The company gathers timestamps and error logs, files a claim, and receives a credit on a later bill.
W: Service credits on claim: when a service misses its SLA, the customer can receive service credits, typically a percentage of the monthly fee, by filing a claim with evidence within the deadline.
X: A=No consequence: an SLA is a contract with defined consequences; an internal reliability goal is what Microsoft calls a service-level objective (SLO).
B=Full refund plus lost revenue: credits are a percentage of the service fee, they do not cover lost revenue, and they usually must be claimed.
D=Breach of a zero-downtime promise: an SLA is a commitment to a stated uptime level over a period, not a promise that downtime never happens, and its remedy for a miss is service credits, not contract cancellation.
T: Missing an SLA target entitles the customer to claim service credits, not to a full refund or compensation for business losses.

@ AZ-900 D2 T2.2 applied troubleshooting
S: To save money overnight, an administrator signs in to a Windows virtual machine every evening and shuts it down from the Start menu. At month end, the VM's compute charges are almost the same as if it had run all day. What explains this, and what should the administrator do instead?
A: Windows VMs are billed for compute per month rather than per hour of use; switching the VM to a Linux image would make the charges hourly
B: A shutdown inside the guest OS leaves the VM in the Stopped (allocated) state; deallocating it through Azure ends the compute charges
C: The VM's operating system disk keeps the compute meter running; detaching the OS disk before each shutdown would end the charges
D: Compute billing continues until the VM resource is deleted; the administrator should delete the VM and recreate it from its image daily
K: B
G: ## VM power states and billing
A virtual machine's compute charge depends on whether Azure still holds hardware for it:
- **Running**: billed.
- **Stopped** (also called Stopped (allocated) or PoweredOff): the VM is still **allocated on a host** but not running. This happens when you shut down from **inside the guest OS** or call the PowerOff operation. **Still billed** for compute.
- **Deallocated** (Stopped (deallocated)): the VM has released the underlying hardware. **Not billed** for compute.
Some related resources, such as **disks** and **networking** (for example a public IP), keep incurring charges in every state.
## How to stop paying for compute
Stop and deallocate the VM through Azure's management tools instead of from inside the OS, for example with the Azure CLI command `az vm deallocate` or the Azure PowerShell cmdlet `Stop-AzVM`, both of which stop and deallocate the VM.
Example: a nightly script runs `az vm deallocate` for a dev VM at 19:00, and the VM shows Stopped (deallocated) until it is started the next morning.
W: Stopped (allocated) versus deallocated: a guest-OS shutdown keeps the VM allocated on its host, which is still billed; deallocating it through Azure releases the hardware so compute billing stops (disks are still billed).
X: A=Monthly billing for Windows: VM compute is billed by the power state and size for the time the VM is allocated, not as a monthly fee, and Windows and Linux VMs follow the same rule; a deallocated Windows VM does not incur compute charges.
C=OS disk keeps the meter running: disks have their own storage charge, but they do not cause compute charges, and detaching an OS disk is not a normal way to stop a VM.
D=Billing until deletion: deallocation already stops compute charges, so deleting and recreating the VM each day adds risk and work for no benefit.
T: Only a deallocated VM stops compute billing; a shutdown from inside the OS leaves it allocated and billed.
V: Azure Virtual Machines

S: A new operations team is learning how Azure Monitor stores data. Which TWO statements correctly describe Azure Monitor Metrics and Azure Monitor Logs? (Select TWO)
A: Metrics require an agent on each Azure resource, so new resources show no platform metrics until one is installed
B: Logs accept data from Azure resources but not from on-premises servers, which need a separate monitoring tool
C: Logs are timestamped records with structured or free-form data, kept in a Log Analytics workspace and queried with KQL
D: Metrics are stored by default in a Log Analytics workspace and are read by writing KQL queries in Log Analytics
E: Metrics are numeric values sampled at regular intervals and stored in a time-series database suited to fast alerting
K: C,E
G: ## Two data stores in Azure Monitor
Azure Monitor consolidates monitoring data into two platforms, each built for different work:
- **Metrics**: numeric values sampled at regular intervals, stored in a time-series database. Best for alerting and fast detection of performance problems. Analysed in Metrics explorer and used by metric alerts.
- **Logs**: timestamped event records with structured or free-form data, stored in a Log Analytics workspace. Best for root-cause analysis and deep, contextual queries. Analysed with KQL in Log Analytics.
Platform metrics from Azure resources are collected automatically. Logs can come from Azure, other clouds and on-premises machines, for example through the Azure Monitor Agent.
## Example
"CPU went above 90% at 02:14" is a metric. "Service X stopped with error 7031" is a log record that you find with a KQL query and correlate with the metric spike.
W: C=Logs: event records stored in a Log Analytics workspace and analysed with Kusto Query Language for detailed investigation.
E=Metrics: numeric values with a timestamp, stored in a time-series database, which makes them light and quick to evaluate for alerts.
X: A=Agent needed for metrics: platform metrics are collected from Azure resources with no configuration. Only guest OS data needs an agent.
B=No on-premises logs: Azure Monitor collects logs from Azure, other clouds and on-premises environments into the same workspace.
D=Metrics in a workspace by default: platform metrics live in the metrics time-series database and are analysed in Metrics explorer. You can send them to a workspace, but that is not where they are stored by default.
T: Metrics are numbers in a time-series database for fast alerting; logs are detailed records in a Log Analytics workspace queried with KQL.
V: Azure Monitor Metrics, Azure Monitor Logs, Log Analytics

A: Azure VMs are already Azure Resource Manager resources; Arc-enabled servers is designed for machines outside Azure
B: Azure VMs are enrolled in Azure Arc at creation time, so onboarding them again would create duplicate Arc resources
C: Azure Policy and tags apply to resource groups and subscriptions, not to individual virtual machine resources

Azure Arc **projects non-Azure resources into Azure Resource Manager**, so that servers, Kubernetes clusters and data services running on-premises, at the edge or in another cloud can use Azure governance and management.
## Native Azure resources do not need it
An Azure VM is created through Azure Resource Manager in the first place. It already has a resource ID and can be placed in resource groups, tagged, covered by Azure Policy assignments, controlled with RBAC and monitored with Azure Monitor.

## Rule of thumb
Running in Azure → manage it directly. Running anywhere else → connect it with Azure Arc to get the same management experience.

D=Arc on the subscription: Azure VMs need no Arc at all; Arc is not enabled per subscription as a prerequisite for governing Azure resources.
T: Azure Arc brings resources outside Azure under Azure management; Azure resources already have it natively.

@ AZ-900 D2 T2.1 advanced tradeoff
S: During a design review, an engineer says: "We only deploy our VMs in East US. Because East US is paired with West US, Azure will fail our VMs over to West US automatically if East US has an outage." Which statement about region pairs is accurate?
A: Pairing replicates the VMs deployed in East US to West US and restarts them there once Azure detects a regional outage
B: Nonpaired regions offer no protection against regional failures, so workloads should be placed in paired regions instead
C: Each Azure region has one paired region in the same geography, so choosing a region also brings a ready secondary site
D: Pairing gives benefits such as staggered planned updates and prioritized recovery, but the team must design its own failover
K: D
G: ## What a region pair is
Microsoft associates some regions with another region, usually in the same geography, for example East US and West US. A few services use the pair for geo-replication: geo-redundant storage (GRS) can replicate data to the paired region.
## What pairing gives you
- **Sequential updates**: Azure tries to stagger planned system updates across the two regions of a pair, so a faulty update is less likely to hit both.
- **Recovery order**: in a rare geography-wide outage, one region in every pair is prioritized for recovery.
- **Data residency**: almost all pairs stay inside one geography.
## What pairing does not give you
Microsoft states that deploying to a paired region does not automatically make resources more resilient and gives no automatic high availability, disaster recovery or failover. You still have to deploy to the second region and plan the failover yourself.
Not every region is paired: many newer regions are nonpaired and rely on availability zones, and some pairs are one-directional (Brazil South is paired with South Central US, which is outside its geography).
W: Benefits but no automatic failover: the pair brings staggered updates and a recovery order, but VMs that exist only in East US are not copied or restarted anywhere; the team must deploy and fail over to West US itself.
X: A=Pairing replicates VMs: only specific services, such as geo-redundant storage, replicate to the pair, and only when configured. VMs that exist only in East US are not copied or restarted in West US.
B=Nonpaired regions unprotected: nonpaired regions use availability zones as their main redundancy, and resilient multi-region designs can use any regions, paired or not.
C=Each region has a same-geography pair: many regions are nonpaired and rely on availability zones instead, some pairs cross geographies or are one-directional, such as Brazil South with South Central US, and a pair is never a ready secondary until you deploy to it.
T: Region pairs give staggered updates and recovery priority, not automatic failover; you design high availability and disaster recovery yourself.
V: Azure regions, region pairs

@ AZ-900 D3 T3.3 applied comparison
S: A company moves from scripted, step-by-step deployments to ARM templates. Which TWO benefits of ARM templates should it expect? (Select TWO)
A: Enforcement: after deployment the template reverts manual portal changes made to the deployed resources
B: Declarative syntax: the template states which resources should exist instead of listing commands to run
C: Isolation: a template holds a single resource, which keeps each deployment small and simple to review
D: Scope: a template deploys to the region the user signed in from, rather than to a region set in the file
E: Orchestration: Azure Resource Manager works out dependency order and deploys resources in parallel where it can
K: B,E
G: ## Imperative scripts versus declarative templates
A script is **imperative**: "create this, then that, then configure the other". If a step fails or is run twice, you must handle it yourself.
An **ARM template** (JSON) or **Bicep** file is **declarative**: it states the end result. Azure Resource Manager then:
- validates the template before deploying
- creates resources in the right order based on dependencies, in parallel where possible
- gives repeatable results across environments
- supports modularity (nested templates or Bicep modules) and deployment scripts when extra setup is needed
## Limits to know
A template describes what to deploy; it is not a guard rail afterwards. Preventing later changes is the job of Azure Policy, RBAC or resource locks.
W: B=Declarative syntax: you define what to deploy, not each command, which removes most step-by-step scripting.
E=Orchestration: ARM handles dependency order and parallel creation for you, so you do not script the sequence.
X: A=Reverts manual changes: a deployed template does not watch or undo later edits. Use Azure Policy, RBAC or locks to control changes.
C=One resource per template: a single template can define many resources and whole solutions; splitting into modules is optional, not a limit.
D=Signed-in region: templates choose regions through parameters and resource definitions; the user's sign-in location does not decide where resources go.
T: ARM templates and Bicep are declarative and let Azure Resource Manager handle ordering and parallel deployment for repeatable results.
V: Azure Resource Manager templates, Bicep

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: A retailer runs a private cloud in its own datacenter, and company policy says customer payment records must stay there. Each holiday season its web storefront needs far more servers than the datacenter has for a few weeks. Which cloud model fits best?
A: A private cloud, buying extra servers for the holiday peak and keeping them in the datacenter for the rest of the year.
B: A public cloud, moving the storefront and the payment records to Azure so the datacenter can be closed down.
C: A hybrid cloud, keeping payment records in the private cloud and adding public cloud capacity for the holiday storefront.
D: A multicloud setup, running the storefront on two different public cloud providers at the same time in each season.
K: C
G: ## The cloud models
- **Private cloud**: cloud resources used by a single organization, hosted on-site or in a dedicated offsite datacenter. Greatest control, but the organization buys and maintains the hardware.
- **Public cloud**: built and run by a third-party provider such as Microsoft; anyone can buy services. No capital expenditure to scale up and you pay only for what you use.
- **Hybrid cloud**: public and private clouds used together in an interconnected environment. You decide where each application runs.
- **Multicloud**: using two or more public cloud providers, for example to use features from each or during a migration.
## Classic hybrid use cases
- **Cloud bursting**: let a private cloud surge into the public cloud for temporary, increased demand.
- **Data placement**: keep sensitive or regulated data in the private cloud while other services run in the public cloud.
## Example
The storefront's extra web servers run in Azure from late November to early January and are removed afterwards; the payment database never leaves the company's datacenter.
W: Hybrid cloud: it lets the private cloud surge into public cloud capacity for the temporary holiday demand while the payment records stay in the private cloud, as the policy requires.
X: A=Private cloud with extra servers: buying servers for a few peak weeks means paying up front for hardware that sits idle most of the year, which the public cloud part of a hybrid avoids.
B=Public cloud: moving the payment records to Azure breaks the policy that they must stay in the company's own datacenter.
D=Multicloud: using two public providers does not address where the payment records live, and the need here is temporary extra capacity next to an existing private cloud.
T: Private cloud plus temporary public capacity (cloud bursting) or data kept on-premises by policy = hybrid cloud.

S: A company uses a SaaS customer-support app. An employee's personal phone, which the company let sign in to the app without any device checks, was stolen and used to read customer tickets. Under the shared responsibility model, who was responsible for controlling which devices may connect?
A: Microsoft as the platform owner, because the device connected over the internet to servers inside a Microsoft-operated datacenter.
B: The SaaS provider, because in SaaS the provider runs the whole application, so it decides which phones and laptops may connect to it.
C: Responsibility is split evenly, because in SaaS the provider and the customer share every duty except the physical datacenter.
D: The company, because the phones and laptops used to reach a cloud service remain the customer's duty in IaaS, PaaS and SaaS.
K: D
G: ## Three duties that never move to the provider
Whatever the service type (IaaS, PaaS or SaaS), the customer is **always** responsible for:
- the **information and data** stored in the cloud;
- the **devices** that are allowed to connect to the cloud, such as phones and computers;
- the **accounts and identities** of the people, services and devices in its environment.
The provider is always responsible for the physical datacenter, the physical network and the physical hosts.
## What SaaS changes and what it does not
In SaaS the provider manages almost the whole application stack: infrastructure, platform and application maintenance. Microsoft's description of SaaS says the customer still manages its **data, identity and access settings, and device access posture**.
## Example
A company that requires phones to be enrolled and compliant before they can open the support app would have stopped an unmanaged personal phone from signing in at all.
W: The company: device access is in the group of duties that stays with the customer in every service type, so deciding which phones may connect, and under which conditions, was the company's job even though the app is SaaS.
X: A=Microsoft as platform owner: running the physical datacenter does not make the provider responsible for the customer's devices that connect from outside.
B=The SaaS provider: the provider runs the application, but it does not decide which of the customer's devices are trusted; that is the customer's device access posture.
C=Split evenly: SaaS does not split every duty evenly; data, devices and accounts stay with the customer in every model.
T: Data, devices and accounts/identities are the customer's job in every service type, SaaS included.

@ AZ-900 D2 T2.2 applied use-case
S: A small company runs an internal web portal on one Azure VM. The portal is idle most of the day, with CPU usage usually under 10%, but it needs more CPU for short bursts when employees sign in each morning. Which type of VM size fits this pattern at a low cost?
A: A storage-optimized size, which provides high disk throughput and I/O for big data and large databases
B: A memory-optimized size, which provides a high memory-to-CPU ratio for large in-memory databases and caches
C: A B-series size, which banks credits during quiet periods and draws on them when demand rises above baseline
D: A GPU-accelerated size, which adds graphics processors for rendering, visualization and model training work
K: C
G: ## VM sizes
The **size** of a VM sets its processing power, memory, storage capacity and network bandwidth, and it largely decides the price. Azure groups sizes into families:
- **General purpose**: balanced CPU-to-memory ratio, good for dev/test, small to medium databases and low to medium traffic web servers. This includes the **B-series**.
- **Compute optimized**: high CPU-to-memory ratio.
- **Memory optimized**: high memory-to-CPU ratio, for in-memory analytics and large databases.
- **Storage optimized**: high disk throughput and I/O.
- **GPU accelerated** (and FPGA accelerated): specialized hardware for graphics, visualization and AI.
## Burstable B-series
B-series VMs use a **CPU credit** model: while the workload runs below the base CPU performance, the VM accumulates credits; when it needs more, it spends them to burst above the base. When credits run out it is throttled back to the base level. That suits workloads that are usually quiet but occasionally busy.
Example: a portal that idles all day and spikes at 9:00 runs on a small B-series size instead of a larger fixed-performance size.
W: B-series burstable: credits earned while the portal runs below its baseline pay for the morning bursts above it, so a small, inexpensive size can handle occasional peaks.
X: A=Storage optimized: these sizes are built for heavy disk I/O, which does not match a mostly idle portal with brief CPU peaks.
B=Memory optimized: these sizes target memory-hungry workloads; the portal's problem is short CPU bursts, not memory.
D=GPU accelerated: graphics processors are for rendering and AI work and cost much more; a web portal does not need them.
T: Choose a VM size family by workload shape; B-series burstable sizes suit low average CPU with occasional bursts.

S: A company on an Enterprise Agreement has divided its enrollment into departments and set a limit on how much each may spend. The head of HR wants an email when HR's costs pass a set percentage of that limit. Which alert type in Cost Management covers this?
A: A Service Health alert, which emails administrators when an Azure service issue affects a region HR uses
B: A department spending quota alert, which emails department owners at fixed thresholds such as 50% or 75%
C: A credit alert, which emails account owners as the organisation's Azure Prepayment balance is consumed
D: An activity log alert, which emails administrators when someone creates or deletes resources in HR's subscriptions

G: ## Cost alerts in Microsoft Cost Management
The **cost alerts** view in Cost Management is a single place to see three types of alert:
- **Budget alerts**: when spending reaches a threshold on a budget you created.
- **Credit alerts**: when an Enterprise Agreement's **Azure Prepayment** is being used up; generated automatically.
- **Department spending quota alerts**: specific to Enterprise Agreement customers. A department can have a spending quota; when spending reaches a set threshold of that quota (for example 50% or 75%), an email goes to the department owners and the alert appears in cost alerts.
## Example
Enrollment `Contoso EA` has departments Sales, HR and IT. HR has a quota with a 75% threshold. When HR's spending crosses it, HR's department owner receives an email.
W: Department spending quota alert: built for EA departments, it fires when a department's spending reaches a fixed threshold of its quota (the limit set for the department) and emails the department owners, exactly what the head of HR asked for.
X: A=Service Health alert: Service Health alerts report outages and planned maintenance in Azure services. They have nothing to do with how much a department spends.
C=Credit alert: credit alerts track the organisation's Azure Prepayment balance as a whole and go to account owners, not one department's share of spending.
D=Activity log alert: activity log alerts in Azure Monitor react to control-plane events such as creating or deleting resources. They do not measure spending against a quota.
T: EA departments with spending quotas get department spending quota alerts; Prepayment use gets credit alerts; budgets get budget alerts.
