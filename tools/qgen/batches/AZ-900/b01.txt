@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: A company runs SQL Server on an Azure virtual machine that its own team set up. It plans to move the database to Azure SQL Database, a managed database service. Under the shared responsibility model, which task moves from the company to Microsoft?
A: Deciding which employees and applications are allowed to sign in to the database and read its tables.
B: Making sure the customer records loaded into the database are accurate, classified and kept only as long as needed.
C: Installing patches and updates for the database engine and the operating system that the database runs on.
D: Securing the physical servers, power and cooling in the datacenter that hosts the database's hardware.
K: C
G: ## The shared responsibility model
In an on-premises datacenter the company is responsible for everything: the building, the hardware, the operating system, the software and the data. In the cloud, these duties are **shared** between the cloud provider and the customer, and the split depends on the service type.
- **Always the provider**: the physical datacenter, physical network and physical hosts.
- **Always the customer**: the information and data stored in the cloud, the devices allowed to connect, and the accounts and identities of people, services and devices.
- **Depends on the service type**: operating systems, network controls, applications, and identity and access.
## Database on a VM versus a managed database
- **SQL Server on an Azure VM** is IaaS. Microsoft runs the hardware; the customer patches the operating system and the database software and looks after the data.
- **Azure SQL Database** is PaaS. Microsoft maintains the database engine and the platform under it, including updates; the customer is still responsible for the data that goes into the database and for who can access it.
## Example
A team that spent every month patching its SQL Server VM stops doing that after moving to Azure SQL Database, but it still decides who gets database access and still owns the quality of its customer records.
W: Engine and OS patching: with a managed (PaaS) database, Microsoft maintains the database engine and the platform it runs on, so patching that the company did on its own VM becomes Microsoft's job.
X: A=Who can sign in: accounts, identities and access decisions stay with the customer in every service type. Microsoft runs the sign-in platform, but the company still decides who gets access.
B=Data quality and retention: the information stored in the cloud is always the customer's responsibility, whether it lives on a VM or in a managed database.
D=Physical datacenter security: physical security, power and cooling were already Microsoft's responsibility while the database ran on an Azure VM, so nothing moves here.
T: Moving from a database on a VM (IaaS) to a managed database (PaaS) hands engine and OS maintenance to Microsoft; data and access always stay with the customer.
V: Azure SQL Database, Azure Virtual Machines

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied definition
S: A manager asks which security duties stay with her company no matter whether it uses IaaS, PaaS or SaaS services in Azure. Which TWO responsibilities always remain with the customer? (Select TWO)
A: The information and data that the company stores in its cloud services and applications.
B: The physical hosts and the physical network inside the Azure datacenters that run the services.
C: The operating systems of the servers that run each service, including their security updates.
D: The accounts and identities of the people, services and devices in the company's environment.
E: The power supply, cooling and building access controls of the datacenters that hold the hardware.
K: A,D
G: ## Three groups of responsibility
The shared responsibility model sorts every duty into one of three groups:
- **Always the cloud provider**: the physical datacenter, the physical network and the physical hosts. The customer is not in the building, so these duties could not sensibly be theirs.
- **Always the customer**: the information and data stored in the cloud, the devices allowed to connect (phones, laptops), and the accounts and identities of people, services and devices.
- **Varies by service type**: operating systems, network controls, applications, and identity and access settings.
## How the middle group moves
- In **IaaS** (for example Azure Virtual Machines) the customer manages the operating system and its patches.
- In **PaaS** (for example Azure App Service) Microsoft manages the operating system and runtime.
- In **SaaS** (for example Microsoft 365) Microsoft runs almost the whole application stack.
No matter how far the line moves, a provider never becomes responsible for what the customer's data is or for who the customer lets in.
W: A=Stored information and data: the data you put in the cloud is always yours to classify, protect and govern, in every service type.
D=Accounts and identities: you always manage the accounts and identities in your environment, even when Microsoft runs the sign-in platform such as Microsoft Entra ID.
X: B=Physical hosts and network: these are always Microsoft's responsibility, because customers have no access to the hardware inside Azure datacenters.
C=Server operating systems: OS patching depends on the service type. The customer patches it in IaaS, while Microsoft manages it in PaaS and SaaS.
E=Power, cooling and building access: physical datacenter operations are always the cloud provider's job, whatever service type the customer uses.
T: Data, devices, and accounts and identities always stay with the customer; the physical datacenter, network and hosts always stay with Microsoft.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: A retailer runs a private cloud in its own datacenter, and company policy says customer payment records must stay there. Each holiday season its web storefront needs far more servers than the datacenter has for a few weeks. Which cloud model fits best?
A: A public cloud only, moving the storefront and the payment records to Azure so the datacenter can be closed down.
B: A private cloud only, buying enough additional servers for the holiday peak and keeping them in the datacenter all year.
C: A multicloud setup, running the storefront on two different public cloud providers at the same time for every season.
D: A hybrid cloud, keeping payment records in the private cloud and adding public cloud capacity for the holiday storefront.
K: D
G: ## The cloud models
- **Private cloud**: cloud resources used by a single organization, hosted on-site or in a dedicated offsite datacenter. Greatest control, but the organization buys and maintains the hardware.
- **Public cloud**: built and run by a third-party provider such as Microsoft; anyone can buy services. No capital expenditure to scale up and you pay only for what you use.
- **Hybrid cloud**: public and private clouds used together in an interconnected environment. You decide where each application runs.
- **Multicloud**: using two or more public cloud providers, for example to use features from each or during a migration.
## Classic hybrid use cases
- **Cloud bursting**: let a private cloud surge into the public cloud for temporary, increased demand.
- **Data placement**: keep sensitive or regulated data in the private cloud while other services run in the public cloud.
## Example
The storefront's extra web servers run in Azure from late November to early January and are removed afterwards; the payment database never leaves the company's datacenter.
W: Hybrid cloud: it lets the private cloud surge into public cloud capacity for the temporary holiday demand while the payment records stay in the private cloud, as the policy requires.
X: A=Public cloud only: moving the payment records to Azure breaks the policy that they must stay in the company's own datacenter.
B=Private cloud only: buying servers for a few peak weeks means paying up front for hardware that sits idle most of the year, which the public cloud part of a hybrid avoids.
C=Multicloud: using two public providers does not address where the payment records live, and the need here is temporary extra capacity next to an existing private cloud.
T: Private cloud plus temporary public capacity (cloud bursting) or data kept on-premises by policy = hybrid cloud.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied comparison
S: A hospital group wants cloud-style self-service for its IT resources, but no other organization may share the hardware. It plans to rent a dedicated datacenter that a hosting company operates only for the group. Which cloud model is this?
A: Public cloud, because the datacenter is built and maintained by a third-party company rather than by the group's own staff.
B: Private cloud, because the cloud resources are used by a single organization even though a third party hosts them.
C: Hybrid cloud, because the hardware belongs to one company while the resources are used by a different organization.
D: Multicloud, because the resources involve two separate companies: the hospital group and the hosting provider.
K: B
G: ## What makes a cloud private or public
The deciding question is **who can use the resources**, not who owns the building.
- A **private cloud** is used by a single entity. It may be hosted in the organization's own datacenter or in a dedicated datacenter offsite, even one run by a third party. It gives the most control, but also costs more and offers fewer of the public cloud's benefits.
- A **public cloud** is built, controlled and maintained by a third-party provider, and **anyone** who wants to buy services can use it. General public availability is the key difference.
- A **hybrid cloud** connects a private and a public cloud.
- **Multicloud** means using two or more **public cloud providers**.
## Example
A dedicated hosted environment used only by one hospital group is a private cloud. If the group later connects it to Azure for extra capacity, the combination becomes a hybrid cloud.
W: Private cloud: resources dedicated to one organization make a private cloud, and Microsoft's definition explicitly allows it to be hosted offsite by a third party.
X: A=Public cloud: a public cloud is open to anyone who wants to buy services; a third party running a datacenter for one customer does not make it public.
C=Hybrid cloud: hybrid means a private cloud and a public cloud working together, not a split between who owns and who uses the hardware.
D=Multicloud: multicloud means using two or more public cloud providers. A hosting company running a dedicated datacenter is not a public cloud provider in this setup.
T: Single organization using the resources = private cloud, wherever it is hosted; open to anyone = public cloud.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: A finance director asks how moving the company's servers from its own datacenter to Azure will change the way IT spending is recorded and planned. Which statement describes the consumption-based model correctly?
A: Spending becomes capital expenditure, because the company buys an Azure subscription that is depreciated over several years.
B: Spending becomes a fixed annual fee that Microsoft sets in advance, whatever the company actually runs during that year.
C: Spending becomes operational expenditure, paid for the resources used, with no up-front purchase of server hardware.
D: Spending stays capital expenditure, because the company must still reserve its peak capacity before resources run.
K: C
G: ## CapEx and OpEx
- **Capital expenditure (CapEx)**: up-front spending on physical infrastructure such as servers, network hardware and datacenter space.
- **Operational expenditure (OpEx)**: ongoing spending on services over time.
## The consumption-based model
Cloud computing is consumption-based: you rent compute and storage, pay for what you use, and release resources when you are done. Because you pay as you consume, cloud computing is classified as an **operational expense**.
Benefits listed by Microsoft:
- no up-front costs for hardware or datacenter infrastructure;
- no need to buy capacity that may sit underused;
- add resources when demand rises and release them when it falls.
## Example
Instead of buying ten servers sized for the busiest week of the year, the company runs four VMs most of the year, adds six for the busy week, and pays only for the hours each VM actually ran.
W: Operational expenditure: cloud services are paid for as they are consumed, so spending shifts from up-front hardware purchases (CapEx) to ongoing operational costs (OpEx).
X: A=Capital expenditure for a subscription: an Azure subscription is not a depreciating asset that is bought once; charges follow the resources actually used.
B=Fixed annual fee: under pay-as-you-go pricing the bill follows actual consumption, so it changes with what the company runs.
D=Reserving peak capacity up front: the consumption model lets you add resources when demand increases instead of buying or reserving peak capacity in advance.
T: Cloud = consumption-based, pay for what you use, classified as OpEx rather than CapEx.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 advanced comparison
S: A company runs a line-of-business application on one VM size in one Azure region, 24 hours a day. It expects the same size and region for the next three years and the VM must not be interrupted. Which pricing option gives the deepest discount for this usage?
A: Azure Spot Virtual Machines, which run on unused Azure capacity at a lower price and can be evicted whenever Azure needs it back.
B: Azure Reservations, which commit to a specific resource, size and region for one or three years in return for a lower price.
C: Pay-as-you-go pricing, which bills each hour the VM runs at the standard rate with no commitment and no up-front payment.
D: An Azure savings plan, which commits to a fixed hourly spend that applies flexibly across eligible services and regions.
K: B
G: ## Azure pricing models
- **Pay-as-you-go**: no commitment; you pay the standard rate for what you use. Best for short-lived or unpredictable workloads.
- **Azure Reservations**: commit to a **specific** resource, size and region for one or three years. The discount applies automatically to matching usage. Microsoft describes reservations as giving deeper discounts that work best when usage is **stable and predictable**.
- **Savings plan**: commit to a fixed **amount per hour** for one or three years. The discount applies automatically across eligible services and regions, which suits changing or dynamic workloads.
- **Azure Spot Virtual Machines**: use Azure's unused capacity at a large discount, but Azure can evict the VM when it needs the capacity back, and there is no SLA. Good for interruptible work such as batch jobs or dev/test.
Reservations and savings plans are billing discounts only: they do not change how the resource runs.
## Example
A database server that has run on the same VM size in the same region for years is the textbook case for a reservation.
W: Azure Reservations: the workload is stable, always on and locked to one size and region, which is exactly the case where a reservation's commitment gives the deepest discount.
X: A=Spot Virtual Machines: Spot VMs can be evicted at short notice when Azure needs the capacity, which the application cannot accept.
C=Pay-as-you-go: it gives full flexibility but no discount, so a predictable three-year workload would pay more than it needs to.
D=Savings plan: it also discounts committed usage, but its strength is flexibility across services and regions; for fixed size and region usage, a reservation gives the deeper discount.
T: Stable, specific usage for 1 or 3 years = reservation; changing usage with a spend commitment = savings plan; interruptible = Spot; no commitment = pay-as-you-go.
V: Azure Reservations, Azure Virtual Machines

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: A small insurer needs to resize each photo that customers upload with a claim. Uploads arrive at random, from none for hours to hundreds in a few minutes. The team wants to write only the resize code and not pay for idle servers. Which approach fits best?
A: Azure Virtual Machines in a scale set that always keeps at least two instances running and adds more when CPU usage is high.
B: Azure Functions, which runs the resize code when an upload event arrives and charges only for the compute time it uses.
C: An Azure App Service plan that hosts the resize code as a web app with a fixed number of instances running around the clock.
D: Azure Virtual Desktop, which gives each claims handler a cloud desktop where the photos can be resized by hand as needed.
K: B
G: ## What serverless means
**Serverless** does not mean there are no servers. It means the cloud provider runs and scales them, and you neither manage nor pay for idle capacity.
In Azure, the main serverless compute option is **Azure Functions**:
- It is **event-driven**: an event (an HTTP request, a timer, a new file, a message) wakes the function and runs your code.
- It **scales automatically** with demand, including down to nothing when there are no events.
- When the code finishes, resources are released, and in the serverless hosting plans you are charged for the time your code actually runs.
## When serverless fits
Short pieces of work triggered by events with **variable** demand: resizing an uploaded image, processing a queue message, running a nightly cleanup.
## Contrast
VMs and containers must keep running for the app to answer, so you pay for them even while they wait.
W: Azure Functions: it is event-driven and serverless, so each upload triggers the resize code, scaling follows the bursts automatically, and nothing is billed while no uploads arrive.
X: A=VM scale set with two instances: scale sets add VMs under load, but the minimum instances run and are billed all the time, and the team must manage the operating systems.
C=App Service plan with fixed instances: App Service removes OS management, but a fixed set of always-on instances is paid for during the idle hours as well.
D=Azure Virtual Desktop: it delivers cloud-hosted desktops to people. It does not run code automatically in response to uploads.
T: Event-driven, short tasks with unpredictable demand and no wish to pay for idle servers = serverless, for example Azure Functions.
V: Azure Functions

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.2 applied definition
S: An online ticketing site must stay usable even when a server or other component fails during a major concert sale. Which cloud benefit does this requirement describe, and how does Azure state what customers can expect from each service?
A: Scalability, because Azure adds resources during the sale, and each service's expected uptime is printed in its Azure Pricing Calculator estimate.
B: High availability, because the site must remain available despite disruptions, and Azure states uptime guarantees per service in SLAs.
C: Manageability, because the site is managed through the portal and APIs, and expected uptime is shown in each subscription's usage reports.
D: Governance, because policies keep the site compliant during the sale, and Azure states expected uptime in each region's compliance documents.
K: B
G: ## High availability
**High availability** focuses on keeping resources available when they are needed, regardless of disruptions or events such as a failed server.
When you design a solution you must account for **service availability guarantees**. Azure gives uptime guarantees that depend on the service, and these guarantees are part of each service's **service-level agreement (SLA)**.
## How it differs from related benefits
- **Scalability**: adjusting resources to meet demand (more traffic, more resources).
- **Reliability**: the ability of a system to recover from failures and continue to function.
- **Manageability**: how you manage resources (automation, templates, monitoring) and through which tools (portal, CLI, APIs, PowerShell).
- **Governance**: keeping resources compliant with standards and rules.
## Example
A ticketing site runs several instances behind a load balancer, so when one instance fails, buyers keep reaching the others. Before choosing services, the team checks each one's SLA.
W: High availability and SLAs: staying available through failures is the definition of high availability, and Azure publishes the uptime it guarantees for each service in that service's SLA.
X: A=Scalability and the Pricing Calculator: scalability handles more load, not component failures, and the Pricing Calculator estimates costs, not uptime commitments.
C=Manageability and usage reports: managing through the portal or APIs is about operations, and usage reports show consumption and cost rather than guaranteed uptime.
D=Governance and compliance documents: governance keeps resources within rules and standards; it does not keep a site running when a component fails.
T: Remain available despite disruptions = high availability; Azure's uptime guarantees per service are in its SLAs.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.2 applied comparison
S: A school's enrollment portal runs on two Azure virtual machines. On enrollment day traffic jumps tenfold, and the team adds eight more identical VMs, then removes them the next week. What type of scaling is this, and what is the main cost effect?
A: Vertical scaling, because adding identical VMs raises the CPU and memory of the existing two, and costs stay fixed afterwards.
B: Horizontal scaling, because it adds and removes resource instances, and the school pays for the extra VMs only while they run.
C: Vertical scaling, because the portal's capacity goes up, and the school pays a one-time charge for the extra peak capacity used.
D: Horizontal scaling, because it changes the size of each existing VM, and costs stay higher until the reserved capacity expires.
K: B
G: ## Scalability
**Scalability** is the ability to adjust resources to meet demand. Because the cloud is consumption-based, scaling down when demand drops also reduces cost.
## Two kinds of scaling
- **Vertical scaling (scale up / scale down)**: change the capabilities of a resource, for example give a VM more CPUs or RAM, or lower them if it was over-specified.
- **Horizontal scaling (scale out / scale in)**: change the **number** of resources, for example add VMs or containers during a spike and remove them afterwards. This can be manual or automatic.
## Example
- A reporting VM that runs out of memory moves to a larger VM size: vertical.
- A web front end goes from 2 to 10 identical VMs for a sale and back to 2: horizontal.
W: Horizontal scaling: adding eight identical VMs and removing them later changes the number of resources (scale out, then scale in), and with consumption-based billing the extra VMs cost money only while they exist.
X: A=Vertical scaling of the existing two: vertical scaling would resize the existing VMs; adding separate VMs does not change the size of the original two.
C=Vertical with a one-time charge: the capacity came from more instances, not bigger ones, and Azure bills VMs for the time they run rather than a one-time peak fee.
D=Horizontal by resizing VMs: changing the size of each VM is vertical scaling, and nothing in the scenario involves a reservation.
T: More or fewer instances = horizontal (out/in); bigger or smaller instances = vertical (up/down); scaling in also cuts cost.

- **High availability**: keeping resources available despite disruptions, backed by per-service SLAs.
- **Predictability**: forecasting performance and cost.
- **Manageability**: managing resources and the tools used to do it.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.3 applied use-case
S: A manufacturer must move an old inventory application to Azure within a month. The application needs a specific operating system version and custom software installed on the server, and there is no time to redesign it. Which service type and Azure service fit best?
A: SaaS, buying a ready-made inventory application that the vendor runs and updates for all of its many customers.
B: PaaS, with Azure Functions running the application's code each time an inventory event arrives from the factory.
C: IaaS, with Azure Virtual Machines where the company installs and controls the operating system and the software.
D: PaaS, with Azure App Service hosting the application on a platform whose operating system Microsoft manages.

G: ## Infrastructure as a service (IaaS)
IaaS is the most flexible cloud service type and gives the most control. The cloud provider maintains the hardware, network connectivity to the internet and physical security. You are responsible for the rest, including:
- installing, configuring and maintaining the **operating system**;
- network configuration;
- database and storage configuration.
In effect you rent hardware in a cloud datacenter and decide what to do with it. In Azure, **Azure Virtual Machines** is the main IaaS service.
## Typical IaaS scenarios
- **Lift-and-shift migration**: set up cloud resources similar to the on-premises datacenter and move the workloads over with little or no redesign.
- **Testing and development**: quickly replicate configurations and start or stop environments while keeping full control.
## Example
A legacy application that needs a particular OS version and a custom driver moves unchanged onto an Azure VM built from the same OS image.
W: IaaS with Azure Virtual Machines: a VM gives full control of the operating system and installed software, which is what a quick lift-and-shift of an application with specific OS needs requires.
X: A=Ready-made SaaS application: replacing the application with a vendor's product is a different project and not a move of the existing application within a month.
B=Azure Functions: Functions runs short, event-driven pieces of code. The existing application would have to be redesigned, and it cannot control the OS or install custom software.
D=Azure App Service: App Service hosts web apps on a platform that Microsoft manages, so the company cannot pick the OS version or install custom server software.
T: Lift-and-shift, specific OS or custom software, full control = IaaS (Azure Virtual Machines).
V: Azure Virtual Machines

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.3 applied use-case
S: A three-person development team builds a web API in Python. They want to deploy code straight from their Git repository and get automatic scaling and high availability, without patching or licensing any operating system. Which option fits best?
A: Azure Virtual Machines, where the team installs Python and a web server and then applies OS updates on its own schedule.
B: Microsoft 365, where the team publishes the web API inside the company's existing productivity apps for all of its users.
C: Azure Virtual Desktop, where each developer runs the API on a cloud desktop that can be reached from anywhere they work.
D: Azure App Service, a PaaS offering that hosts web apps and APIs while Microsoft maintains the platform beneath the code.

G: ## Platform as a service (PaaS)
PaaS sits between renting infrastructure (IaaS) and buying a finished application (SaaS). The cloud provider maintains:
- the physical infrastructure, physical security and internet connection;
- the **operating systems**, middleware, development tools and runtimes.
You don't have to worry about licensing or patching operating systems and databases. You focus on your **application code, data and access controls**.
## Typical PaaS scenarios
- **Development framework**: developers build or customize cloud applications using built-in components; scalability, high availability and multitenancy come with the platform, so less code is needed.
- **Analytics or business intelligence**: tools offered as a service to analyze data and find patterns.
## Azure App Service
App Service hosts web apps, REST APIs and mobile back ends in languages such as .NET, Java, Node.js and Python, with automatic scaling, high availability and continuous deployment from GitHub, Azure DevOps or any Git repository.
W: Azure App Service: as PaaS it runs the team's Python API with automatic scaling, high availability and Git-based deployment, while Microsoft manages and patches the operating system underneath.
X: A=Azure Virtual Machines: VMs are IaaS, so the team would have to install, patch and maintain the operating system, which it wants to avoid.
B=Microsoft 365: Microsoft 365 is a SaaS productivity suite for using finished applications, not a platform for hosting custom code.
C=Azure Virtual Desktop: it provides cloud-hosted desktops for users; running an API on someone's desktop gives no automatic scaling or high availability.
T: Deploy your own code without managing the OS, with scaling and high availability built in = PaaS (for example Azure App Service).
V: Azure App Service

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.3 applied use-case
S: A ten-person law firm has no IT staff. It needs email, calendars and document editing for every employee, and wants the provider to handle the servers, the application updates and their availability. Which service type fits best?
A: IaaS, running mail and document servers on Azure virtual machines that the firm configures and patches on its own.

G: ## Software as a service (SaaS)
SaaS is the most complete cloud service model from a product perspective: you rent or use a **fully developed application**. Email, financial software, messaging applications and connectivity software are common examples.
- It is the **least flexible** model, because you use the application as the provider built it.
- It is the **easiest to get running** and needs the least technical knowledge.
- The provider manages almost the whole stack: infrastructure, platform and application maintenance, including patching, updates and availability.
- You still manage your **data**, **identity and access settings** and which devices may connect.
## Typical SaaS scenarios
Email and messaging, productivity applications, and finance and expense tracking. **Microsoft 365** is a well-known example.
## Example
The firm's office manager adds each lawyer as a user, assigns a license and sets who can open which shared folders. Microsoft runs everything else.
W: SaaS: a finished email and productivity application such as Microsoft 365 needs no servers or development, and the provider handles infrastructure, updates and availability, which suits a firm without IT staff.
X: A=IaaS with VMs: the firm would have to install, configure and patch servers itself, which it has no staff to do.

  - database and storage configuration;

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.3 applied comparison
S: A consultant compares the three cloud service types for a client. Which service type does Microsoft describe as the least flexible, but the easiest to get up and running and the one needing the least technical expertise?
A: Infrastructure as a service (IaaS), because the provider hands over ready-made virtual machines with nothing else to set up.
B: Platform as a service (PaaS), because the provider maintains the operating system so that developers only deploy code.
C: Software as a service (SaaS), because the customer uses a fully developed application that the provider runs and maintains.
D: Serverless computing, because the provider runs event-driven code and the customer never sees or manages any servers.

G: ## Flexibility versus effort across service types
Think of a line from most control to least effort:
- **IaaS**: the **most flexible** category and gives maximum control, but you manage the OS, network configuration, storage and software. Most operational responsibility.
- **PaaS**: a middle ground. The provider manages the OS, middleware and runtime; you manage your code and data. Responsibility is roughly shared.
- **SaaS**: the **least flexible**, because you use an application as built, but the **easiest** to get running, needing the **least technical knowledge**. The provider manages almost everything.
## Where serverless fits
Serverless options such as Azure Functions are a way of running your own code without managing servers. You still write and maintain that code, so it is not "ready to use" in the way SaaS is.
## Example
Starting to use a SaaS email service can take an afternoon; building the same capability on IaaS means choosing VM sizes, installing mail server software and patching it.
W: SaaS: Microsoft describes it as the least flexible model but the easiest to get up and running, requiring the least technical knowledge, because you use a finished application.
X: A=IaaS: IaaS is the most flexible model and needs the most expertise, since the customer manages the operating system, networking and software on the VMs.
B=PaaS: PaaS removes OS management but still requires developers to build, deploy and maintain their own application.
D=Serverless computing: serverless removes server management, but customers still write and maintain the code, so it needs more expertise than using a finished application.
T: Most flexible and most work = IaaS; least flexible, least expertise, fastest to start = SaaS; PaaS sits in between.

@ AZ-900 D1 T1.3 advanced troubleshooting
S: A company uses Microsoft 365 (SaaS). A former employee could still read shared files two weeks after leaving, because nobody disabled her account. The CIO asks whether this was Microsoft's failure under the shared responsibility model. What is the correct answer?
A: Yes, because in SaaS the provider manages the whole stack, so Microsoft must remove accounts when employees leave a company.
B: Yes, because Microsoft runs the sign-in platform, so it is responsible for every account in the company's tenant and its use.
C: No, because user accounts are only a customer duty in IaaS, and this failure came from a datacenter problem on Microsoft's side.
D: No, because in SaaS the customer still manages its users and their access, while Microsoft runs the authentication platform.
K: D
G: ## Identity and access in SaaS
Even in SaaS, where the provider manages almost the entire application stack, some duties always stay with the customer:
- the **information and data** stored in the service;
- the **devices** allowed to connect;
- the **accounts and identities** of people, services and devices.
For identity and access, responsibility is **shared** in PaaS and SaaS: you manage your own users, roles and policies, while the provider runs the authentication platform, such as **Microsoft Entra ID** (formerly Azure AD).
## What this means in practice
Microsoft keeps the sign-in service running and secure. Only the company knows that an employee has left, so only the company can disable that account, remove licenses and review who still has access to its shared files.
## Example
A leaver checklist that disables the person's Microsoft Entra account on the last working day would have prevented this access.
W: Customer manages users and access: the provider runs the authentication platform, but accounts, identities and access decisions are always the customer's responsibility, so disabling a leaver's account was the company's job.
X: A=Provider manages everything in SaaS: SaaS moves most of the stack to the provider, but accounts and identities always remain with the customer.
B=Microsoft owns every account: running the sign-in platform does not make Microsoft responsible for deciding who in the company should still have access.
C=Datacenter problem: the access came from an account that was never disabled, not from a datacenter fault, and accounts stay a customer duty in every service type, not only IaaS.
T: In every service type, including SaaS, the customer owns its accounts, identities and access decisions; the provider owns the platform.
V: Microsoft 365, Microsoft Entra ID
