G: ## Why chatbots invent policies
A foundation model generates likely-sounding text from patterns in its training data. - **Human escalation**: when the answer is not supported or confidence is low, the bot hands off to a human agent instead of guessing.
## Example
A customer asks about returning opened electronics. The bot retrieves the current policy passage, answers from it, and if no relevant passage exists it says so and transfers the chat.

C=Larger model without human agents: a larger model can still hallucinate about company-specific policies it never saw, and removing humans takes away the safety net.
D=Higher temperature: raising temperature makes outputs more varied and less predictable, which tends to increase invented content rather than reduce it.

@ AIB-C01 D4 T4.4 advanced troubleshooting
S: A generative AI proof of concept performed well in testing, but after being pushed directly into production without establishing monitoring, versioning, or rollback procedures, an unexpected model update caused inconsistent outputs that disrupted a customer-facing process. Which gap most directly caused this disruption?
A: Missing MLOps practices and continuity safeguards for operating the model after the pilot ended
B: Too little workforce training, so staff could not interpret the model's outputs in daily work
C: Weak executive sponsorship, which left the project without a clear budget owner after launch
D: Limited cross-functional collaboration between the data science team and the business owners
K: A
G: ## From proof of concept to production
A proof of concept (PoC) answers one question: can this work? Production asks different ones: will it keep working, can we tell when it stops, and can we undo a bad change quickly?
**MLOps** is the set of practices that answers those questions for ML and generative AI systems:
- **Versioning**: pin the model version, prompts and configuration, so a provider or team update does not silently change behavior.
- **Monitoring**: track output quality, error rates, latency and cost, with alerts when they drift.
- **Controlled release and rollback**: test a new model version against an evaluation set, roll it out gradually, and switch back to the last known-good version if results degrade.
**Business continuity safeguards** add a fallback (a manual or rules-based path) so the customer process keeps running while the AI is fixed.
## Example
A team calls a model through a "latest" alias. The provider updates the model, answer formats change, and a downstream parser breaks. With a pinned version, an evaluation gate and a rollback plan, the update would have been caught before customers saw it.
W: Missing MLOps and continuity safeguards: no version pinning, no monitoring and no rollback meant an unexpected model update reached customers unnoticed and could not be reversed quickly, which is exactly the failure described.
X: B=Too little workforce training: training helps people use AI well, but it does not pin model versions or provide a rollback, so it cannot explain inconsistent outputs after a model update.
C=Weak executive sponsorship: sponsorship provides funding and priority. The disruption came from missing operational controls on the model, not from who owns the budget.
D=Limited cross-functional collaboration: closer work between teams helps adoption, but the direct cause here is that an untested model change reached a customer process with no monitoring or way back.
T: A PoC proves feasibility; production needs MLOps (versioning, monitoring, rollback) and a fallback, otherwise any model change becomes a customer-facing incident.

@ AIB-C01 D2 T2.2 advanced use-case
S: An AI initiative cost 500,000 dollars to implement in year one and is projected to generate 200,000 dollars in combined cost savings and revenue gains in that same year. Using the standard formula ROI = (Gain minus Cost) divided by Cost, what is the year-one ROI?
A: Positive 250 percent
B: Negative 60 percent
C: Negative 150 percent
D: Positive 40 percent
K: B
G: ## The basic ROI formula
Return on investment compares what an initiative gains with what it costs:
`ROI = (Gain - Cost) / Cost`, usually shown as a percentage.
- Gain is the total benefit attributed to the initiative (cost savings plus new revenue).
- Cost is the total investment for the same period (build, licenses, infrastructure, people, change management).
- A negative ROI means the initiative has not yet paid back its cost in that period; it does not automatically mean the project is a failure.
## Working the numbers
Gain = 200,000 and Cost = 500,000.
`(200,000 - 500,000) / 500,000 = -300,000 / 500,000 = -0.6`, so the year-one ROI is -60 percent.
AI projects often show a negative first year because implementation costs land up front, while benefits accumulate over several years. That is why business cases also look at multi-year ROI and payback period.
W: Negative 60 percent: subtracting the 500,000 cost from the 200,000 gain gives a net loss of 300,000, and dividing by the 500,000 cost gives -0.6, or -60 percent for year one.
X: A=Positive 250 percent: this is Cost divided by Gain (500,000 / 200,000 = 2.5), which inverts the ratio and ignores that the gain is smaller than the cost.
C=Negative 150 percent: this divides the net loss by the gain (-300,000 / 200,000) instead of by the cost, so the denominator is wrong.
D=Positive 40 percent: this is Gain divided by Cost (200,000 / 500,000), which shows how much of the cost was recovered but not the net return after subtracting the cost.
T: Subtract the cost first, then divide by the cost: a 200,000 gain on a 500,000 investment is -60 percent ROI, not a recovery ratio like 40 percent.
