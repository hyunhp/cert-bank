@ SC-900 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: A law firm replaces its own on-premises mail servers with a software as a service (SaaS) email offering. Under the shared responsibility model, which security task is still the firm's responsibility after the move?
A: Applying security updates to the operating systems of the servers that run the email service for the firm.
B: Deciding who in the firm can use the service and how the firm's tenant-level security settings are configured.
C: Maintaining the runtime environment and the application code that deliver mail to the firm's mailboxes.
D: Securing the physical hosts, network hardware and datacenter buildings where the mailboxes are stored.
K: B
G: ## How the split moves with the service model
In an on-premises datacenter the organization secures everything, from the building to the data. In the cloud some duties move to the provider, and how many depends on the model:
- **IaaS**: the provider secures the physical datacenter, network hardware and hosts. The customer runs the operating systems, applications, network controls it configures, and its data.
- **PaaS**: the provider also takes the operating system and the runtime. The customer keeps its application code, configuration, access controls and data.
- **SaaS**: the provider manages the entire application stack and the infrastructure under it. The customer keeps access management (who uses the service), its data, and its tenant-level settings.
## What never moves
Whatever the model, the customer keeps its **data**, its **identities and access**, its **endpoints** (laptops and phones) and its **configuration choices**. A misconfigured tenant can expose data even when the platform itself is secure.
## Example
After the move the firm no longer patches mail servers, but it still decides which staff get mailboxes, requires MFA for them, and sets the tenant's sharing options.
W: Access and tenant settings: in SaaS the provider runs the whole application stack, but controlling who uses the service and configuring tenant-level settings always stay with the customer.
X: A=Server OS updates: in SaaS (and already in PaaS) the provider manages the operating systems under the service, so the firm no longer patches them.
C=Runtime and application code: the SaaS provider owns the application it sells; the firm uses the email service but does not write or maintain its code.
D=Physical infrastructure: physical hosts, network hardware and buildings are the provider's job in every cloud model, including IaaS.
T: In SaaS the provider runs the whole stack; the customer still owns its data, who gets access and how the tenant is configured.

@ SC-900 D1 T1.1 applied comparison
S: A team moves a line-of-business application from its datacenter onto virtual machines that it creates in a public cloud (infrastructure as a service). Which security duty does the team keep that a platform as a service offering would have taken over?
A: Physical security of the datacenter, including badge readers and guards at the doors of the building.
B: Replacing failed disks and network cards in the physical host machines that run the virtual machines.
C: Patching and hardening the operating system installed on each virtual machine that the team created.
D: Classifying the application's data and deciding which users are allowed to read the stored records.
K: C
G: ## IaaS versus PaaS in the shared responsibility model
- In **IaaS** the provider secures the physical datacenter, network hardware and host machines. Everything from the operating system up is the customer's: OS patching, installed applications, the network controls it configures, and the data.
- In **PaaS** the provider also takes responsibility for the **operating system and the runtime environment**. The customer keeps its application code, configuration, access controls and data.
So the operating system is the clearest item that changes hands between IaaS and PaaS.
## Duties that never change hands
- The provider always owns the physical layer (buildings, hosts, network hardware).
- The customer always owns its data, identities and access, endpoints and configuration choices.
## Example
A VM-based web app needs the team to install monthly OS updates. Moved to a PaaS web hosting service, those updates are the provider's work, but the team still controls who can sign in and what data the app keeps.
W: Guest operating system: with IaaS the customer installs, patches and hardens the OS on its VMs; PaaS moves the OS and runtime to the provider.
X: A=Datacenter physical security: the provider already secures the building in IaaS, so this duty does not differ between IaaS and PaaS.
B=Host hardware: host machines and network hardware belong to the provider in IaaS as well, so the team never replaces disks in a public cloud.
D=Data classification and access: data and access decisions stay with the customer in every model, so PaaS would not take them over either.
T: The operating system is the customer's in IaaS and the provider's in PaaS; data and access are always the customer's.

@ SC-900 D1 T1.1 advanced use-case
S: Employees use an AI assistant delivered as a SaaS service. The assistant is connected to internal documents, and the security team worries that hidden instructions inside those documents could manipulate its answers. Under the AI shared responsibility model, who must address this risk?
A: The provider alone, because it hosts the AI model and runs the platform-level content filtering for every tenant.
B: The customer, through access controls, the connectors it enables, input validation and monitoring of the AI's use.
C: Nobody in particular, because prompt injection affects only AI models that the customer trains on its own hardware.
D: The provider alone, because connecting a SaaS assistant to internal documents makes those documents provider data.

Microsoft describes an AI-enabled solution in three layers:
- **AI platform**: infrastructure, the model, and platform-level safety controls such as built-in content filtering.

For SaaS (for example Microsoft 365 Copilot) or PaaS (for example Microsoft Foundry) AI, the provider typically secures the physical infrastructure, the model hosting environment and platform safety controls.

@ SC-900 D1 T1.1 applied definition
S: In a defense-in-depth strategy, a company divides its network into isolated zones and uses network security groups to restrict which systems can talk to each other. Which layer is the company strengthening?
A: The perimeter security layer, which protects the network boundary with DDoS protection and edge firewalls.
B: The compute security layer, which keeps virtual machine operating systems patched and closes unused ports.
C: The network security layer, which controls communication between resources inside the network itself.
D: The identity and access layer, which authenticates and authorizes users before they reach any resource.
K: C
G: ## Defense in depth
Defense in depth uses several layers of protection so that if one layer fails, an attacker still faces the next. Learn lists these layers, from outside in:
- **Physical security**: locked facilities, guards, badge readers, cameras.
- **Identity and access**: MFA, role-based access control, Conditional Access policies.
- **Perimeter**: DDoS protection and perimeter firewalls at the network boundary.
- **Network**: segmentation into isolated zones and network security groups (NSGs) that limit traffic between resources.
- **Compute**: patched operating systems, closed ports, restricted admin access for VMs and containers.
- **Application**: secure development, input validation against SQL injection and XSS.
- **Data**: access controls, encryption and classification; the innermost and most critical layer.
## Why segmentation matters
If an attacker compromises one server, segmentation and NSG rules stop it from freely reaching every other system, which limits the damage.
W: Network security layer: segmentation and NSGs control traffic between resources inside the network, so one compromised system cannot reach everything else.
X: A=Perimeter layer: this layer guards the boundary against large external attacks with DDoS protection and edge firewalls, not traffic between internal zones.
B=Compute layer: patching and closing ports protect each individual machine; they do not decide which internal systems may communicate.
D=Identity and access layer: this layer verifies users with MFA and RBAC before access; network zones and NSG rules are a different control.
T: Segmentation and NSGs belong to the network layer; DDoS protection and edge firewalls belong to the perimeter layer.

@ SC-900 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: An IT manager needs to protect two security goals: confirming that downloaded software files were not altered, and making sure that changes to finance records can be detected. Which TWO controls mainly protect the integrity of data? (Select TWO)
A: Comparing the cryptographic hash of each file with the value the publisher has published.
B: Load balancing across redundant servers so that the finance application stays online.
C: Digital signatures applied by the sender with a private key and verified with its public key.
D: Distributed denial-of-service protection that filters attack traffic before it reaches services.
E: Regular backups with tested recovery plans that restore service after a hardware failure.
K: A,C
G: ## The CIA triad
Security controls ultimately protect three properties:
- **Confidentiality**: only authorized people can read the information. Encryption and access controls protect it; a data breach violates it.
- **Integrity**: data stays accurate, complete and unmodified except through authorized processes. Tampering, corruption and unauthorized deletion violate it.
- **Availability**: authorized users can reach data and systems when they need them. DDoS attacks, ransomware, hardware failures and disasters threaten it.
## Integrity controls
Learn lists cryptographic **hashing** (a fingerprint that changes if the data changes), **digital signatures**, audit logs of changes and database transaction controls. Checking a downloaded file's hash against the publisher's value is a classic integrity check.
## Availability controls
Redundant infrastructure, load balancing, automatic failover, backups with tested recovery plans and DDoS protection keep systems reachable.
W: A=Hash comparison: a hash changes if even one byte of the file changes, so a match with the publisher's value shows the file was not altered.
C=Digital signatures: verifying a signature with the sender's public key proves the data came from that sender and was not modified after signing.
X: B=Load balancing: spreading traffic across redundant servers keeps the application reachable, which is availability rather than integrity.
D=DDoS protection: filtering flood traffic stops services from being knocked offline, an availability control.
E=Backups and recovery: tested restores bring systems back after failures; Learn groups them with availability controls, not with detecting changes.
T: Hashes and digital signatures detect changes (integrity); redundancy, backups and DDoS protection keep systems reachable (availability).

@ SC-900 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: A company stops giving administrators permanent admin rights. Instead, an administrator receives elevated access only for the specific task and only for the hours that task needs, and then it is removed. Which Zero Trust principle does this put into practice?
A: Verify explicitly, because every request is authenticated using all available signals such as device and location.
B: Use least privileged access, because access is limited with just-in-time and just-enough-access practices.
C: Assume breach, because the network is segmented and all traffic is encrypted end to end between the zones.
D: Trust the internal network, because administrators work from the corporate office on managed company devices.
K: B
G: ## Zero Trust and its three principles
Older "castle-and-moat" security trusted everything inside the network. Zero Trust assumes that no network, device, user or application is trusted by default, even after it has authenticated or joined a trusted network. Three principles guide it:
- **Verify explicitly**: authenticate and authorize using all available signals, such as identity, location, device and its compliance, the app, data classification and anomalies.
- **Use least privileged access**: give only the minimum access needed, for only as long as it is needed. This is done with **just-in-time (JIT)** access (granted when needed, revoked afterward), **just-enough-access (JEA)** (only the specific permissions a task needs) and risk-based adaptive policies.
- **Assume breach**: design as if an attacker will get in: segment access, encrypt end to end, and monitor so breaches are detected and contained quickly.
## Example
An admin requests a role to reset a server for two hours; the role expires automatically and never grants more than server reset rights.
W: Use least privileged access: time-limited (JIT) and task-limited (JEA) elevation is exactly how Learn describes implementing least privilege.
X: A=Verify explicitly: this principle is about checking many signals at each request, not about how much access is granted or for how long.
C=Assume breach: segmentation and end-to-end encryption limit damage after an intrusion, but the scenario is about narrowing admin rights.
D=Trust the internal network: Zero Trust rejects trusting a request because it comes from inside the corporate network.
T: Just-in-time and just-enough-access are the tools of the least privileged access principle.

@ SC-900 D1 T1.1 applied comparison
S: A security architect designs the environment as if attackers are already inside: workloads are split into segments, data is encrypted end to end, and analytics watch for unusual activity so intrusions are found fast. Which Zero Trust principle drives this design?
A: Verify explicitly, which bases every access decision on identity, device health, location and other signals.
B: Use least privileged access, which grants only the minimum permissions a person needs for a limited time.
C: Assume breach, which limits the blast radius and focuses on detecting and containing attackers quickly.
D: Defense at the perimeter, which keeps attackers out with strong firewalls at the edge of the corporate network.
K: C
G: ## Assume breach
The third Zero Trust principle asks you to design your security as if an attacker will eventually get through. Its typical controls are:
- **Segmenting access** so a compromised account or device cannot freely reach everything else.
- **Encrypting data end to end** so intercepted data is unreadable.
- **Monitoring and analytics** so a breach is detected quickly, its scope is understood, and the damage is limited.
## How it differs from the other two principles
- **Verify explicitly** concerns how each request is checked (many signals, not just a password or network location).
- **Use least privileged access** concerns how much access is granted and for how long (JIT and JEA).
Assume breach is about what happens after defenses have been passed.
## Example
Even if a phishing email gives an attacker one user's session, segmented networks keep the attacker away from the payroll database, and anomaly alerts start an investigation within minutes.
W: Assume breach: segmentation, end-to-end encryption and monitoring are the three controls Learn names for designing as if an attacker is already inside.
X: A=Verify explicitly: this principle covers checking signals before granting each access, not limiting damage after attackers are inside.
B=Use least privileged access: it narrows permissions with JIT and JEA; the scenario's segmentation and monitoring go beyond permission size.
D=Defense at the perimeter: relying on the network edge is the castle-and-moat approach that Zero Trust replaces, and it is not a Zero Trust principle.
T: Segment, encrypt end to end and monitor to detect quickly = assume breach.

@ SC-900 D1 T1.1 applied definition
S: In the Zero Trust model, one pillar aggregates and correlates signals from identities, devices, applications, data, infrastructure and networks, and triggers automated responses using SIEM and SOAR tools. Which pillar is this?
A: The applications pillar, which gives visibility into sanctioned and unsanctioned apps used across the organization.
B: The infrastructure pillar, which continuously assesses servers for version currency and configuration compliance.
C: The networks pillar, which applies micro-segmentation, real-time threat protection and encrypted network traffic.
D: The visibility, automation, and orchestration pillar, which integrates the signals of the other six pillars.
K: D
G: ## The seven Zero Trust pillars
Zero Trust is applied across seven connected pillars:
- **Identities**: users, services or devices, verified with strong authentication and governed by least privilege.
- **Devices**: health and compliance status feed access decisions.
- **Applications**: visibility into all apps, including unsanctioned shadow IT, and the permissions apps request.
- **Data**: classified, labeled and encrypted so it carries its own protection.
- **Infrastructure**: continuously assessed for version, configuration and unusual behavior, with JIT admin access.
- **Networks**: segmented, with micro-segmentation, real-time threat protection and encrypted traffic.
- **Visibility, automation, and orchestration**: the integrating seventh pillar.
## Why the seventh pillar exists
Each of the six pillars produces its own alerts. Without central correlation, analysts drown in separate low-severity events. A **SIEM** ingests and correlates signals across all pillars to detect threats; a **SOAR** uses those detections to run automated response playbooks.
W: Visibility, automation, and orchestration: it is the seventh pillar that collects signals from the other six and uses SIEM and SOAR to correlate them and respond automatically.
X: A=Applications pillar: it is one of the signal sources (app inventory, shadow IT, app permissions), not the layer that correlates all signals.
B=Infrastructure pillar: it assesses servers and produces telemetry, but it does not aggregate signals from every other pillar.
C=Networks pillar: segmentation and traffic encryption protect the network; correlation across all pillars belongs elsewhere.
T: Six Zero Trust pillars generate signals; the seventh (visibility, automation, and orchestration) correlates them with SIEM and SOAR.

@ SC-900 D1 T1.1 applied comparison
S: Two companies need to exchange encrypted files over the internet, but they have no secure way to agree on a shared secret key beforehand. Which approach solves this key distribution problem?
A: Symmetric encryption, because the same secret key is used to encrypt and decrypt, so it is the fastest to share.
B: Asymmetric encryption, because anyone can encrypt with a freely shared public key that only the private key opens.
C: Hashing, because each file is converted into a fixed-length digest that only the receiving company can reverse.
D: Salting, because a random value is added to each file so that the two companies no longer need any keys at all.
K: B
G: ## Two kinds of encryption
- **Symmetric encryption** uses one key to encrypt and decrypt. It is fast and suits large amounts of data, such as files on disk or database records. Its weakness is **key distribution**: both parties need the same secret key, and sending that key safely over a network is hard.
- **Asymmetric encryption** uses a mathematically related **public key and private key**. Data encrypted with the public key can only be decrypted with the matching private key, and the private key cannot be derived from the public key. Because the public key can be shared openly, asymmetric encryption solves key distribution. HTTPS and email encryption rely on it.
## Hashing is not encryption
Hashing turns input into a fixed-length digest with no key, and it cannot be reversed. It verifies data but never hides data that someone later needs to read.
## Example
The receiving company publishes its public key on its website. The sender encrypts the files with it; only the receiver's private key can open them.
W: Asymmetric encryption: the public key can be shared openly and only the holder of the matching private key can decrypt, so no secret has to be exchanged first.
X: A=Symmetric encryption: it is fast, but both sides need the same secret key, and sharing that key safely is exactly the problem described.
C=Hashing: a hash is one-way and uses no key, so the receiver could never turn the digest back into the original file.
D=Salting: a salt is added to passwords before hashing to defeat precomputed tables; it does not encrypt files or replace keys.
T: Asymmetric encryption solves key distribution; symmetric encryption is faster for bulk data once a key is shared.

@ SC-900 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: An application stores user passwords as hashes. Auditors warn that an attacker who steals the database could look up common passwords in precomputed rainbow tables, and that users with the same password have identical hashes. What best addresses both findings?
A: Encrypt the passwords with a symmetric key instead, so that the stored values can be decrypted later when needed.
B: Add a unique random salt to each password before hashing it, so identical passwords produce different hashes.
C: Hash every password twice with the same algorithm, so the stored value is longer and harder for attackers to guess.
D: Store the plain-text passwords in a separate database on a server that is not connected to the internet at all.
K: B
G: ## Why systems hash passwords
Hashing converts any input into a fixed-length **hash** (digest). It is one-way and uses no key, and the same input with the same algorithm always gives the same hash. A system stores only the hash of a password; at sign-in it hashes what the user typed and compares the two values. The real password is never stored.
## The weakness of plain hashing
Because hashing is **deterministic**, attackers can precompute hashes for millions of common passwords (**rainbow tables** or dictionary attacks) and look up stolen hashes. Two users with the same password also share the same hash.
## Salting
A **salt** is a unique, randomly generated value created for each password and added to it before hashing. Now two users with the password `Summer2026!` get different hashes, and a precomputed table is useless because the attacker would need a separate table for every salt. Learn calls salting a standard requirement for properly implemented authentication.
W: Per-password salt: a unique random value changes each hash, so identical passwords no longer match and precomputed rainbow tables stop working.
X: A=Symmetric encryption: reversible encryption means anyone who steals the key gets every password back; passwords only need to be compared, not decrypted.
C=Hashing twice: the same input still gives the same output each time, so identical passwords still match and attackers can precompute double hashes too.
D=Plain text on an isolated server: anyone who reaches that server reads every password directly, which is worse than storing salted hashes.
T: Hash passwords with a unique salt each: no stored passwords, no identical hashes, no rainbow tables.

@ SC-900 D1 T1.1 advanced definition
S: A bank already encrypts customer data at rest in storage and in transit with TLS. It also wants the data protected while it is loaded into memory and processed on shared cloud hardware. Which approach addresses this state of data?
A: Full-disk encryption on the storage accounts, which keeps data unreadable if the physical media are stolen.
B: HTTPS for every connection, so that the data moving between the browser and the servers cannot be read.
C: A VPN connection between offices, which protects traffic that crosses public networks between locations.
D: Confidential computing, which processes the data inside protected secure enclaves isolated from the host.
K: D
G: ## Three states of data
- **Data at rest**: stored on a device, such as a disk, a database or a cloud storage account. Encryption at rest keeps it unreadable even if the media are stolen.
- **Data in transit**: moving across the internet, between services or over a private network. **TLS** (as used by HTTPS) and VPNs protect it from eavesdropping.
- **Data in use**: being processed in memory (RAM) or by a CPU. Traditionally data is decrypted when loaded into memory, which leaves a window of exposure.
## Protecting data in use
**Confidential computing** creates protected execution environments, sometimes called **secure enclaves**, where data can be processed without being exposed even to the operating system or hypervisor. This matters most in multitenant clouds where customers share physical hardware.
W: Confidential computing: secure enclaves protect data while it is in memory and being processed, even from the host's operating system and hypervisor.
X: A=Full-disk encryption: it protects data at rest on storage, which the bank already has; data is decrypted when loaded into memory.
B=HTTPS: TLS protects data in transit between browser and server, not data being processed inside the server's memory.
C=VPN between offices: a VPN protects traffic over public networks, which is still data in transit.
T: At rest = storage encryption, in transit = TLS or VPN, in use = confidential computing (secure enclaves).

@ SC-900 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: A company encrypts its databases and now needs a policy for its encryption keys. Which TWO practices follow key management guidance? (Select TWO)
A: Store the keys in the same storage account as the encrypted data so that applications always find them quickly.
B: Keep keys in tamper-resistant hardware security modules or a managed key service, separate from the data.
C: Use one permanent key for all data indefinitely so that older backups can always be decrypted without lookups.
D: Give every developer and administrator read access to the keys so that troubleshooting is never blocked.
E: Rotate the keys regularly so that the amount of data exposed if one key is compromised stays limited.

G: ## Encryption is only as strong as its keys
A stolen or exposed key makes encrypted data readable, defeating the purpose of encryption. **Key management** covers how keys are generated, stored, protected, rotated and retired.
## Best practices on Learn
- **Store keys separately from the data**: if both sit together, an attacker who reaches one has both.
- **Use dedicated hardware**: **hardware security modules (HSMs)** are tamper-resistant devices built to store and protect keys.
- **Rotate keys regularly**: changing keys periodically limits how much data a compromised key exposes.
- **Control access tightly**: only authorized users and systems reach decryption keys, with strong authentication and least privilege.
## Managed key services
Cloud services such as **Azure Key Vault** centrally store and manage keys, certificates and secrets, support rotation, and integrate with other services so key material is not exposed to application code or administrators.
W: B=Separate, protected key storage: HSMs or a managed service such as Azure Key Vault keep keys apart from the data and resistant to theft.
E=Regular rotation: periodically changing keys limits the data an attacker can read with any one compromised key.
X: A=Keys next to the data: an attacker who gets into the storage account would then have both the encrypted data and the key to open it.
C=One permanent key: never rotating means a single compromise exposes all data ever encrypted with it.
D=Broad read access to keys: this violates least privilege; only the users and systems that need decryption should reach the keys.
T: Keep keys apart from the data in HSM-backed storage, rotate them and restrict who can use them.

@ SC-900 D1 T1.1 applied comparison
S: A retailer collects customer data in France, stores it in a cloud region in Germany and analyzes it with a service running in the United States. Its lawyers say the data is subject to the laws of each of these locations. Which compliance concept are they describing?
A: Data residency, which governs only the single physical location where the data must be stored by contract.
B: Data sovereignty, which says data is subject to the laws of each country or region where it is held or processed.
C: Data classification, which labels each record by sensitivity so that the strongest protections go to it first.
D: Data integrity, which keeps the records accurate and unmodified except through authorized business processes.
K: B
G: ## Three related compliance concepts
- **Data residency**: regulations about the physical locations where data can be stored, and how and when it may be transferred, processed or accessed internationally. In the cloud, organizations verify where the provider physically stores their data.
- **Data sovereignty**: data is subject to the laws and regulations of the country or region where it is physically collected, held or processed. One piece of data collected in one country, stored in a second and processed in a third can be subject to the laws of all three.
- **Data privacy**: the appropriate handling of personal data, including transparency, consent and the individual's rights to access, correct or delete it.
## Telling residency and sovereignty apart
Residency asks "where may this data be stored and moved?" Sovereignty asks "whose laws apply to this data because of where it is?"
W: Data sovereignty: the concept that data falls under the laws of every jurisdiction where it is collected, stored or processed, which is exactly what the lawyers describe.
X: A=Data residency: residency regulations govern where data may be stored and transferred; the lawyers' point is which laws apply in each place.
C=Data classification: labeling by sensitivity decides how strongly data is protected, not which jurisdictions' laws apply.
D=Data integrity: integrity is a security property (accurate, unmodified data) from the CIA triad, not a legal-jurisdiction concept.
T: Residency = where data may be stored and moved; sovereignty = the laws of every place data is collected, held or processed apply to it.

@ SC-900 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: During risk management, a company finds that a regional flood could damage its warehouse systems. It decides to buy an insurance policy that covers the financial losses from such an event. Which risk response is this?
A: Accept the risk, because the company decides the impact is low and takes no further action against it.
B: Mitigate the risk, because the company implements technical controls that reduce how likely the flood is.
C: Transfer the risk, because another party takes on the financial impact through a measure such as insurance.
D: Avoid the risk, because the company changes or stops the process that creates the risk in the first place.
K: C
G: ## GRC: governance, risk and compliance
- **Governance**: the rules, practices and processes an organization uses to direct and control its activities, such as who may access resources and who has admin rights and for how long.
- **Risk management**: identifying, assessing and responding to threats or events that could harm objectives or customer trust. The goal is not to remove all risk but to make informed decisions.
- **Compliance**: adhering to the laws, regulations, standards and policies that apply because of industry, geography and data types.
## The risk process
1. **Identify** risks through interviews, vulnerability assessments, audits and monitoring.
2. **Assess** impact and likelihood to produce a risk score.
3. **Respond**: **accept** (low impact), **mitigate** (implement controls), **transfer** (for example insurance) or **avoid** (change the process that creates the risk).
4. **Monitor** risks and the effectiveness of controls.
W: Transfer the risk: buying insurance moves the financial impact of the flood to the insurer, which is Learn's example of transferring risk.
X: A=Accept: accepting means taking no further action; the company actively buys coverage instead.
B=Mitigate: mitigation adds controls that reduce likelihood or impact; insurance changes who pays, not how likely a flood is.

@ SC-900 D1 T1.2 applied comparison

G: ## Authentication factors
Learn groups proof of identity into three categories:
- **Something you know**: a password, PIN or security question.
- **Something you have**: a mobile device, hardware security key or smart card.

Using one factor, such as a password alone, is **single-factor authentication**; if that factor is phished, the attacker has everything. **MFA** requires two or more factors **from different categories**. An identity provider talks to applications through **security tokens**: structured, time-limited packages of data issued after it verifies a user.
