@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.1 applied tradeoff
S: A legal summarization workflow handles 200 long contracts per day; quality matters more than speed, and the budget allows a premium model. A reference sheet shows a top-tier model with a large context window and higher cost, and a mid-tier model with a smaller window. Which selection is justified?
A: The mid-tier model, since the cheaper option is always the right default for any workflow regardless of the task's quality or length requirements
B: The top-tier model with the larger context window, verified on the suite, because the task is high-value, low-volume and long-document
C: The top-tier model for the first 100 contracts and the mid-tier for the rest, to average the cost
D: Whichever model the team used last time, to keep configurations consistent across workflows
K: B
G: ## Selection follows requirements
The exam guide asks you to select models by capability, latency, cost, geography and task requirements. Here the requirements point up-market: long documents, quality priority, low volume, available budget.
## Reasoning
- Context window: long contracts must fit, or the workflow needs chunking, which can lose cross-clause reasoning.
- Capability tier: nuanced legal summaries benefit from stronger models.
- Cost: 200 calls a day keeps even a premium model affordable; the evaluation confirms the quality gain is real.
Example: the top-tier model scores 12 points higher on the legal rubric and costs a few dollars a day more; the choice is justified by evidence.
W: Top-tier model justified by requirements and verified by evaluation: the selection criteria (quality, length, budget, low volume) all point the same way.
X: A=Cheapest by default: cost is one criterion; the task's quality and context requirements outweigh it here.
C=Split by count: the same task on different models yields inconsistent quality for no principled reason.
D=Same as last time: consistency is not a requirement of this task; each workflow is selected on its own needs.
T: Choose the model the task's requirements justify, including premium models when quality, length and budget support it.

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.3 applied troubleshooting
S: A workflow's execution history shows a growing number of runs ending in "timeout" over the last two weeks, with no change to the workflow. Which investigation sequence is right?
A: Increase the timeout immediately and move on, since timeouts simply mean the limit is too low
B: Use traces of timed-out runs to find which stage grew, correlate it with inputs or dependencies, fix that stage, and set a timeout with a fallback
C: Restart the workflow and its dependencies, since timeouts that appear after a long period of normal operation usually indicate a stuck process somewhere
D: Switch to a faster model, since model latency is the only stage that can cause a timeout
K: B
G: ## Timeouts have a cause
The exam guide names execution history, traces and logs as the evidence for operating AI systems, and asks for targeted optimization. A rising timeout rate is a trend to explain, not a limit to raise.
## Investigation
- Traces: which stage's duration grew? Retrieval over a larger corpus, a slower external tool, longer contexts?
- Correlation: input sizes over time, dependency response times, model version changes.
- Fix the stage: tighten retrieval, add caching, parallelize, or negotiate the dependency's performance.
- Timeout policy: set a limit that reflects the fixed workflow, with a defined fallback (partial answer, async completion) rather than a silent failure.
Example: an external tool's latency doubled after their release; traces show it, and the tool is made asynchronous with a status update.
W: Trace-based diagnosis of the growing stage, then a fix and a sensible timeout with fallback: the trend is explained and resolved rather than papered over.
X: A=Raise the timeout: the stage keeps growing; users wait longer until the next timeout.
C=Restart: a trend over two weeks is not a stuck process; restarting changes nothing.
D=Faster model: traces may show the model is not the stage that grew; guessing a stage skips the evidence.
T: Treat rising timeouts as a trend to diagnose from traces, then fix the stage and define fallback behaviour.

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.4 applied use-case
S: An agent answers the same 50 frequently asked policy questions thousands of times a day, alongside a long tail of unique questions. Costs are high. Which optimization is appropriate?
A: Cache answers for recurring context-independent questions, keyed on normalized question and policy version; unique questions still run fully
B: Cache every answer the agent ever gives and serve the cached one whenever the new question is somewhat similar
C: Refuse to answer the frequently asked questions and point users to the policy document
D: Lower the model's temperature so that repeated questions generate identical answers, which the provider bills at a discounted rate for repeated output
K: A
G: ## Caching where it is safe
The exam guide lists caching among optimizations, with the caveat "where safe". Safe caching requires that the answer depends only on the cache key: the same question, the same policy version, and no user-specific context.
## Design
- Normalize the question (case, punctuation, canonical phrasing); map to a cached answer keyed with the policy version.
- Invalidate on policy change.
- Exclude questions that depend on the user (their balance, their contract).
- Measure: hit rate, cost saved, and quality of cached answers in the evaluation suite.
Example: 60% of traffic hits the cache; cost falls by more than half with no change to answer quality.
W: Keyed cache for recurring context-independent questions with invalidation: it cuts cost on the repetitive share while keeping correctness and freshness.
X: B=Cache everything by similarity: "somewhat similar" questions can need different answers, and user-specific answers would leak between users.
C=Refuse FAQs: the agent's main value is removed to save cost.
D=Temperature discount: providers do not bill by output determinism; identical answers still cost a model call.
T: Cache answers only for context-independent, recurring questions, keyed on question and content version, with invalidation.

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.2 applied use-case
S: A company registers a custom model endpoint and a provider-hosted catalog model for the same workflow. Which is the right role for each, and what must be verified?
A: Use the custom endpoint only, since self-hosted models are always more secure
B: Define primary and fallback by evaluation and policy; verify both pass the suite, meet residency rules, and are visible in observability
C: Alternate between them randomly to balance the load
D: Use the catalog model only, since custom endpoints are meant for experimentation and are not supported for production workflows
K: B
G: ## Operating multiple models
The exam guide covers multi-provider resilience, BYOM and custom endpoints, and observability of which model served each request. Having two models is only useful if roles, quality and compliance are defined.
## What to verify
- Evaluation: both models pass the suite; know the quality difference.
- Policy: data residency and sensitivity rules allow each model for this data.
- Observability: traces record the model and endpoint per request; cost and latency tracked per model.
- Switching: automatic fallback on errors, manual switch for maintenance.
Example: the custom endpoint is primary for sensitive data; the catalog model is fallback for availability, with the suite confirming acceptable quality.
W: Defined primary and fallback with evaluation, policy and observability verified: the two models become a resilient, governed configuration.
X: A=Custom only: self-hosting is not automatically more secure or more available; a single model is a single point of failure.
C=Random alternation: inconsistent answers and no principle; it is not load balancing for an AI workflow.
D=Catalog only: custom endpoints are a supported option; dismissing them ignores the residency or licensing reasons they exist.
T: With several models, define roles by evaluation and policy and make every request's model visible in observability.

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.4 advanced troubleshooting
S: A trace shows a workflow calling the model four times in sequence: classify, extract, validate with the model, and summarize. Each call is short, but the end-to-end latency is dominated by the four round-trips. What should the engineer consider?
A: Merging all four steps into one large prompt regardless of the effect on accuracy, since fewer model calls is always better for latency and cost
B: Run independent steps in parallel, replace model-based validation with deterministic checks, and merge steps only where the suite shows no loss
C: Adding a fifth call that decides whether the other four are needed
D: Accepting the latency, since multi-step workflows are inherently slow
K: B
G: ## Execution structure as a performance lever
The exam guide lists execution structure among the things to optimize without sacrificing quality or safety. Sequential model calls add their latencies; some can be parallelized, some replaced by code, some merged.
## Approach
- Dependency analysis: classify and extract may be independent of each other.
- Validation: if it checks formats and ranges, code is faster and more reliable than a model call.
- Merging: combine steps into one structured-output call where the suite shows quality holds.
- Measure latency and quality before and after each change.
Example: parallel classify/extract, deterministic validation, and a single summarize call cut latency by 60% with no evaluation regression.
W: Parallelize, replace with code, merge with evidence: this targets the round-trips directly while protecting quality through evaluation.
X: A=Merge everything blindly: a single overloaded prompt often loses accuracy; the suite must decide.
C=A fifth call: it adds latency to decide whether to add latency.
D=Accept it: the structure is improvable; accepting the latency ignores clear levers.
T: Reduce sequential model calls by parallelizing, replacing with deterministic code and merging where evaluation allows.

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.3 applied use-case
S: A product owner asks for proof that a new AI feature is "working well" after two weeks in production. Which evidence from observability answers this properly?
A: A screenshot of one impressive conversation
B: Volume, success and failure rates, latency distribution, cost trends, tool-call patterns, user feedback, and the suite score versus baseline
C: The number of distinct users who opened the feature during the two weeks, since adoption by users is the clearest sign of success
D: The provider's monthly invoice showing increased usage
K: B
G: ## Evidence of a working AI system
The exam guide asks engineers to operate AI systems with observability (traces, execution history, token telemetry, cost signals) and feedback, and to monitor real behaviour and regressions. "Working well" is a multi-metric claim.
## The evidence set
- Volume and success/failure rates.
- Latency (p50, p95) and trends.
- Cost per request and total, with token telemetry.
- Tool-call patterns and error categories.
- User feedback and overturn rates.
- Evaluation score of the deployed version versus baseline.
Example: 12,000 requests, 1.2% failures, p95 3.1 s, cost within budget, 4% thumbs-down, suite at 91%: a defensible "working well".
W: A multi-metric view with a baseline comparison: it substantiates the claim across quality, reliability, cost and user response.
X: A=One screenshot: a single good conversation is an anecdote; it says nothing about failure rates, latency, cost or the thousands of other conversations.
C=Adoption count: people may keep using a feature that answers badly because they have no alternative; adoption is not quality.
D=Invoice: spend shows that the feature was used, not whether the usage went well; a broken feature that retries endlessly also raises the invoice.
T: Demonstrate an AI feature's health with observability metrics, feedback and evaluation compared with baseline.

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.1 applied comparison
S: Which situation most justifies routing a request to a more capable (and more expensive) model within a workflow that otherwise uses a mid-tier model?
A: Every request, to be safe
B: Requests the mid-tier model flags as low-confidence or fails validation on, plus pre-identified high-consequence request types
C: Requests that arrive during business hours, when users are more demanding and more likely to notice a lower-quality answer
D: Requests from the engineering team, who can judge the quality difference
K: B
G: ## Tiered routing
Model selection need not be one model per workflow. The exam guide asks for targeted optimization rather than simply choosing a larger model; routing difficult or consequential cases to a stronger model is such a targeted choice.
## Criteria
- Confidence or validation failure: the cheap model could not produce an acceptable answer.
- High-consequence request types: the cost of error justifies the cost of the model.
- Measured: the evaluation suite shows the premium model actually improves these cases.
Example: 8% of requests are escalated; accuracy on those rises from 71% to 90%, at a tenth of the cost of using the premium model for everything.
W: Routing by low confidence, validation failure and consequence: the expensive model is used where it measurably matters.
X: A=Every request: this is the untargeted choice the guide warns against; most requests gain nothing.
C=Business hours: time of day does not change what a request needs.
D=By requester: quality should not depend on who asks; routing is about the request.
T: Escalate to stronger models on evidence (confidence, validation, consequence), not blanket usage.

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.3 applied troubleshooting
S: Cost for an AI workflow doubled last month. Token telemetry shows input tokens per request are unchanged, but requests per day doubled. What should the engineer conclude and do?
A: The prompt has become too long; shorten it to cut the cost back
B: The increase is volume-driven; investigate where the extra requests come from (new consumer, retries, a loop, growth) before changing the workflow
C: Switch to a cheaper model immediately, since the doubled bill shows that cost is the metric that matters most to the business right now
D: Reduce the maximum output tokens, which halves cost regardless of volume
K: B
G: ## Reading cost signals correctly
The exam guide lists token telemetry and cost signals among observability evidence. Cost = requests × tokens × price. With tokens per request flat, the driver is request count.
## Investigation
- Who sends the extra requests: a new application, an automation with a loop, a retry policy misfiring, or real adoption.
- Traces and execution history by caller and time of day.
- Then decide: fix a defect (loop, retries), or plan for growth (caching, tiered models, budget).
Example: a new automation retried failed calls without backoff, generating thousands of extra requests per hour; fixing retries halves the bill.
W: Volume-driven cost traced to its source: the telemetry says per-request size is not the issue, so the right move is to find out where the requests come from.
X: A=Shorten the prompt: input tokens did not change; the prompt is not the cause.
C=Cheaper model now: it masks a possible defect (loop or retry storm) and may lower quality for nothing.
D=Fewer output tokens: it reduces per-request cost somewhat but ignores the doubling in volume and its cause.
T: Decompose cost into volume and per-request size, and investigate whichever factor moved.
