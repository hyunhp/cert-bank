# PLTR-AIE batch 38: Domain 5 round 5 (Evaluation, Testing, and Quality Assurance), 8 questions

@ PLTR-AIE D5 T5.1 applied use-case
S: A team must evaluate an agent whose answers should be short, cite a source, and avoid promises about delivery dates. Which evaluator design captures all three?
A: One exact-match evaluator against a reference answer written for each case, which fails any answer that differs from it in wording
B: Three criteria: a deterministic length check, a citation check against retrieved chunks, and a calibrated rubric for promises
C: A single model-graded score from 1 to 10 for overall answer quality, applied uniformly to every case in the suite
D: Human review of all of the agent's outputs once a quarter by the support team, with a summary report of what they noticed
K: B
G: ## Composite evaluators
The exam guide asks for evaluators matched to the behaviour measured. Three different requirements need three different checks.
## Design
- Length: character or sentence count; deterministic.
- Citation: present and refers to a retrieved chunk id; deterministic.
- No delivery promises: rubric with examples; model-graded; calibrated with humans.
- Per-criterion reporting; release gates per criterion.
Example: an answer that is short and cited but says "it will arrive Tuesday" fails only the promise criterion, which points to the fix.
W: Criterion-specific evaluators, deterministic where possible: each requirement is measured precisely and failures are attributable.
X: A=Exact match: correct answers in different words fail; the three criteria are not measured.
C=Single score: a 6 out of 10 does not say whether the answer was too long, uncited or promised a date; nothing is attributable.
D=Quarterly humans: three months of unmeasured outputs between reviews, and no automated check on changes in between.
T: Build evaluators per requirement, using deterministic checks wherever the requirement allows.

@ PLTR-AIE D5 T5.2 applied use-case
S: A workflow decides whether to auto-approve small purchase requests. The team wants evaluation cases that specifically test unauthorized requests. What should these cases contain?
A: Only valid requests from authorized users, since unauthorized requests are rejected by platform permissions before the workflow ever runs
B: Requesters without rights (expired role, wrong department, contractor), spoofed authority in the text, and on-behalf requests, expecting refusal
C: Requests with very large amounts far above the small-purchase threshold, submitted by users who are fully authorized
D: Requests written entirely in capital letters or with unusual punctuation, to see whether the workflow handles messy formatting
K: B
G: ## Unauthorized-case design
The exam guide lists unauthorized cases among those to test and asks for object-state assertions. Authorization can fail at several points: identity, role, impersonation.
## Cases
- Requester without rights: expected refusal.
- Text claiming authority ("approved by the CFO"): expected refusal; the text is data.
- Requests on behalf of others: expected routing to the right approver.
- Assertion: no approval object or state change created.
Example: a contractor's request is refused and logged; no approval exists afterwards.
W: Identity, role and impersonation cases with no-change assertions: the ways authorization can be bypassed are tested directly.
X: A=Only valid: assumes permissions are perfect; the workflow's own checks must be tested.
C=Large amounts: a high-consequence case, not an authorization case.
D=Capital letters: a formatting robustness case; it says nothing about whether the workflow checks who is asking.
T: Test unauthorized requests across identity, role and impersonation, asserting that nothing is approved.

@ PLTR-AIE D5 T5.3 applied troubleshooting
S: A suite's score dropped after a release, but the drop is entirely in cases whose expected answers were recently edited by a new team member. What is the likely issue?
A: The release broke the function on exactly those cases, and the timing of the edits to their expected answers is a coincidence
B: The expected answers may have been edited incorrectly; review the edits against the source of truth first, and version the suite
C: The grader has started malfunctioning and is now marking correct answers as wrong for a subset of the cases
D: The model provider silently changed the underlying model, which happens to affect those particular cases the most
K: B
G: ## Suites are data too
The exam guide asks you to separate regression from test-distribution change. Edited expected answers change the suite; the function may be unchanged.
## Steps
- Diff the suite version before and after the edits.
- Verify the new expected answers against the source of truth (documentation, domain experts).
- Version control the suite; review changes like code.
Example: the new member "corrected" 20 expected answers using outdated policy; reverting them restores the score, and the release is fine.
W: Review the suite edits first: a score drop confined to edited cases points at the suite, not the release.
X: A=Release broke it: possible, but the coincidence with the edits must be checked first.
C=Grader broken: a grader failure would not be confined to edited cases.
D=Provider change: a model change would shift results across many case types, not land exactly on the cases a new colleague edited.
T: Version evaluation suites and review changes to expected answers like code.

@ PLTR-AIE D5 T5.4 applied use-case
S: A workflow's outputs are reviewed by humans who sometimes reject them without giving a reason. The team wants useful feedback. What should the review interface capture?
A: Only a reject button, to keep the review process as fast as possible for reviewers who handle hundreds of outputs a day
B: A structured reason (category plus optional text), the corrected output when available, and the input reference
C: A thumbs-up button only, on the theory that outputs without a thumbs-up can be assumed to have been rejected
D: A field for the reviewer's mood at the time of review, which the team can use to normalize the rejection rate across reviewers
K: B
G: ## Structured feedback capture
The exam guide asks for feedback loops that convert corrections into evaluation cases and priorities. A bare rejection cannot be converted into anything.
## Capture
- Reason category: wrong fact, missing info, tone, unsafe, other.
- Corrected output (optional).
- Input reference and trace id.
- Aggregation by category → fixes and cases.
Example: 60% of rejections are "missing info"; retrieval k is raised and 25 cases added; rejections halve.
W: Structured reasons with corrections and references: rejections become analyzable and actionable.
X: A=Reject only: a count of rejections with no reasons cannot be turned into a fix or a test case.
C=Thumbs-up only: silence is ambiguous (not reviewed, or rejected?) and there is no information about what was wrong.
D=Mood: the reviewer's state of mind does not explain what was wrong with the output; it is noise.
T: Capture structured reasons and corrections with each human rejection.

@ PLTR-AIE D5 T5.1 applied tradeoff
S: A team debates whether to grade a summarization function with a reference summary and similarity scoring, or with a rubric. Summaries can legitimately differ in structure. What should they choose?
A: Similarity scoring against one reference summary per case, since a numeric similarity is objective and easy to track over time
B: A rubric for required facts, absence of hallucinated facts, length and clarity, graded by a calibrated model with human spot checks
C: Exact string match to the reference summary, since anything less than an identical summary might be hiding an error
D: No automated evaluation at all, since summaries are subjective and any metric would be arbitrary and misleading
K: B
G: ## Evaluating open-ended text
The exam guide asks for evaluators that fit the output. Summaries with legitimate structural variation need content-based rubrics, not similarity to a single reference.
## Rubric
- Must-include facts (per case); must-exclude (hallucinations).
- Length bounds; clarity criterion.
- Model-graded with calibration; disagreements reviewed.
Example: two summaries with different structure both pass because they contain the five required facts and nothing false.
W: Content-based rubric with calibration: measures what matters (facts, no fabrication) and tolerates legitimate variation.
X: A=Similarity to a reference: penalizes valid summaries that differ in form.
C=Exact match: two correct summaries almost never match word for word; the metric would fail nearly every valid output.
D=No evaluation: whether the five required facts are present and nothing false was added is objective and measurable.
T: Grade summaries on required content and absence of fabrication, not on similarity to one reference.

@ PLTR-AIE D5 T5.2 applied use-case
S: Which case best tests an agent's high-consequence behaviour around data deletion?
A: A request to list the records that would be deleted by a filter, expecting an accurate count and sample without any change
B: A request to delete all records matching a broad filter, expecting the agent to show scope, require confirmation, delete nothing
C: A request to rename a single record's title, expecting the rename to be applied correctly and nothing else to change
D: A question about what the retention policy says regarding deletion, expecting a grounded answer with a citation
K: B
G: ## High-consequence cases
The exam guide asks for high-consequence cases with escalation as the expected outcome. Bulk deletion is irreversible and broad.
## Case
- Input: "delete all records with status X".
- Expected: the agent reports the count, asks for confirmation (or escalates above a threshold), and deletes nothing yet.
- Assertion: record count unchanged after the turn.
Example: "This would delete 4,120 records. Do you want me to proceed?" with no deletion until confirmed.
W: A broad deletion request expecting scope disclosure and confirmation with no change: the archetypal high-consequence case.
X: A=List only: a read-only preview; useful as a normal case, but it cannot test how the agent behaves when asked to destroy data.
C=Rename one: a reversible change to one record; the consequence is minimal and no escalation is expected.
D=Policy question: an informational answer; nothing is at stake beyond a possibly wrong explanation.
T: Test destructive bulk actions with cases that expect confirmation and no change.

@ PLTR-AIE D5 T5.3 applied use-case
S: A team runs evaluation only on release day. A regression is found and the release slips a week. How can the process be improved?
A: Skip the pre-release evaluation in future so that releases can never slip because of a regression found at the last minute
B: Run the fast tier on every change during development and the full suite nightly, so regressions surface when introduced
C: Run the evaluation suite only after each release, when the function is already in production, to avoid delaying the release
D: Reduce the suite to ten representative cases so that it runs instantly on release day and cannot delay anything
K: B
G: ## Shift evaluation left
The exam guide asks for regression detection across changes. Finding regressions at release time is the most expensive moment.
## Practice
- Fast tier on every commit or draft publish.
- Full suite nightly and before release.
- Failures fixed while the change is fresh.
Example: a prompt change that regresses multilingual cases is caught the same afternoon it is made.
W: Continuous fast evaluation with nightly full runs: regressions surface when introduced, not on release day.
X: A=Skip: the regression that was caught would instead have reached every user; avoiding a slip by shipping a defect is no improvement.
C=After release: users experience the regression first and the suite merely confirms what they already reported.
D=Ten cases: a suite that small cannot cover the behaviours that regress; speed is bought with blindness.
T: Evaluate continuously during development, not only at release.

@ PLTR-AIE D5 T5.4 advanced tradeoff
S: A function's evaluation suite has grown from feedback to 3,000 cases, many near-duplicates. Running it is slow and the score barely moves. What is a sound maintenance approach?
A: Keep adding every feedback case to the suite forever, since each one represents a real failure that must never recur
B: Curate: deduplicate near-identical cases, keep a stratified core, archive the rest, and admit only cases covering new failure types
C: Delete the whole suite and start over from scratch with a small set of cases written by the team this week
D: Stop adding any new cases to the suite from now on, so that its runtime stays fixed and the score remains comparable over time
K: B
G: ## Suite curation
The exam guide asks for representative suites and sustainable regression testing. Size is not coverage; near-duplicates add cost without information.
## Approach
- Deduplicate by similarity; keep one per cluster.
- Stratify: categories × difficulty; ensure coverage.
- Archive: full historical set for periodic deep runs.
- Admission rule: new cases must add a new failure type or category.
Example: 3,000 cases become a 400-case core that runs in minutes and a 3,000-case archive run monthly.
W: Deduplicated, stratified core with an archive and admission rules: coverage preserved, cost controlled.
X: A=Add forever: near-duplicates multiply runtime without adding information until nobody runs the suite at all.
C=Start over: years of real failure cases are discarded, and the regressions they guarded against can return unnoticed.
D=Stop adding: new failure types discovered in production would never be covered; the suite freezes while the system evolves.
T: Curate evaluation suites for coverage, not size.
