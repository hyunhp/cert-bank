# PLTR-AIE batch 08: Domain 7 (Model Selection, Observability, and Performance), 8 questions

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.1 applied tradeoff
S: A workflow must classify 1 million short product descriptions into 12 categories nightly, within a four-hour window and a fixed budget. A reference sheet lists available models with their capability tier, latency, cost per token and hosting region. Which selection approach is right?
A: Choose the most capable model on the sheet, since classification accuracy is what matters most and the extra cost is justified by measurably better results
B: Choose the smallest model whose tier handles simple classification, verify it on the evaluation suite, and confirm throughput and cost fit the constraints
C: Choose the model with the lowest latency, since one million calls in four hours means that per-call speed decides whether the job finishes
D: Choose the model hosted closest to the data, since network distance is the main driver of both latency and cost for batch workloads
K: B
G: ## Selecting a model from reference characteristics
The exam guide asks you to select a model from supplied reference characteristics using capability, latency, cost, geography and task requirements. The task here is simple and large: short texts, 12 categories, a million rows.
## Matching the task
- Capability: a classification of short text does not need the top tier; a smaller model usually performs comparably and costs far less.
- Throughput and cost: a million calls makes per-call cost dominant; the budget and window are hard constraints to check with arithmetic, not assumptions.
- Verify: run the candidate on the evaluation suite; if accuracy is below the requirement, step up one tier and re-check.
- Geography matters when data residency rules apply; it is a constraint to respect, not the selection criterion here.
Example: a mid-tier model at a fifth of the cost reaches 97% on the suite versus 97.5% for the largest, and fits the budget with room to spare.
W: Smallest adequate model, verified on the suite and against the window and budget: the choice is driven by the task's requirements and constraints rather than by maximum capability.
X: A=Most capable model: cost per call multiplied by a million likely breaks the budget, and the accuracy gain for simple classification is marginal.
C=Lowest latency only: batch jobs can run calls in parallel; throughput and cost decide, and the fastest model may be inaccurate or expensive.
D=Closest hosting: region matters for compliance and some latency, but it does not determine capability or cost for a batch job.
T: Pick the smallest model that meets the task's accuracy requirement, then confirm cost and throughput against the constraints.
V: AIP, model catalog

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.2 applied use-case
S: A customer-facing assistant depends on a single model provider. During a provider outage last month the assistant was down for three hours. The business wants the assistant to keep working through such outages. Which design fits?
A: Configure a fallback to an equivalent model from a second provider, tested with the evaluation suite so answers stay acceptable on the fallback
B: Increase the retry count and timeout for the primary provider, so that requests keep trying until the provider recovers
C: Cache every answer the assistant has ever given and serve cached answers during an outage, since most questions have been asked before
D: Train a small in-house model on past conversations to use during outages, so the assistant never depends on any external provider
K: A
G: ## Multi-provider resilience
The exam guide lists multi-provider resilience, bring-your-own-model (BYOM) and custom endpoints among the operational options. A single provider is a single point of failure; resilience means having a second path that is ready before it is needed.
## Doing it properly
- Fallback model: choose a comparable model from another provider; the prompt may need small adjustments.
- Evaluate: run the suite with the fallback so quality, groundedness and refusal behaviour are known, not hoped for.
- Switching: automatic on provider errors, with observability showing which model served each request.
- Cost and geography: confirm the fallback respects the same residency and budget constraints.
Example: during the next outage, requests fail over within seconds; the dashboard shows 100% of traffic on the fallback and a slightly lower but acceptable groundedness score.
W: Tested fallback on a second provider: the assistant keeps working during an outage, and the evaluation proves the fallback's answers are acceptable rather than assumed.
X: B=More retries: retries against a provider that is down just wait longer; the outage lasts as long as it lasts.
C=Answer cache: new or rephrased questions miss the cache, and cached answers can be stale or wrong for a different user's context.
D=In-house training: training models is outside the AI engineer's role and far more effort than a second provider; a small model would also be a quality downgrade.
T: For availability, configure and evaluate a fallback model on another provider before an outage happens.
V: AIP, model catalog

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.3 applied troubleshooting
S: Users report that an agent's answers have become slower and less accurate over the past week, but nobody changed the agent. Which source of evidence should the engineer consult first?
A: The agent's system prompt, re-reading it line by line to find instructions that could cause slow or inaccurate answers
B: AIP Observability: session traces, execution history, latency and token telemetry over the week, to see which stage changed
C: The model provider's public status page, since degradation on the provider side is the most common cause of a slow week
D: A fresh evaluation run of the agent, comparing the score with last month's, which tells whether the degradation is real or imagined
K: B
G: ## Observability first
The exam guide lists AIP Observability, execution history, session traces, distributed traces, logs, token telemetry and cost signals as the evidence for operating AI systems. "Nobody changed the agent" does not mean nothing changed: the corpus, the Ontology data, the model's version at the provider, or the questions users ask can all shift.
## Reading the week
- Latency breakdown per stage: did retrieval get slower (bigger corpus, more chunks) or the model call?
- Token telemetry: longer contexts over time suggest retrieval is returning more or longer chunks.
- Tool calls: more calls per session point to changed tool behaviour or new question types.
- Error and retry rates, and which model version served requests.
Example: traces show retrieved context doubled after a new document set was indexed, raising both latency and noise; the fix is in retrieval, not the prompt.
W: Observability data over the period: it shows which stage changed and when, which is the only way to diagnose a drift that no deliberate change explains.
X: A=Re-read the prompt: the prompt did not change, so reading it cannot explain a change that happened during the week.
C=Provider status page: a possible cause, but the traces will show whether model latency changed; starting from an external page skips the system's own evidence.
D=Evaluation run: a score confirms degradation on fixed cases but does not say which stage drifted or why; the traces do, and they cover real traffic.
T: When a live AI system drifts, start from traces and telemetry to find the stage that changed.
V: AIP Observability

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.4 applied troubleshooting
S: An AIP Logic function's cost has tripled in a month. The trace of a typical run shows the system prompt now includes a 9,000-token policy document that was added "for safety", and the model is called twice because a second call checks the first call's answer. Which optimization should come first?
A: Switch both calls to a cheaper model, which cuts the cost per token and brings the monthly bill back down without changing the prompt or the two-call design
B: Retrieve only the relevant policy section instead of embedding the whole document, and evaluate whether the second checking call improves outcomes
C: Reduce the maximum output tokens for both calls, since output tokens are billed at a higher rate than the input tokens
D: Add caching of the system prompt so that the 9,000-token document is sent once a day rather than on every run
K: B
G: ## Cost follows tokens and calls
Per-run cost is roughly (input tokens + output tokens) × price, times the number of calls. A 9,000-token document sent on every run multiplies the input cost of every call, and a second call doubles everything. The exam guide asks you to optimize prompts, context, model choice, caching and execution structure without losing quality or safety.
## Targeted changes
- Context: policy text belongs in retrieval; inject the section that the run needs, not the whole document.
- Execution structure: measure the second call. If the suite shows it rarely changes the outcome, remove it or run it only for high-consequence cases.
- Only then consider model choice and caching, with the suite confirming quality.
Example: context drops from 9,500 to 1,200 tokens and the checker call is kept only for flagged cases; cost falls below the original level.
W: Retrieve the relevant policy section and evaluate the second call: these address the two causes shown in the trace, and the evaluation ensures the "safety" rationale is tested rather than assumed.
X: A=Cheaper model for everything: this reduces price per token but keeps sending 9,000 tokens twice per run; quality may drop while the structural waste remains.
C=Fewer output tokens: output is a small part of the cost here; the input document dominates.
D=Daily prompt caching: a prompt is sent on every call; provider-side caching, where it exists, reduces but does not remove the cost, and the document still fills the context.
T: Cut cost where the trace shows it: oversized context and unnecessary calls, verified by evaluation, before changing models.
V: AIP Logic, AIP Observability

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.2 applied comparison
S: A regulated bank must run an LLM workflow with a model hosted inside its own environment, using a model it has licensed itself. The platform's catalog models are hosted by external providers. What should the AI engineer use?
A: A catalog model from the provider with the strongest contractual data-protection terms, since contractual guarantees satisfy the hosting requirement
B: A bring-your-own-model or custom endpoint configuration that registers the bank's own hosted model so AIP workflows call it like any other model
C: A local open-source model run inside a Code Repository transform for every call, bypassing the platform's model configuration
D: The catalog model that is hosted in the bank's geographic region, which keeps the data within the required jurisdiction
K: B
G: ## BYOM and custom endpoints
The exam guide asks you to understand multi-provider resilience, BYOM and when a custom endpoint is appropriate. BYOM lets an organization register a model it hosts or licenses so that AIP Logic, agents and pipelines call it through the platform, with the platform's permissions, observability and governance applied.
## When it is the right choice
- The model must run inside the organization's environment or a specific tenancy.
- The organization has licensed a model the catalog does not offer.
- Model behaviour must be pinned and controlled by the organization.
Example: the bank's hosted model is registered as an endpoint; the workflow selects it, and the traces show every call going to that endpoint and never to an external provider.
W: BYOM or custom endpoint: it meets the hosting and licensing requirement while keeping the workflow inside the platform's governance and tooling.
X: A=Strong contractual terms: a contract does not move the model inside the bank's environment; the requirement is about where the model runs.
C=Model inside a transform: running inference ad hoc in code bypasses observability, permissions and reuse, and does not work for interactive agents or Logic functions.
D=Regional catalog model: a region constraint is not the same as self-hosting; the model would still be operated by an external provider.
T: When the model must be self-hosted or self-licensed, register it through BYOM or a custom endpoint rather than bypassing the platform.

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.3 applied use-case
S: An operations lead wants a weekly view of an AI workflow's health: request volume, failure rate, average and p95 latency, token usage and cost, broken down by model and by workflow version. Where does this come from?
A: From the function's own logging statements, which the engineer adds to print volume and timing into a text log that is summarized by hand each week
B: From AIP Observability and execution history, which record each run's status, duration, tokens and model for aggregation
C: From the evaluation suite, which is run weekly and provides the accuracy and latency figures that the operations lead needs
D: From the model provider's monthly invoice, which lists token usage and cost per model for the whole organization
K: B
G: ## Operational telemetry
The exam guide expects AI systems to be observable: traces, execution history, logs, token telemetry and cost signals. Each run of a function or agent is recorded with its outcome, duration, token counts, model and version, which is exactly the raw material for the weekly view.
## Building the view
- Aggregate execution records by week, model and workflow version.
- Failure rate from run status; p95 latency from durations; cost from tokens × price per model.
- Trend the numbers so a regression after a version change is visible.
Example: the view shows version 9 raised p95 latency by 40% because its average context grew; the lead asks for the context to be reduced.
W: Observability and execution history: they already capture per-run status, latency, tokens and model, so the metrics are aggregated rather than invented.
X: A=Hand-written logs: reimplementing telemetry in print statements is fragile and incomplete, and manual weekly summaries do not scale.
C=Evaluation suite: a suite measures quality on fixed cases, not production volume, failure rates or real latency distributions.
D=Provider invoice: it lacks per-workflow, per-version and per-run detail and arrives monthly, too coarse and too late for operations.
T: Operational health metrics come from execution history and observability telemetry, aggregated by model and version.
V: AIP Observability

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.4 advanced tradeoff
S: An interactive agent runs a 20-second analysis of a document before replying. Users find the wait unacceptable and often abandon the session. The analysis itself cannot be made much faster. What should the engineer change?
A: Move the analysis to an asynchronous flow that shows progress and notifies the user when the result is ready, keeping the session responsive
B: Replace the analysis with a quick summary from a smaller model, accepting lower quality so the reply arrives within a few seconds
C: Show a message asking users to wait, since the analysis is necessary and users will accept the delay once they understand why it happens
D: Pre-run the analysis on every document in the system nightly, so that whichever document a user asks about has already been analysed
K: A
G: ## Latency and user experience
The exam guide's decision area on latency, cost and user experience says to use asynchronous execution for long work and to check timeouts and progress visibility. Some tasks are legitimately long; the design question is whether the user has to stare at a spinner.
## Asynchronous pattern
- Acknowledge the request immediately and start the analysis in the background.
- Show progress or an estimate; let the user keep working or ask other questions.
- Deliver the result when ready (notification, updated object, message in the session).
- Keep timeouts and failure handling explicit so an abandoned analysis is visible.
Example: "I'm analysing the contract; this takes about half a minute. I'll post the summary here when it's done." The session stays usable and abandonment drops.
W: Asynchronous execution with visible progress: the long analysis keeps its quality and the interactive experience stays responsive, which is the guide's prescribed pattern for long work.
X: B=Smaller, faster model: trading away quality on an analysis users depend on is a last resort, and a quick summary may not be the analysis they need.
C=Ask users to wait: the abandonment data already shows that explanation does not make a 20-second blocking wait acceptable.
D=Pre-analyse everything: running the analysis on every document nightly wastes compute on documents nobody asks about, and results go stale.
T: Long AI work should run asynchronously with progress and notification rather than blocking an interactive session.

@ PLTR-AIE D7 T7.1 applied troubleshooting
S: An engineer swapped a workflow's model for a newer one with a higher capability tier. The evaluation suite improved slightly, but production latency doubled and the monthly cost quadrupled. The workflow is a simple field extraction. What should happen?
A: Keep the newer model, since any accuracy improvement on an extraction task is worth the additional latency and cost
B: Return to the smaller model unless the measured accuracy gain is required by the task, since the costs and latency are real
C: Keep the newer model but reduce the evaluation suite to the cases where it performs best, so the improvement is more visible in reports
D: Use the newer model only during business hours when accuracy matters more, and the smaller model overnight when cost matters more
K: B
G: ## Right-sizing after the fact
Model selection is a trade-off among capability, latency, cost and the task's needs. A slight accuracy gain on a simple extraction, at double latency and four times the cost, is usually a bad trade unless the task's requirement was not being met before. The exam guide asks for a targeted optimization rather than simply choosing a larger model.
## How to decide
- State the accuracy requirement; compare both models against it on the suite.
- Quantify the gain (how many fields per thousand) versus the cost and latency change.
- If the smaller model meets the requirement, use it; if specific hard cases need the larger one, route only those cases to it.
Example: 99.1% versus 99.4% on fields, at four times the cost: the smaller model meets the 99% requirement and stays.
W: Revert unless the gain is required: cost and latency are measured costs, and the extraction task's requirement decides whether the small improvement justifies them.
X: A=Any gain is worth it: this ignores the stated constraints; a workflow is operated within latency and budget limits, not optimized for accuracy alone.
C=Trim the suite: shrinking the evaluation to flatter the model destroys the suite's value and hides regressions elsewhere.
D=Time-based switching: accuracy requirements do not change by hour, and running two models adds complexity with no principled basis.
T: A larger model is justified only when the task's requirement needs the gain; otherwise the latency and cost are waste.
