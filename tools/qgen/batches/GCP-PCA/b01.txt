@ GCP-PCA D1 T1.1 applied definition
S: An architect is turning a retailer's workshop notes into a requirements document. Which TWO of the following statements are non-functional requirements? (Select TWO)
A: Checkout pages must return within a stated response time at the 95th percentile during seasonal promotions
B: Customers must be able to save several delivery addresses in their account profile
C: The order service must continue to accept orders when one zone of the serving region is unavailable
D: The warehouse team must be able to export the daily picking list as a spreadsheet file
E: Support agents must be able to refund a single line item from an order they are viewing
K: A,C
G: ## Functional versus non-functional requirements
A **functional requirement** states what the system does: a feature, a business rule or a behaviour a user can name ("save an address", "refund a line item", "export a list").
A **non-functional requirement** (NFR) states how well the system does it, under which constraints: performance, availability, scalability, security, compliance, cost and operability. NFRs are what drive most architecture choices, because they decide regions, redundancy, data stores and spend.
## Why the split matters to an architect
- Functional items are usually satisfied by application logic and can be built on almost any platform.
- NFRs must be made measurable (percentile latency, availability target, recovery time) before they can be designed for and tested.
- A requirement that names a quantity or a failure condition is the signal that it is an NFR.
W: A=Latency at the 95th percentile: a stated response time under peak load is a performance NFR. It is measurable and it drives caching, scaling and data-store choices.
C=Survive loss of a zone: continuing to accept orders during a zone failure is an availability NFR. It drives multi-zone deployment of the serving tier and its data store.
X: B=Saving several addresses: this is a feature of the account profile, which is a functional requirement about what a customer can do.
D=Exporting a picking list: producing a file for the warehouse team is a capability the system offers, so it is functional.
E=Refunding a line item: this is a business action an agent performs, so it is functional.
T: Features are functional; measurable qualities under load or failure (latency, availability, scale, security, cost) are non-functional and shape the architecture.

@ GCP-PCA D1 T1.1 advanced use-case
S: A parcel-tracking company runs a customer portal, a driver dispatch system and an internal HR portal. The board asks for a business continuity plan that is affordable. A workshop shows that a dispatch outage stops revenue within minutes, the customer portal can tolerate a few hours of downtime, and the HR portal can be unavailable for days. What should the architect do first when shaping the continuity design?
A: Run a business impact analysis, then assign each system its own recovery time and recovery point targets and size the redundancy to each tier
B: Deploy every system with the strongest multi-region active-active design, so the same continuity target applies to all workloads without exception
C: Defer all continuity design until after migration, then apply the cheapest backup schedule to every workload to keep the budget simple
D: Ask each application team to choose its own continuity design independently, then merge the results without comparing them to business impact
K: A
G: ## Business continuity planning (BCP) in an architecture
BCP asks what the business must keep running, for how long it can be without it, and how much data it can afford to lose. The tools are:
- **Business impact analysis (BIA)**: ranks processes by the cost of interruption.
- **Recovery time objective (RTO)**: how long a process may be down.
- **Recovery point objective (RPO)**: how much recent data may be lost.
## Tiering is a cost decision
Redundancy costs money in infrastructure and operations. A single strategy for everything either overspends on low-value systems or underprotects the critical one. The architect therefore tiers workloads by impact and buys only the protection each tier needs.
## Applying it here
Dispatch has the tightest RTO and RPO, the customer portal a medium tier, and the HR portal a relaxed tier. The detailed fail-over patterns come after these targets are agreed.
W: Business impact analysis with per-system targets: it turns the workshop findings into tiered RTO and RPO values, so spending on redundancy follows revenue risk.
X: B=Strongest design for all: multi-region active-active everywhere would over-protect the HR portal and the portal at a cost the board asked to avoid; it ignores the observed differences in impact.
C=Defer and use cheapest backups: a uniform backup schedule would leave dispatch exposed, and continuity requirements shape the design, so they cannot wait until after migration.
D=Independent team choices: without a shared impact analysis, teams pick inconsistent targets and may protect low-value systems more than critical ones.
T: Start continuity design with a business impact analysis, then set RTO and RPO per tier so protection and cost match business risk.

@ GCP-PCA D1 T1.1 applied tradeoff
S: Altostrat Media keeps a very large podcast and documentary library in Cloud Storage. A small share of titles is requested constantly, many are requested rarely, and nobody can predict which archive titles will suddenly trend. The business wants lower storage cost without losing high availability or adding manual lifecycle tuning. Which approach fits best?
A: Enable Autoclass on the buckets so objects move to colder classes when idle and return to Standard when they are read again
B: Write lifecycle rules that move every object to Coldline after a fixed age, whatever its read history, and review the rules each quarter
C: Move the whole archive to Archive storage, because it has the lowest storage rate and the library is mostly read rarely
D: Keep all objects in Standard storage and rely on Cloud CDN caching, since caching removes the need to choose any storage class
K: A
G: ## Storage cost follows access pattern
Cloud Storage classes trade storage price against retrieval cost and minimum storage duration: colder classes store cheaper, but reading or deleting early costs more. Choosing a class correctly needs knowledge of how often objects are read.
## Autoclass
**Autoclass** moves each object by its own access pattern: untouched data moves to colder classes, and data that is read moves back to Standard. Per the documentation, retrieval fees and early deletion fees are not charged on these managed transitions, and a management fee applies. That removes the risk of paying retrieval penalties when an old title suddenly trends.
## Reading the scenario
Unpredictable access plus a wish for no manual tuning is the textbook fit. Age-based lifecycle rules suit data whose access really does fall with age.
W: Autoclass: it adapts per object to real access, brings trending titles back to Standard automatically, and avoids the retrieval and early deletion fees that a fixed rule could trigger.
X: B=Fixed-age Coldline rule: an age rule cannot see that an old title became popular, so reads would pay Coldline retrieval and could incur early-deletion charges, and it needs the quarterly manual tuning the business wants to avoid.
C=Everything in Archive: archived objects that are read often incur retrieval costs and slower, costlier access than the viewers expect; it optimises only the rarely read part of the library.
D=Standard plus CDN: caching helps delivery latency for popular items but cold titles still sit in the most expensive class, so storage cost is not reduced.
T: Unpredictable object access with no appetite for manual tuning points to Autoclass; age-based lifecycle rules fit data whose access reliably declines with age.
V: Cloud Storage

@ GCP-PCA D1 T1.1 applied comparison
S: A SaaS company will run about the same number of vCPUs and amount of memory in one region on Compute Engine for the next three years. Another team's workloads, on a different machine series and in other regions, change every few months. The finance lead wants the biggest discount that does not risk paying for unused commitments. Which plan fits?
A: Buy resource-based commitments for the steady, region-specific footprint, and consider a spend-based flexible commitment only for a modest part of the variable usage
B: Buy one large spend-based commitment sized to the peak of both teams, because a spend commitment is always cheaper than a resource commitment
C: Buy three-year resource commitments for the variable team's current machine series in every region, so any later change is covered automatically
D: Buy no commitments and rely on on-demand rates for both teams, since commitments cannot be matched to a changing set of workloads
K: A
G: ## Two kinds of committed use discounts (CUDs)
- **Resource-based CUDs**: commit to a minimum amount of resources (vCPUs, memory, block storage) in one region for one or three years. Best for predictable, steady-state usage.
- **Compute flexible CUDs**: spend-based. Commit to an hourly spend that applies across eligible services, giving room to change machine series or regions.
## The trade-off
Every commitment is paid whether or not it is used, so the aim is to commit to what is certain. A larger discount from an exact-match resource commitment must be weighed against the risk of the footprint changing. Flexibility costs a smaller discount but reduces waste when workloads move.
## Reading the scenario
The steady footprint fits resource-based commitments; the shifting one is where flexibility, sized conservatively, belongs.
W: Resource commitments for the steady part, flexible for part of the rest: each team's discount is matched to how predictable its usage is, limiting waste from unused commitments.
X: B=One large spend commitment at peak: committing at peak means paying for the unused part whenever usage falls, and the claim that spend commitments are always cheaper is wrong; they trade discount depth for flexibility.
C=Resource commitments for the shifting team: resource commitments are tied to a region and a resource amount, so changes to machine series or region leave commitments underused.
D=No commitments: the steady workload is ideal for a commitment, and the documentation offers a flexible type precisely for changing workloads, so abstaining forgoes available savings.
T: Commit to what is predictable: resource-based commitments for steady footprints, spend-based flexible commitments (kept conservative) for changing ones.
V: Compute Engine

@ GCP-PCA D1 T1.1 applied tradeoff
S: A company moving to Google Cloud has three product teams that share one billing account. Executives want each team to see its own spend, receive warnings before a monthly limit is exceeded, and be able to trigger automated responses to a runaway cost. Which TWO actions support this? (Select TWO)
A: Create Cloud Billing budgets per team with alert thresholds, and use the budget notifications to start automated responses
B: Apply consistent labels or separate projects per team so billing data can be broken down by team and exported for analysis
C: Ask each team to read the monthly invoice and report increases manually, which avoids any tooling
D: Move every team into one project so that only a single budget needs to be maintained
E: Disable billing on the shared account whenever a team approaches its target so spend stops immediately
K: A,B
G: ## Cost visibility and control (FinOps basics)
Well-Architected cost optimization asks for accountability and continuous monitoring. In Google Cloud that means:
- **Attribution**: spend must be traceable to an owner. Separate projects per team, or consistent labels, let billing reports and exports split costs by team.
- **Budgets and alerts**: Cloud Billing budgets track actual or forecast spend against a plan and send threshold notifications, which can also drive automated responses.
## Limits of the tools
An alerts-only budget does not stop spending by itself; automation must be built from the notifications. Disabling billing is a blunt reaction that can take down production.
W: A=Budgets with alerts: per-team budgets give early warnings at thresholds, and notifications can trigger automated cost controls such as notifying owners or scaling a non-production workload down.
B=Labels or projects: consistent labels or separate projects make costs attributable to each team in billing reports and exports, which is the basis of showback.
X: C=Manual invoice reading: it is slow, detects increases after the month closes, and gives no threshold warning or automation.
D=One shared project: merging the teams destroys the boundary used for attribution and access control, and a single budget cannot show who spent what.
E=Disabling billing: it would stop running services, including customer-facing ones, so it is not a controlled response and is an availability risk.
T: Cost accountability needs attribution (projects or labels) plus budgets and alerts that can drive automation.

@ GCP-PCA D1 T1.1 advanced use-case
S: Cymbal Retail exchanges orders with suppliers through nightly SFTP file drops and batch ETL, and a failure in one step silently delays everything after it. The business wants new gen AI catalog enrichment and order tracking to react as events happen, and wants to add consumers later without changing producers. Which integration pattern best meets this?
A: Publish business events to Pub/Sub topics so producers and consumers are decoupled, and let each consumer subscribe independently
B: Keep the nightly files but make every consumer poll the SFTP server every minute to reduce the delay between steps
C: Have each producing application call every consuming application's API synchronously and wait for each reply
D: Load all data into one shared relational database and let each application read the tables it needs at any time
K: A
G: ## Integration patterns
- **Batch file transfer**: simple, but latency is hours and failures are discovered late.
- **Synchronous request/response (RPC)**: immediate, but the caller depends on every callee being up and fast.
- **Event-driven messaging**: producers publish events and do not know who reads them. Consumers subscribe, process at their own pace, and new consumers can be added with no producer change.
## Pub/Sub
Pub/Sub is an asynchronous, scalable messaging service that decouples the services that produce messages from those that process them, with latency typically around 100 ms. Publishers send events without regard to how or when they are processed, which increases the flexibility and robustness of the system.
## Applying it
Real-time reaction plus the ability to add consumers later is exactly decoupling. A shared database couples everyone to one schema.
W: Pub/Sub events: producers publish once, any number of consumers subscribe independently, and the asynchronous design removes the nightly batch delay and the single step failure that blocks everything.
X: B=Frequent SFTP polling: it shortens the delay but keeps file-based coupling, adds load, and still ties producers to a file format and consumers to a server.
C=Synchronous API calls to everyone: the producer becomes dependent on each consumer's availability, and each new consumer needs a producer change, the opposite of the stated goal.
D=Shared database: applications become coupled through one schema, a change can break many readers, and polling tables is not an event mechanism.
T: To react to events and add consumers without touching producers, use asynchronous publish/subscribe messaging instead of batch files or point-to-point calls.
V: Pub/Sub

@ GCP-PCA D1 T1.1 applied comparison
S: A logistics firm runs an on-premises PostgreSQL database for shipment tracking. Analysts need the data in BigQuery within seconds of each change, the source must keep serving the live application, and the team wants no servers of its own to run for the replication. Which approach should the architect choose?
A: Use Datastream for change data capture from the database into BigQuery, with private connectivity over VPN or Interconnect
B: Schedule a nightly full export of every table to files and load them to BigQuery with a batch job
C: Rewrite the application so that it writes every record to BigQuery and the database at the same time
D: Run a self-managed replication tool on Compute Engine virtual machines that the team patches and scales itself
K: A
G: ## Moving data: batch, streaming or CDC
- **Bulk transfer or export/import** copies whole datasets at a point in time. It is good for one-time loads and historical data, but the target is as old as the last run.
- **Change data capture (CDC)** reads the database's change log and sends only inserts, updates and deletes, so the target follows the source with low latency and little extra load.
## Datastream
Datastream is a serverless CDC and replication service for heterogeneous databases with minimal latency. It supports sources such as MySQL, Oracle, PostgreSQL, SQL Server and MongoDB, replicates into BigQuery for near real-time analytics, scales automatically, and can reach sources privately over VPN or Interconnect.
W: Datastream CDC to BigQuery: it satisfies the seconds-level freshness, reads changes without reloading tables, avoids server management because it is serverless, and reaches the on-premises source over a private link.
X: B=Nightly exports: the data would be up to a day old, and repeated full exports put heavy load on the live database.
C=Dual writes from the application: this needs code changes, risks the two stores diverging when one write fails, and does not address historical data.
D=Self-managed replication on VMs: it can work technically, but the team would patch, scale and monitor it, which the requirement excluded.
T: Low-latency database-to-warehouse replication without servers to run is change data capture with a managed service such as Datastream.
V: Datastream, BigQuery

@ GCP-PCA D1 T1.1 applied troubleshooting
S: A broadcaster must copy hundreds of terabytes of archived video from its on-premises NAS to Cloud Storage over an existing 1 Gbps link that also carries daily business traffic. Past ad hoc copies using scripts stalled and were restarted by hand. Leadership wants a reliable, scheduled, verifiable transfer that can be throttled during working hours. Which solution fits?
A: Storage Transfer Service with agents on-premises, which schedules transfers, retries failures, verifies integrity and can limit bandwidth
B: A single long-running copy script on one workstation that is restarted by an operator after every interruption
C: Streaming each file through Pub/Sub messages so the broker handles retry and bandwidth sharing for the copy
D: Mounting the Cloud Storage bucket on each employee's laptop so editors can drag and drop the video files directly
K: A
G: ## Choosing a data movement tool
Move data with a managed transfer tool when the volume is large, the network is shared, and the transfer must be repeatable and auditable.
## Storage Transfer Service
Storage Transfer Service moves data at scale from on-premises storage, Amazon S3, Azure Blob Storage, HTTP/HTTPS URLs or HDFS to Cloud Storage. With self-hosted agents the customer keeps granular control of bandwidth and network routing, so transfers can be scheduled and throttled. It also covers archival: moving cold data from costly on-premises storage to Cloud Storage.
## When bandwidth is the limit
If the link cannot move the volume in an acceptable time, offline options exist. Here the link is adequate; the problem is reliability and shared use.
W: Storage Transfer Service with agents: it is purpose-built for large on-premises transfers, with scheduling, retries, integrity checks and bandwidth control, which solves stalls and impact on business traffic.
X: B=Single script on a workstation: it is the failing approach already tried; it has no managed retry, scheduling or verification, and depends on an operator.
C=Pub/Sub for file copies: Pub/Sub is a messaging service for events, not a bulk file transfer tool, and does not manage bandwidth for terabyte-scale objects.
D=Bucket mounted on laptops: it creates a per-user, uncontrolled upload path through the same shared link, with no scheduling or verification.
T: Large, repeatable, throttled transfers from on-premises to Cloud Storage use a managed transfer service rather than scripts or messaging.
V: Storage Transfer Service, Cloud Storage

@ GCP-PCA D1 T1.1 applied tradeoff
S: KnightMotives wants a CRM and dealer toolset covering sales, service and inventory. The company's in-house teams are stretched by fragmented legacy code and technical debt, dealers have no budget for new equipment, and the business wants value within months. Which disposition best balances time to value and risk?
A: Buy a commercial dealer-management and CRM product that runs in the browser, and integrate it with existing data through APIs
B: Build a custom CRM from scratch with the in-house teams, so every feature can match current internal processes
C: Modify the legacy dealer application in place by adding new modules to its existing code base and servers
D: Deprecate the dealer tooling without replacement, because dealers can use spreadsheets until the new data platform is complete
K: A
G: ## Disposition: build, buy, modify, deprecate
For each capability an architect compares four moves:
- **Buy** (SaaS or commercial off-the-shelf): fastest, but you adapt to the product and depend on the vendor.
- **Build**: full fit, highest cost, longest delay and an ongoing maintenance burden.
- **Modify** (extend or re-platform an existing system): keeps familiar behaviour but carries forward its technical debt.
- **Deprecate**: stop offering the capability when it no longer earns its cost.
## Decision drivers
Time to value, team capacity, differentiation (does the capability make the company different?), integration needs and risk.
## Applying it
Dealer CRM is a commodity capability, the teams are over-committed, there is a debt-laden code base, and dealers need zero hardware spend. A browser-based product fits all four, with integration work as the main cost.
W: Buy a browser-based product: it delivers quickly, needs no dealer equipment, spares stretched teams a long build, and APIs connect it to the data platform.
X: B=Build from scratch: it maximises fit but is slowest and costliest, and adds to a team that is already overloaded, for a capability that does not differentiate the company.
C=Modify the legacy application: it extends the technical debt and old servers, and the dealer rollout would remain dependent on the fragmented code base.
D=Deprecate without replacement: dealers still need sales, service and inventory support; removing it would hurt the build-to-order experience the business wants to improve.
T: For commodity capabilities with limited team capacity and a need for speed, buy; build only where the capability differentiates the business.

@ GCP-PCA D1 T1.1 applied definition
S: EHR Healthcare is moving customer-facing systems to Google Cloud and promised hospitals at least 99.9% availability. Management also wants proof that the move is paying off after one year. Which set of key performance indicators best fits these goals?
A: Measured monthly availability of customer-facing services against the 99.9% target, plus unit cost per active practice compared with the colocation baseline
B: Total number of virtual machines created, number of projects in the organization and number of engineers who completed training
C: Count of alert emails sent per month and the number of dashboards created in the monitoring tool
D: Total count of services migrated, regardless of their availability, cost or the number of incidents after migration
K: A
G: ## KPIs and ROI
A **KPI** is a measurable indicator tied to a business goal. A good one has a baseline, a target and an owner. An architect separates:
- **Outcome metrics** (availability, cost per unit, onboarding time), which show whether the goal was achieved.
- **Activity metrics** (resources created, tasks completed), which show effort, not result.
## ROI and TCO
Return on investment compares benefit to cost. For a cloud move, compare the total cost of the old estate (leases, hardware, staff time) against the new run cost, and measure service quality alongside, because savings that cut reliability are not a return.
## Applying it
Two goals were stated: availability and proof of payoff. Availability measured against its target, and cost per practice against the colocation baseline, map directly to them.
W: Availability against target plus unit cost: both are outcomes tied to the promise to hospitals and the payoff question, and each has a baseline to compare with.
X: B=Resources, projects and training counts: these measure activity and growth in footprint, which can rise while customers are worse off and costs are higher.
C=Alert emails and dashboards: more alerts or dashboards says nothing about customer experience, and EHR already had ignored email alerts, so volume is a poor indicator.
D=Services migrated: progress through a plan is useful to a project manager, but without availability and cost it cannot show the move is paying off.
T: Choose outcome KPIs with a baseline and target that mirror the business promise (availability, unit cost), not counts of activity.

@ GCP-PCA D1 T1.1 advanced tradeoff
S: In a requirements workshop, a media start-up's product owner asks for "the lowest possible latency worldwide, the highest availability, and the lowest possible cost". The architect knows these goals pull against each other. What is the best next step?
A: Quantify each goal with the business, such as a latency target at a percentile, an availability target and a monthly cost ceiling, and agree which has priority when they conflict
B: Choose the highest availability design with global multi-region replication, since cost can always be reduced later without redesigning
C: Choose the lowest cost design, because availability and latency can be improved after launch simply by adding more servers
D: Record all three goals as written and let the engineering team decide the trade-offs during implementation without further review
K: A
G: ## Turning wishes into design inputs
Words such as "lowest" and "highest" are not requirements: with no target, every extra investment looks justified, and any design can be called a failure. An architect converts each into a measurable target and a priority.
## Conflicting qualities
- Higher availability and lower latency usually mean more regions, replicas and capacity, which cost more.
- Lower cost often means fewer replicas, colder storage or fewer regions.
- The right balance depends on what the business loses from a slow page, an outage or an overrun.
## Process
Quantify (percentile latency, availability %, cost ceiling), rank them, and record the agreement. The ranking then decides which design wins in a conflict.
W: Quantify and prioritise with the business: measurable targets and an agreed order of importance allow a defensible design, and prevent over- or under-building.
X: B=Maximum availability first: global replication is a costly structural choice that cannot simply be undone later, and it may exceed what the business values.
C=Lowest cost first: availability and latency often depend on region and data design that cannot be fixed afterwards just by adding servers.
D=Leave trade-offs to engineers: the trade-off is a business judgement about value and risk; engineers would choose without knowing priorities.
T: Convert vague "best" goals into measurable targets with an agreed priority order before designing.

@ GCP-PCA D1 T1.1 applied comparison
S: A manufacturer compares keeping its data center against moving a steady ERP-reporting workload to Google Cloud. The CFO asks for a fair business case. Which analysis is most defensible?
A: Compare the full multi-year cost of both options, including hardware refresh, facility, power and operations staff on-premises, and cloud run costs with the effort of migrating
B: Compare the monthly cloud bill to the depreciation of servers alone, and ignore facilities, staffing and migration effort
C: Compare only the list price of equivalent virtual machines to the purchase price of servers, and ignore all other items
D: Treat the on-premises hardware as free since it is already purchased, and compare only the cloud bill with zero
K: A
G: ## Total cost of ownership (TCO)
The Well-Architected cost pillar notes that on-premises cost is CapEx (assets bought and depreciated) plus OpEx, while most cloud cost is OpEx incurred as resources are consumed. A fair comparison therefore covers everything each option costs over the same period.
## What to include
- On-premises: hardware refresh, software, facility, power and cooling, network, staff time.
- Cloud: consumption, support, networking, tooling.
- One-time: migration effort, training, parallel running.
Non-financial benefits (agility, resilience) are listed separately, not hidden.
## Pitfall
Sunk costs and partial lists make either option look cheap. The decision looks forward at what each path will cost from now.
W: Full multi-year cost of both options with migration effort: this treats CapEx and OpEx on a common basis, so the real savings or extra cost become visible to the CFO.
X: B=Depreciation only: it leaves out facilities and staff that disappear or shrink on the cloud path, and migration effort that appears on it, so the result is distorted.
C=List prices only: the purchase price of servers is a fraction of their cost to run, and the comparison ignores operations and discounts.
D=Hardware as free: money already spent is sunk, but a refresh, support contracts, power and staff continue to cost on-premises; treating them as zero favours staying put.
T: A business case compares complete multi-year costs and migration effort for both paths, on the same basis, with non-financial benefits shown separately.

@ GCP-PCA D1 T1.5 advanced use-case
S: KnightMotives plans to replace its mainframe supply-chain and ERP system gradually, because a big-bang cutover could halt plants and dealers. Which approach to the future improvement of this system reduces risk the most?
A: Put an API layer in front of the legacy system, move one business capability at a time to new services behind it, and retire legacy parts as traffic moves
B: Freeze all legacy changes for several years while a complete replacement is built, then switch every plant on one weekend
C: Copy the mainframe to virtual machines unchanged and declare the modernization finished, since the hardware is then gone
D: Let each plant choose and build its own replacement on its own timeline, so no central design is needed
K: A
G: ## Incremental modernization (the strangler pattern)
Replacing a core system all at once concentrates risk: every capability must work on day one. The **strangler pattern** instead places a facade (APIs or a proxy) in front of the legacy system, then rebuilds one capability at a time as a new service. The facade routes traffic to the new or old implementation, and old parts are switched off when they carry no traffic.
## Benefits
- Each step is small, testable and reversible.
- Business value arrives early, and lessons shape later steps.
- Hybrid operation during the transition is expected, which matches a hybrid cloud strategy.
## Contrast
Lift-and-shift of the same code is a rehost: it removes the hardware but not the technical debt or the design limits.
W: Facade and capability-by-capability replacement: it limits the blast radius of each change, allows rollback and keeps plants and dealers running while the legacy system shrinks.
X: B=Freeze and big-bang cutover: it delays value for years, concentrates risk in a single weekend, and the requirements will have changed by the time it ships.
C=Rehost unchanged on VMs: it moves the problem without removing technical debt or enabling the modern integrations and AI use cases the business wants.
D=Each plant on its own: independent builds fragment the code and data again, which is the problem the company wants to escape, and prevent a consistent supply chain.
T: Reduce the risk of replacing a core system by strangling it: facade first, then migrate one capability at a time.

@ GCP-PCA D1 T1.5 applied tradeoff
S: Cymbal Retail's pilot for gen AI product descriptions produced good drafts, and the merchandising lead wants to extend it to the whole catalog next month. Wrong descriptions would reach customers directly, and compliance reviewers have not seen the process. What should the architect recommend for the next phase?
A: Expand in stages while keeping a human review step before catalog updates, measure accuracy and rejection rates, and widen scope as targets are met
B: Publish every generated description automatically for the entire catalog, then correct complaints as customers report them
C: Stop the program until models never produce an error, since any human review proves that the approach is unsuitable
D: Replace the pilot with a hand-written description process for every product, to avoid any new risk from automation
K: A
G: ## Planning future improvements with evidence
When a pilot succeeds, the next step is a staged rollout with measurable gates, not a leap to full automation. Generative AI output is probabilistic: it can be fluent and wrong, so quality controls scale with the impact of a mistake.
## Elements of a staged plan
- **Human in the loop**: reviewers approve changes before they reach customers (the case calls for a review interface before catalog updates).
- **Metrics**: accuracy against supplier data, rejection rate, reviewer time per item.
- **Gates**: widen categories only when metrics meet the target; reduce review for low-risk attributes later.
- **Compliance involvement**: reviewers see the process early.
## Result
The benefit (lower manual effort) grows with scope, while risk stays bounded.
W: Staged expansion with human review and metrics: it delivers the efficiency gain progressively while protecting customers and giving compliance evidence to approve each step.
X: B=Automatic publish for all items: errors reach customers at scale before anyone notices, and complaints are an expensive, late detection mechanism.
C=Wait for error-free models: no model is guaranteed error-free; human review is a normal control, not proof of unsuitability, so waiting forgoes value indefinitely.
D=Return to hand-written descriptions: it discards a validated gain and keeps the manual, error-prone process the business wants to remove.
