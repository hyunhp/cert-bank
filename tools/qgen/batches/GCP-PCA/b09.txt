@ GCP-PCA D4 T4.2 applied tradeoff
S: A manufacturing company is closing two data centers and moving its workloads to Google Cloud. The finance director welcomes the end of large hardware purchases but worries that monthly bills will now vary and could surprise the business. As the architect, which advice best addresses both the benefit and the concern?
A: Explain that spending shifts from capital purchases to operating expenses that follow usage, and set up budgets with alerts, resource labels and committed use discounts for the steady baseline
B: Explain that cloud costs are fixed once a project is created, so the monthly bill will always match the first estimate and no monitoring or tagging is needed afterwards
C: Recommend buying the maximum expected capacity up front for three years on every service, so the bill stays predictable but the company keeps capital-style commitments
D: Recommend moving only the cheapest workloads at first and delaying any cost reporting until the migration is complete and the usage has stabilized
K: A
G: ## CapEx versus OpEx in the cloud
On premises, hardware is a capital expense (CapEx): money is spent up front for capacity that must cover the peak, and it depreciates over years. In Google Cloud most services are an operating expense (OpEx): you pay for what you use, when you use it, and you can add or remove capacity quickly. The cost optimization pillar of the Well-Architected Framework treats this as a change of discipline, not just of accounting.
## Keeping variable spend under control
- **Budgets and alerts** tell the owners when spend crosses chosen thresholds.
- **Labels, folders and projects** let finance attribute spend to teams and products (showback or chargeback).
- **Committed use discounts (CUDs)** trade a one-year or three-year commitment for a lower rate on the steady part of the load. They exist as spend-based and resource-based commitments.
- Variable or spiky load stays on demand, so the company does not pay for idle peak capacity.
W: Naming the shift from CapEx to usage-based OpEx and pairing it with budgets and alerts, labels for attribution and CUDs for the predictable baseline answers both points: the director keeps the benefit (no up-front hardware) and gains visibility and a lower rate where demand is stable.
X: B=Fixed costs: cloud bills follow consumption, so they change when usage changes. Believing in a fixed bill removes the monitoring that prevents surprises.
C=Maximum capacity for three years: committing to peak capacity recreates the over-provisioning of a data center. CUDs fit the steady baseline only, not the peak.
D=Delay cost reporting: cost visibility is easiest to set up at the start, and delaying it is exactly how unexpected bills arise. Moving the cheapest workloads first is also unrelated to the stated concern.
T: Cloud moves spend from CapEx to usage-based OpEx; control it with budgets, labels and CUDs for the steady baseline.
V: Cloud Billing, committed use discounts

@ GCP-PCA D4 T4.2 applied use-case
S: A company's cloud bill is growing, and 40 teams share a few projects. Finance cannot tell which product or team causes the growth, and engineers suspect many oversized and idle VMs. Which TWO actions give finance and engineering the data to attribute and reduce the spend? (Select TWO)
A: Move all workloads into one large project so that a single total appears on the invoice
B: Apply consistent resource labels, one project per team or product, and export detailed billing data to BigQuery for reporting by team
C: Buy committed use discounts for the full current spend before analyzing which resources are in fact idle or oversized
D: Review the idle and rightsizing recommendations that Google Cloud generates from observed usage and resize or remove the flagged resources
E: Ask every team to estimate its own monthly usage in a spreadsheet and send it to finance at the end of each quarter
K: B,D
G: ## Two steps of cost optimization
Cost control starts with attribution (who spends what) and continues with removal of waste (what is not needed).
- **Attribution**: labels on resources, a project or folder structure that matches teams and products, and an export of detailed billing data to BigQuery. Finance can then group, filter and chart cost by label or project with SQL or a dashboard.
- **Waste removal**: the Recommender service analyses utilization and suggests actions such as resizing over-provisioned VMs or deleting idle resources. Acting on these is the fastest saving that needs no commitment.
- **Commitments** (CUDs) come after this: you commit only for load you know is needed, not for the waste.
W: B=Labels, projects and billing export: they make spend attributable to teams and products, which is the missing data.
D=Rightsizing and idle recommendations: they use measured utilization to identify oversized or idle VMs, so engineering can cut cost without changing the architecture.
X: A=One large project: a single total hides who spends what and weakens the separation of access and quotas. It removes the attribution that is missing.
C=Commit first: a commitment on the current spend locks in a discount on resources that may be waste, so the company pays for idle capacity for one or three years.
E=Self-reported spreadsheets: estimates are late, manual and unverified, whereas billing data is exact and available continuously.
T: Attribute with labels, project structure and billing export, then cut waste with recommendations, and only then commit with CUDs.
V: Cloud Billing, BigQuery, Recommender

@ GCP-PCA D4 T4.2 applied use-case
S: EHR Healthcare's customer-facing systems must reach at least 99.9% availability, and the company is about to choose disaster recovery designs for dozens of services. The architect wants the business to agree on what each design must achieve before any design is priced. What should be done first?
A: Choose multi-region active-active deployments for all services, since that gives the highest availability and the business can accept the cost
B: Copy the disaster recovery approach of the largest competitor, because that is a proven design for electronic health record software
C: Run a business impact analysis that ranks each service by the harm of an outage and assigns recovery time and recovery point objectives per service tier
D: Ask each engineering team to describe its current backup schedule and treat the existing schedule as the recovery requirement
K: C
G: ## Business continuity starts with business impact
Business continuity planning keeps critical functions running through a disruption. The reliability pillar of the Well-Architected Framework asks you to set recovery objectives from business need before choosing a technology.
- **RTO** (recovery time objective): how long a service may be down.
- **RPO** (recovery point objective): how much data, measured in time, may be lost.
- A **business impact analysis** ranks services by the cost of an outage (patient care, contracts, regulation) so that objectives differ by tier.
Tight objectives need more redundancy and cost more. A claims-reporting batch job can accept hours of downtime; a clinical record lookup cannot.
W: A business impact analysis with tiered RTO and RPO turns the 99.9% target and clinical risk into per-service requirements, so each design can then be priced and justified against a stated need.
X: A=Active-active everywhere: it is the most expensive pattern and over-serves low-criticality services. The cost is justified only for tiers whose objectives require it.
B=Copying a competitor: another company's design reflects its own requirements and risk. It is not evidence of what this business needs.
D=Existing backup schedules: current practice is a result of past decisions, not a requirement. Treating it as the objective repeats gaps such as backups that cannot meet a needed RPO.
T: Define business impact and per-tier RTO/RPO first; choose and price the continuity design afterwards.

@ GCP-PCA D4 T4.2 applied troubleshooting
S: A retailer wrote a business continuity plan that says "restore the order system in another region within one hour". During a real regional outage the restore took six hours, because nobody had practised the steps and a configuration secret existed only in the failed region. How should the plan have been validated?
A: Review the written plan once a year in a meeting, since the document already states the one-hour objective clearly
B: Run scheduled recovery exercises in a test environment that fail over to the second region, record the actual recovery time, and fix every gap that the exercise exposes
C: Increase the size of the backup storage, because a larger backup shortens the time needed to restore the system
D: Replace the plan with a statement that the order system is built on a managed service, so no recovery steps are needed
K: B
G: ## A plan that is not tested is a hypothesis
RTO and RPO are targets. The only evidence that a design meets them is a measured recovery. Typical gaps found only by exercises are missing dependencies (secrets, DNS, IAM bindings or images that exist in one region only), undocumented manual steps, and stale runbooks.
## Good practice
- Exercise failover regularly in a representative environment and record the measured RTO and RPO.
- Treat each gap as a defect: replicate secrets and configuration, automate steps with infrastructure as code, update runbooks.
- Repeat after significant changes.
W: Scheduled recovery exercises measure real recovery time and expose single-region dependencies such as the secret, so they are fixed before the next outage instead of during it.
X: A=Annual document review: reading a plan does not test it. This process produced the one-hour claim that failed in practice.
C=Bigger backup storage: capacity is not the failure. Restore time came from missing steps and a regional dependency, so more storage changes nothing.
D=Rely on managed services: managed services still have regional scope and customer-owned dependencies. Having no recovery steps removes accountability, not risk.
T: Validate continuity plans by exercising recovery and measuring the result; fix hidden regional dependencies found on the way.
V: Cloud Architecture Center reliability guidance

S: EHR Healthcare onboards insurers one after another and wants to show that the cloud move helps these customers succeed, not only that systems are running. Which set of measures best shows customer success?

Customer success means customers reach the outcome they bought the service for. Infrastructure metrics are inputs; the measures of success describe what the customer experiences.

- **Service level indicators and objectives (SLIs/SLOs)**: availability and latency of the interfaces the customer uses, measured against the agreed target.

C=Clusters and migrated percentage: migration progress is a project milestone. The insurers experience the service, not the migration plan.

@ GCP-PCA D4 T4.2 applied use-case
S: A software company is moving its customer billing system to Google Cloud over a weekend cutover. The business cannot accept a failed migration that leaves invoices unprocessed. Which plan best protects business continuity during the migration?
A: Switch all traffic at once during the weekend, and rely on the support team to fix any problems on Monday morning
B: Keep the old system intact and in sync, rehearse the cutover, define a go/no-go checkpoint and a tested rollback path, and retire the old system only after a stable period
C: Shut down the old system before the cutover starts so that no data can change while the data is copied
D: Migrate a copy of the data to the cloud first and delete the original data right away to save cost
K: B
G: ## Continuity during change
A migration is a planned disruption, so it needs its own continuity plan.
- **Rehearsal**: run the cutover in a staging environment and time every step.
- **Go/no-go checkpoint**: agreed criteria decide whether to proceed.
- **Rollback path**: the source stays intact and in sync so traffic can return quickly if validation fails.
- **Stabilization**: the old system is decommissioned only after the new one has run through a full business cycle (for example, a month-end billing run).
W: Keeping the source intact, rehearsing, checking criteria at a go/no-go point and testing rollback means a failure delays the migration instead of stopping invoicing, and the old system is removed only when the new one has proven itself.
X: A=Big-bang switch with Monday repair: it has no rollback and no rehearsal. Billing could be down until the problem is solved.
C=Shut down first: a long outage of billing is itself the business impact to avoid. Data sync tools allow a short final cutover window instead.
D=Delete the original: removing the source eliminates the rollback path and deletes the only validated copy if the copy was incomplete.
T: Plan migration continuity with rehearsal, a go/no-go checkpoint, a tested rollback and a delayed decommission of the source.

@ GCP-PCA D5 T5.1 applied comparison
S: A bank offers a payments API to partner fintechs. Each partner has a contract with a monthly request allowance. Separately, the operations team wants to protect the backend from sudden bursts of traffic from any single caller. Which TWO Apigee policies match these two needs? (Select TWO)
A: The Quota policy, which counts requests per app or developer over a period such as a month and enforces the contractual allowance
B: The Quota policy, set to a very short period, as the only mechanism both for contracts and for burst protection
C: The SpikeArrest policy, which smooths the rate of requests that reach the backend to protect it from sudden surges
D: The ResponseCache policy, which stores successful backend answers so the backend does not receive any requests
E: The SpikeArrest policy, configured with a monthly count so that it tracks each partner's contract
K: A,C
G: ## Two different limits
Apigee has two policies that both "limit requests" but for different reasons.
- **Quota** keeps a distributed counter of the requests a client app may send over an hour, day, week or month. It enforces business contracts or SLAs with developers and partners.
- **SpikeArrest** throttles the rate of requests processed by an API proxy and sent to the backend. It protects against performance lags and downtime caused by sudden traffic spikes, and it is an operational control rather than a contract control.
A production proxy for external partners often uses both: Quota for what a partner has bought, SpikeArrest for how fast calls arrive.
W: A=Quota policy: it counts requests per app over a long period, which fits a monthly contractual allowance.
C=SpikeArrest policy: it limits the rate at which requests reach the backend, which protects against bursts from any caller.
X: B=Short-period Quota only: Quota is for consumption limits over hours to months and keeps counters per client. It is not designed as the operational burst protection, and one tool cannot serve both needs.
D=ResponseCache: caching reduces repeated backend calls for identical requests, but payment requests are not cacheable and the cache does not enforce limits for callers.
E=SpikeArrest with monthly count: SpikeArrest limits a request rate, not a counted allowance over a month, so it cannot represent the contract.
T: Quota enforces partner contracts over time; SpikeArrest protects the backend from bursts; use both.
V: Apigee

@ GCP-PCA D5 T5.1 applied use-case
S: A public transport authority publishes a timetable API. The data changes every ten minutes, yet thousands of apps request the same routes, and the backend database is struggling at rush hour. The team wants to relieve the backend without changing its code and accepts answers that are a few minutes old. Which Apigee capability fits?
A: A SpikeArrest policy set to reject every second request, so that the number of backend calls is halved
B: A VerifyAPIKey policy, since checking keys removes the load that comes from valid callers
C: A ResponseCache policy that serves stored responses for identical requests between data refreshes
D: A Quota policy that limits each app to one request per day so that traffic stays low
K: C
G: ## Caching at the API layer
The ResponseCache policy caches data from a backend resource so that repeated requests to the same URI are answered from the cache without reaching the backend. It is most useful when the backend data is updated only periodically, for instance a report refreshed every ten minutes.
## The trade-off
Cached answers can be stale up to the cache lifetime. The time to live must be no longer than the staleness the business accepts. Personal or rapidly changing data is a poor fit.
W: ResponseCache with a lifetime matching the ten-minute refresh returns the same correct answer to thousands of identical requests and removes most backend load, with no backend code change.
X: A=Reject every second request: it breaks half the legitimate calls and does nothing to avoid repeated identical work.
B=VerifyAPIKey: it authenticates callers, which is a security function. Valid callers still send the same requests to the backend.
D=One request per day: it destroys the usefulness of the API for its users and is a contract limit, not a performance solution.
T: Periodically refreshed, widely shared data: cache at the API layer with ResponseCache and a lifetime that matches acceptable staleness.
V: Apigee

@ GCP-PCA D5 T5.1 applied use-case
S: A hospital group runs a self-managed MySQL database on premises with an order-entry application. It will move the database to Cloud SQL for MySQL, and the application can tolerate only a few minutes of downtime. Which approach should the architect advise?

T: Low-downtime database migration: DMS initial load plus continuous replication, then promote at a short cutover.

@ GCP-PCA D5 T5.1 applied use-case
S: KnightMotives has several hundred terabytes of test-drive sensor recordings at a rural proving ground whose network link is slow and shared with plant operations. The company wants the data in Cloud Storage within weeks for analytics. Which method fits best?
A: Transfer Appliance, filled on site and shipped to a Google upload facility, which loads the data into the Cloud Storage bucket
B: A single script on a laptop that copies the files over the existing link and is left running until it finishes
C: Storage Transfer Service agents saturating the shared link around the clock, which would disrupt plant operations
D: Streaming the recordings into Pub/Sub topics from every test vehicle in real time
K: A
G: ## Online or offline transfer?
The time to move data is size divided by usable bandwidth. When that figure is weeks or months, or when the link must not be saturated, shipping storage is faster.
- **Storage Transfer Service** moves data online (from other clouds, on-premises storage, URLs) with parallelism, retries and integrity checks. It is optimized for transfers of more than 1 TiB, but it still depends on the network.
- **Transfer Appliance** is a high-capacity device: you copy data to it on site, ship it to a Google facility, and Google uploads the data to your Cloud Storage bucket and wipes the device.
A rule of thumb: calculate transfer time over the real, available bandwidth. If it exceeds your deadline or harms other traffic, choose the offline route.
W: Transfer Appliance avoids the constrained link entirely: the data is copied locally, shipped and loaded by Google, so it arrives within weeks and plant traffic is not affected.
X: B=Laptop script: a single script is neither parallel nor resumable at scale, and the slow shared link would take far longer than the deadline.
C=Storage Transfer Service over the shared link: the service is the right online tool, but it would still use the slow link and degrade the plant's own traffic.
D=Pub/Sub streaming: Pub/Sub is a messaging service for live events, not a bulk loader for existing recordings, and it relies on the same weak connectivity.
T: Large data over a slow or shared link: calculate transfer time; if it misses the deadline, use Transfer Appliance, otherwise Storage Transfer Service.
V: Transfer Appliance, Storage Transfer Service

@ GCP-PCA D5 T5.1 applied troubleshooting
S: A team deploys a new application version that changes a database column from one format to another. It uses a blue/green release for safety, but after switching traffic, the green version works while rolling back to blue fails because blue cannot read the new column format. What should the architect advise for future releases?
A: Make schema changes backward compatible in steps, so that both old and new versions work against the database during the release, and remove the old format only after the rollback window has passed
B: Never roll back; always fix forward, because rollback strategies are not reliable when a database exists
C: Change the schema first, and keep the old application version offline until the new one has proven itself
D: Run each version on its own separate database so that the schema is independent, and merge the two databases manually each night
K: A
G: ## The database is shared state
Blue/green and canary releases run two application versions side by side. If the new version changes the schema in a way the old version cannot read, the rollback path is broken, because the data is shared.
## Expand and contract
- **Expand**: add the new column or format while keeping the old one; the application writes both (or the database keeps both in sync).
- **Migrate**: move existing data in the background.
- **Contract**: remove the old format only after the new version is stable and the rollback window has closed.
This lets the application version change independently of the schema and keeps the rollback useful.
W: Backward-compatible, stepwise schema changes mean old and new versions both run against the database, so the rollback to blue remains safe until the old format is retired.
X: B=Fix forward only: rollback is a major safety tool and is valuable when the schema is designed to support it. Dropping it increases the risk of long incidents.
C=Schema first, old version offline: that creates downtime and is the same breaking change without any rollback.
D=Separate databases merged nightly: two diverging copies of the data create consistency conflicts and lose writes made during the day.
T: Release safety needs compatible schema steps (expand, migrate, contract), not just two copies of the application.

@ GCP-PCA D5 T5.1 advanced use-case
