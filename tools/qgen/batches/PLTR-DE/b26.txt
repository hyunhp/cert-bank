# PLTR-DE batch 26: Domain 1 round 4 (Data Connectivity and Ingestion), 8 questions

@ PLTR-DE D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: A source system exposes its data only through nightly compressed archive files placed in a cloud bucket, each containing dozens of CSV files. Which ingestion design handles this cleanly?
A: A JDBC sync against the bucket, since cloud buckets can be queried with SQL once the archives have been mounted as tables
B: A file sync that lands the archives as raw files, followed by a transform that unpacks and parses the CSVs into typed datasets with expectations
C: A manual process in which an engineer downloads each archive, unzips it on a laptop and uploads the CSVs one by one through the browser
D: A streaming sync that reads each archive byte by byte as it arrives and emits the rows as messages for downstream consumers
K: B
G: ## Raw landing, then parsing
The exam guide asks you to choose connectors by protocol and to layer ingestion (land raw, then validate and transform). Archives in a bucket are files; the sync's job is to land them unchanged.
## Design
- File sync: pick up new archives by name pattern; append transactions.
- Transform: unpack, parse CSVs, enforce schema, write typed datasets.
- Expectations on the parsed output; the raw archives stay as the audit trail.
Example: the nightly archive lands at 01:10; the parsing build produces 30 typed datasets by 01:40.
W: File sync for the raw archives plus a parsing transform: the right connector for files and clean separation of landing from processing.
X: A=JDBC on a bucket: object storage is not a database; there is no SQL endpoint for archive files.
C=Manual unzip and upload: unrepeatable, unscheduled and ungoverned, and it breaks the first night someone is away.
D=Streaming bytes: a nightly archive is a batch artefact; a stream of bytes from a zip file is not a meaningful event stream.
T: Land raw files with a file sync and parse them in a transform; do not force batch files into database or streaming patterns.
V: Data Connection, Code Repositories

@ PLTR-DE D1 T1.2 applied troubleshooting
S: An update sync uses a `modified_at` column, but the source stores it in local time with daylight-saving changes. Twice a year, rows go missing or duplicate around the clock change. What is the robust fix?
A: Run a full snapshot on the two days a year when the clocks change and keep the update sync for the rest of the year
B: Use a timezone-independent signal (a UTC timestamp or a monotonic version number), or a lookback window with deduplication
C: Pause the sync for the hour of the clock change so that no rows are processed during the ambiguous period
D: Ask analysts to ignore any anomalies that appear in the data during the weeks after a clock change
K: B
G: ## Watermarks must be monotonic
The exam guide asks you to understand sync patterns and their failure modes. A local-time watermark jumps backwards or skips an hour at daylight-saving changes, breaking the "greater than last watermark" logic.
## Fix
- Prefer a UTC column or a monotonic version counter as the change signal.
- If unavailable, a lookback window (longer than the discontinuity) with primary-key deduplication absorbs the jump.
- Add a reconciliation check around the change dates.
Example: switching to the source's `row_version` column removes the problem; no special handling is needed on clock-change days.
W: A monotonic, timezone-independent signal (or lookback with dedupe): the watermark logic holds through the clock change without special-casing.
X: A=Snapshot on two days: a calendar-dependent workaround that someone must remember and that still misses late rows around the change.
C=Pause for an hour: rows modified during that hour are never picked up, creating a permanent gap.
D=Ignore anomalies: missing and duplicate rows are data errors; telling analysts to ignore them is not a fix.
T: Base incremental syncs on monotonic signals such as UTC timestamps or version numbers, not on local time.
V: Data Connection

@ PLTR-DE D1 T1.3 applied use-case
S: A partner needs a daily extract of a curated dataset, but only three of its twelve columns and only rows for their own region. How should the export be designed?
A: Export the full twelve-column dataset to the partner and ask them in the contract to use only the columns and rows they are entitled to
B: Build a partner-specific dataset with exactly the permitted columns and rows, and export it through a governed export under the egress policy
C: Give the partner a Foundry account with Viewer access to the curated dataset so they can filter it themselves
D: Email the partner a spreadsheet each morning prepared by an analyst who filters the data by hand
K: B
G: ## Minimize before you export
The exam guide covers exports and egress policies and least privilege for consumers. Data that leaves the platform should be the minimum needed.
## Design
- A derived dataset: select the three columns, filter to the partner's region.
- Expectations: no other regions, no extra columns.
- Governed export of that dataset only; lineage shows what the partner receives.
Example: the partner's daily file has 3 columns and about 40,000 rows; nothing else ever leaves.
W: A minimized partner dataset exported under policy: the partner gets exactly what they may see, and the export is governed and auditable.
X: A=Full export with a request: the partner receives data they are not entitled to; a request is not a control.
C=Foundry account on the curated dataset: exposes all columns and regions, and over-provisions an external party.
D=Hand-filtered spreadsheet: unreliable, ungoverned and error-prone.
T: Export derived datasets that contain only the permitted columns and rows, through governed exports.
V: exports, egress policies

@ PLTR-DE D1 T1.4 applied troubleshooting
S: A sync's schema changed when the source added a column, and the sync now fails with a schema mismatch error. What is the correct handling?
A: Delete the dataset and recreate it so that the new schema is inferred from scratch, accepting the loss of transaction history
B: Classify the change as additive, update the sync's expected schema, verify downstream transforms on a branch, and rerun the sync
C: Ask the source team to remove the new column so that the existing sync configuration continues to work unchanged
D: Ignore the failure and wait, since the sync will adapt to the new schema automatically after a few runs
K: B
G: ## Schema evidence and classification
The exam guide asks you to diagnose ingestion with schema evidence and to classify changes as additive or breaking. A new column is additive; the sync's schema definition needs updating, not the dataset recreating.
## Handling
- Confirm with the error and the source's schema that only a column was added.
- Update the sync's schema configuration.
- Branch-validate downstream transforms (strict schema expectations may need the column added).
- Rerun; keep history intact.
Example: the sync resumes with the extra column; one downstream expectation is updated to permit it.
W: Additive classification, schema update, downstream validation: the sync continues with history preserved and consumers checked.
X: A=Delete and recreate: loses transaction history and lineage for a routine additive change.
C=Remove the column: the source team added it for a reason; the pipeline should adapt.
D=Wait: a failing sync does not fix itself; the data goes stale meanwhile.
T: Handle additive source schema changes by updating the sync's schema and validating downstream, never by recreating datasets.
V: Data Connection, Data Health

@ PLTR-DE D1 T1.1 applied comparison
S: A new team member asks how Data Connection agents work before planning ingestion from an on-premises database. Which statement about agents is accurate?
A: An agent is a cloud service run by the platform that pulls data from any source over the public internet without customer involvement
B: An agent is software installed inside the customer's network that connects to local sources and communicates outbound to the platform
C: An agent is only required for streaming sources with continuous message flow and is never used for ordinary batch database syncs
D: An agent replaces the need for source credentials entirely, since it authenticates to every source using the platform's own identity
K: B
G: ## What an agent is
The exam guide covers agents among connection architecture concepts.
- Installed on a host inside the customer network (or a network segment).
- Connects to sources locally (databases, file shares) and talks outbound to the platform.
- Used for batch and other syncs alike when the source is not reachable directly.
- Still uses source credentials, configured securely in the source definition.
Example: an on-premises Oracle database behind a closed firewall is synced nightly by the agent on a server in the same data centre.
W: Customer-hosted, outbound-connecting software for reaching sources the platform cannot reach directly: the accurate description.
X: A=Platform cloud service: that describes a direct connection, not an agent.
C=Streaming only: agents are used for batch database and file syncs all the time; the question is reachability, not sync type.
D=No credentials: sources still require credentials, stored in the connection configuration.
T: Agents run inside the customer network and connect outbound; they are the answer to unreachable sources.
V: Data Connection, agents

@ PLTR-DE D1 T1.2 applied use-case
S: A team must ingest a reference table of 2,000 rows that changes a few times a month, from a database that supports CDC but at significant setup effort. Which pattern is proportionate?
A: Set up CDC despite the effort, since it is the most capable pattern and guarantees every change is captured within seconds of occurring
B: A daily or more frequent snapshot sync, since the table is tiny, changes rarely, and a full copy is cheap and always correct
C: A streaming sync so that the reference table is updated the instant any row changes
D: An append sync, since reference tables only ever gain rows
K: B
G: ## Proportionate ingestion
The exam guide asks you to choose patterns from source behaviour, volume and cost. For a tiny, slowly changing table, the simplest correct pattern wins.
## Reasoning
- 2,000 rows: a snapshot costs almost nothing.
- Snapshot captures inserts, updates and deletes without any change signal.
- CDC's setup effort buys nothing here.
Example: the hourly snapshot keeps the reference table current with no engineering beyond a schedule.
W: Snapshot: correct for all change types, negligible cost at this size, and no setup burden.
X: A=CDC: it buys second-level latency and change-event detail that a 2,000-row reference table changed monthly does not need, at real setup cost.
C=Streaming: a reference table is not a stream; instant updates are not required.
D=Append: reference rows are updated and deleted too; append would miss those changes.
T: For small, slowly changing tables, snapshot; reserve CDC for large tables with frequent changes and tight latency.
V: Data Connection

@ PLTR-DE D1 T1.3 applied troubleshooting
S: Analysts querying a virtual table report that results differ from what the warehouse team sees for "the same query". What is a likely explanation to check first?
A: Virtual tables return randomized samples of the source for performance, so differences between the two sides are expected and unimportant
B: The queries differ: Foundry may apply filters, permissions or type coercions, or the data changed between runs; compare the exact query and timing
C: The warehouse team is reading the wrong database or schema on their side and should be corrected before anything else is investigated
D: Virtual tables cache the source data for a week after the first query, so Foundry shows stale results by design until the cache expires
K: B
G: ## Diagnosing discrepancies on virtual tables
A virtual table queries the source in place, but what each side actually executed can differ. The exam guide expects awareness of virtual table behaviour and of permission layers.
## Checks
- The pushed-down query: filters, column selection, casts.
- Permissions applied on the Foundry side (row-level restrictions).
- Timing: the source changed between queries.
Example: the Foundry side applied a region restriction for the analyst; the warehouse team queried all regions.
W: Compare the exact queries, permissions and timing: the difference almost always lies in what was actually executed and by whom.
X: A=Random samples: virtual tables do not sample; results are deterministic for a given query.
C=Wrong database: possible but unlikely; verify the query first.
D=Week-long cache: virtual tables query the source live; they do not cache for a week.
T: When virtual-table results differ from the source, compare the executed queries, the permissions and the timing.
V: virtual tables, restricted views

@ PLTR-DE D1 T1.4 advanced tradeoff
S: An organization has 300 syncs. The data engineering team wants to find systemic ingestion problems rather than reacting to each failure. What should they build?
A: A rule that every sync failure is emailed immediately to the head of data engineering for personal attention and follow-up
B: A monitoring view over execution history and health checks: failure rates by source and agent, lateness distributions, trends, reviewed regularly
C: A policy of automatically retrying every failed sync up to ten times, which resolves most failures without any human involvement at all
D: A reduction in the number of syncs to a manageable fifty by asking business teams which sources they can do without
K: B
G: ## Fleet-level operations
The exam guide treats monitoring, alerting and operational review as data engineering responsibilities. At scale, patterns matter more than individual incidents.
## Build
- Aggregate execution history: failures by source type, agent, time of day.
- Freshness lateness distribution; chronic offenders.
- Weekly review: root causes (one agent host causing 40% of failures; one vendor's credentials expiring monthly).
- Fixes at the cause: host replacement, credential rotation automation.
Example: the view shows that half of failures come from two agents on an overloaded host; moving them removes a third of all incidents.
W: An aggregated monitoring view with regular review: systemic causes become visible and are fixed once instead of handled 300 times.
X: A=Email the head: a firehose to one person; no aggregation, no pattern detection.
C=Ten retries: masks persistent problems and delays detection of real failures.
D=Fewer syncs: the business needs the data; the fix is operational maturity, not scope reduction.
T: Operate ingestion as a fleet: aggregate failures and freshness, find shared causes, fix at the root.
V: Data Health, Data Connection
