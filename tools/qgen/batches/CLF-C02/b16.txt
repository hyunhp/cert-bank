@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.1 expert comparison
S: A furniture retailer runs its online store in one AWS Region in Europe. Last year it bought a three-year Compute Savings Plan that covers its steady, predictable traffic, so its compute bill barely changes from month to month. Leadership now wants to open the store to shoppers in Japan and Australia within six weeks, with pages loading as quickly for them as for European shoppers. The developers also want to try several page designs in the new markets and throw away the ones that fail, without waiting for hardware. Which TWO advantages of cloud computing does this plan rely on most directly? (Select TWO)
A: Go global in minutes, because the store can be deployed in AWS Regions close to Japan and Australia without the retailer building facilities there.
B: Increase speed and agility, because the resources for each trial page design arrive in minutes and cost little to discard when a design fails.
C: Trade fixed expense for variable expense, because the existing three-year commitment turns the retailer's compute bill into pure pay-as-you-go spending.
D: Stop guessing capacity, because the expansion is driven by unpredictable traffic spikes that the current European deployment can no longer absorb.
E: Benefit from massive economies of scale, because opening the new markets requires the retailer to negotiate volume hardware discounts with AWS first.
K: A,B
G: ## The six advantages of cloud computing
AWS describes six advantages. Each one answers a different business question, so the trick is to match the advantage to what the plan actually needs:
- **Trade fixed expense for variable expense**: pay only for what you consume instead of investing in data centers up front.
- **Benefit from massive economies of scale**: AWS aggregates usage from many customers and passes on lower pay-as-you-go prices. The customer does nothing to get this.
- **Stop guessing capacity**: scale up or down with demand instead of buying for a forecast.
- **Increase speed and agility**: new resources are minutes away instead of weeks, so experiments are cheap and fast.
- **Stop spending money running and maintaining data centers**: focus on customers, not racking and powering servers.
- **Go global in minutes**: deploy in Regions around the world for lower latency at minimal cost.
## Reading the scenario
The goals are new markets far away with low latency (go global) and fast, disposable experiments (speed and agility). The details rule out two tempting choices: the traffic is steady and predictable, so capacity guessing is not the problem, and a three-year commitment is the opposite of a pure variable expense.
W: A=Go global in minutes: serving shoppers in Japan and Australia with low latency means running the store in Regions near them, and AWS lets the retailer deploy there in days instead of building or leasing data centers abroad.
B=Increase speed and agility: trying several designs and discarding the failures is only cheap when resources appear in minutes and stop costing money when deleted, which is exactly what this advantage describes.
X: C=Trade fixed expense for variable expense: the retailer's Savings Plan is a three-year commitment, so its compute bill is deliberately not pure pay-as-you-go. The expansion does not depend on this advantage.
D=Stop guessing capacity: the stem says the traffic is steady and predictable. The expansion is about reaching new markets, not about absorbing unpredictable spikes.
E=Benefit from massive economies of scale: customers receive the lower prices that come from AWS's scale automatically; there is no negotiation of hardware discounts, and the plan does not hinge on price.
T: Reaching distant users with low latency is "go global in minutes"; cheap, fast, disposable experiments are "increase speed and agility".
V: AWS Global Infrastructure, Savings Plans

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.2 expert comparison
S: A payroll company has finished an AWS Well-Architected review of its main workload and must send each high-risk finding to the owner of the matching pillar. The owners insist that a finding goes to the pillar whose guidance it most directly breaks, not to a pillar it only touches indirectly. The sustainability owner has asked to receive every finding that is framed around the energy the workload consumes. Which THREE pairings of finding and pillar are correct? (Select THREE)
A: Releases are deployed by hand from a wiki checklist with no rollback plan, so the finding belongs to operational excellence.
B: Database backups have never been restored to prove that recovery works, so the finding belongs to performance efficiency.
C: An IAM role used by the payroll application grants full administrator access to every service, so the finding belongs to security.
D: Servers run at 15 percent average CPU around the clock, wasting energy on every payroll run, so the finding belongs to sustainability.
E: Nobody can tell which team's resources drive the monthly bill because nothing is tagged, so the finding belongs to reliability.
F: The database type was chosen without checking whether it fits the data access patterns, so the finding belongs to cost optimization.
K: A,C,D
G: ## The six pillars and what each one owns
The AWS Well-Architected Framework groups its guidance into six pillars:
- **Operational excellence**: how the workload is run and changed. Treat operations as code, make changes small and reversible, observe the workload and learn from events.
- **Security**: protecting data, systems and identities. Least privilege, traceability, protection at every layer.
- **Reliability**: performing the intended function consistently and recovering from failure. Back up data and test recovery, isolate faults, adapt to changes in demand.
- **Performance efficiency**: using resources efficiently to meet requirements. Choose technology that fits the problem (for example, pick a database by its access patterns), experiment, use serverless and managed services.
- **Cost optimization**: delivering business value at the lowest price. Cloud financial management, a consumption model and attributing spending to its owners.
- **Sustainability**: minimizing environmental impact. Understand your impact and maximize utilization, because two hosts at 30% use more energy than one host at 60%.
## Some findings touch several pillars
Low utilization costs money and wastes energy. The stem settles it: findings framed around energy go to sustainability.
W: A=Manual releases to operational excellence: operations as code, small reversible changes and rollback plans are core operational excellence practices, so a hand-run checklist with no rollback breaks that pillar most directly.
C=Over-broad IAM role to security: granting full administrator access violates the principle of least privilege, a central security pillar practice.
D=Idle servers to sustainability: the finding is framed around wasted energy, and maximizing utilization is a sustainability design principle. The stem assigns energy-framed findings to that owner.
X: B=Untested backups to performance efficiency: backing up data and testing that it can be restored is failure management under the reliability pillar, not a question of how efficiently resources meet demand.
E=Untagged resources to reliability: attributing spending to teams through tags is cloud financial management, which belongs to cost optimization. It has nothing to do with recovering from failure.
F=Database fit to cost optimization: choosing a data store by its access patterns is the performance efficiency idea of selecting the right technology for the job. Cost may follow, but it is not the guidance the finding breaks.
T: Map a finding to the pillar whose practice it breaks directly: manual changes to operational excellence, over-broad access to security, untested recovery to reliability, technology fit to performance efficiency, cost attribution to cost optimization, energy waste to sustainability.
V: AWS Well-Architected Framework

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.2 expert tradeoff
S: An insurance company's claims platform runs in two Availability Zones and has an automated failover runbook. During a real Availability Zone outage last quarter, the failover stalled because a certificate used by the runbook had expired, and nobody had noticed beforehand. The team wants to find such problems before the next real event and wants the operations staff, not only the automation, to practice their response. The change board approves only exercises that inject simulated failures into the live production environment during scheduled windows. Which Well-Architected general design principle does this plan follow most directly?
A: Test systems at production scale, by building a production-sized copy of the platform on demand, testing it and decommissioning it afterward.
B: Improve through game days, by regularly scheduling events that simulate failures in production so that people and processes get exercised.
C: Stop guessing your capacity needs, by letting the platform scale out and in with demand so an outage never leaves it short of servers.
D: Consider evolutionary architectures, by redesigning the failover components every year as the business and its technology choices change.
K: B
G: ## The general design principles of the Well-Architected Framework
Besides the six pillars, the framework lists general design principles for good design in the cloud:
- **Stop guessing your capacity needs**: use as much or as little capacity as needed and scale automatically.
- **Test systems at production scale**: create a production-scale **test environment** on demand, test, then decommission it. You pay for it only while it runs.
- **Automate with architectural experimentation in mind**: automation makes workloads cheap to create, change and roll back.
- **Consider evolutionary architectures**: let the design change as the business changes instead of fixing it once.
- **Drive architectures using data**: base design decisions on data collected from the workload.
- **Improve through game days**: regularly schedule game days that **simulate events in production** to find weak spots and build the organization's experience in handling them.
## Two testing principles side by side
Both principles are about testing, but they answer different needs. Production-scale testing checks a copy of the system under realistic load. Game days exercise the real system and the people who run it during a simulated event. An expired certificate in a runbook, plus staff who need practice, is a game day problem.
W: Improve through game days: game days simulate events in production on a schedule, which matches the change board's rule, and they exercise both the automation and the people, which is how an expired certificate would be caught before a real outage.
X: A=Test systems at production scale: this principle builds a separate production-sized test environment and tears it down afterward. The change board only approves exercises in the live production environment, and the goal is practicing the response, not load testing a copy.
C=Stop guessing your capacity needs: automatic scaling addresses demand, not a broken failover runbook. More servers would not have fixed an expired certificate.
D=Consider evolutionary architectures: redesigning components as the business evolves is about long-term design flexibility. It does not test whether today's failover works or train the staff.
T: Simulated failures in production that exercise people and processes are game days; a temporary production-sized copy for testing is "test systems at production scale".
V: AWS Well-Architected Framework

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.3 expert use-case
S: A hospital group is assessing 120 applications for a move to AWS over the next 18 months. One of them is a vendor-supplied staff rostering system running on servers that were replaced with new hardware last year. The vendor has announced a SaaS edition of the product, but it will not be released for another 14 months, and the hospital does not want to move this system twice. The rostering data has no residency restrictions, and every ward uses the system daily. Which migration strategy should the team assign to this application for now?
A: Retain, keeping the system on its recently refreshed servers until the vendor releases the SaaS edition and the hospital can switch to it.
B: Repurchase, moving every ward to the vendor's SaaS edition right away, because a SaaS product removes the infrastructure the hospital maintains.
C: Rehost, moving the current servers onto Amazon EC2 now as they are, then moving the system again when the SaaS edition arrives.
D: Retire, shutting the system down now because a newer SaaS edition from the same vendor will replace it within the next two years.
K: A
G: ## The 7 Rs of migration
AWS Prescriptive Guidance lists seven migration strategies:
- **Retire**: decommission applications with no business value (for example, no inbound connections for 90 days).
- **Retain**: keep the application where it is for now. Common reasons include a recent hardware or software investment, dependencies that must move first, and **waiting for a vendor's SaaS version to be released**.
- **Rehost** (lift and shift): move without changes, for example onto Amazon EC2.
- **Relocate**: move to a cloud version of the same platform, such as VMware-based workloads.
- **Repurchase** (drop and shop): replace the application with a different product, often SaaS.
- **Replatform** (lift, tinker and shift): move with some optimization, such as a managed database.
- **Refactor or re-architect**: redesign the application with cloud-native features.
## Timing matters
Repurchase is the eventual destination here, but it is impossible until the SaaS edition exists. Rehosting first would mean two migrations, which the hospital explicitly rejects, and the servers were just refreshed. Retain is a valid, temporary assignment in a migration plan, not a refusal to migrate.
W: Retain: AWS guidance names "plans to migrate to SaaS" and "recently upgraded" systems as typical retain cases. The hospital keeps the system on its new servers and switches once, directly to the SaaS edition, when it is released.
X: B=Repurchase: this is the long-term target, but the SaaS edition will not be released for another 14 months, so nobody can move to it right away.
C=Rehost: moving to Amazon EC2 now and to SaaS later is exactly the double migration the hospital wants to avoid, and it throws away last year's hardware investment.
D=Retire: retiring suits applications nobody uses. Every ward depends on this system daily, so shutting it down before its replacement exists would stop rostering.
T: An application waiting for a vendor's SaaS release, especially on recently refreshed hardware, is assigned retain until it can be repurchased.
V: AWS Prescriptive Guidance

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.4 expert tradeoff
S: A manufacturer is building a total cost of ownership (TCO) model for moving 50 Linux virtual machines from its leased data center to AWS. To meet the lease deadline it will rehost them on Amazon EC2 without changing the applications or adopting any managed services. The finance team wants every current on-premises cost line marked as either removed or still owed by the company after the move. The servers currently send about 20 TB of reports per month to customers over the internet. Which statement about the TCO model is correct?
A: Keep staff time for operating system patching and backups as still owed, while power, cooling, floor space and server refresh are removed.
B: Mark operating system patching labor as removed, because AWS patches the guest operating system of EC2 instances under the shared model.
C: Keep a five-year server refresh budget in the AWS column, because customers must replace the physical hosts behind their EC2 instances.
D: Remove every network cost line, because data that EC2 instances send to customers over the internet is free of charge on AWS.
K: A
G: ## What a TCO comparison must capture
A fair TCO comparison lists every cost, not just server prices: hardware and its refresh cycle, facilities (power, cooling, floor space, physical security), network, software licenses and the **staff time** needed to operate everything.
## What moves to AWS depends on the service
The shared responsibility model decides which work AWS takes over:
- AWS operates everything from the **host operating system and virtualization layer down to the physical facilities**. Facility costs and hardware refresh disappear from the company's books.
- On Amazon EC2 (infrastructure as a service), the customer still manages the **guest operating system, including updates and security patches**, plus installed software and security groups. Backups of instances also stay with the customer unless it adopts a service for them.
- Managed services such as Amazon RDS would move more of this work to AWS, but this migration deliberately rehosts without them.
## Network costs do not vanish
Data transfer **into** AWS is generally free, but data transfer **out to the internet** is charged per GB after an allowance of 100 GB per month aggregated across services. At 20 TB a month, outbound transfer is a real line item in the AWS column.
W: Patching and backup labor stays, facilities go: with EC2 the customer still owns the guest operating system and its patching, so that staff time remains a cost, while AWS takes over the physical facilities and hardware lifecycle.
X: B=OS patching removed: the shared responsibility model says EC2 customers manage the guest operating system, including updates and security patches. AWS patches the host and virtualization layer, not the guest OS.
C=Server refresh budget in the AWS column: AWS owns and replaces the physical hosts. The company pays for instance usage and never buys replacement servers for EC2.
D=Every network cost removed: data transfer out to the internet is billed per GB beyond the free 100 GB each month, so 20 TB of monthly reports creates an ongoing data transfer charge.
T: Rehosting on EC2 removes facility and hardware costs, but guest OS operations and internet data transfer out remain in the TCO.
V: Amazon EC2

@ CLF-C02 D2 T2.1 expert troubleshooting
S: A logistics company runs two kinds of AWS Lambda functions. Its routing functions are .zip packages on an AWS managed Python runtime, with automatic runtime updates left at the default setting. Its billing functions are deployed as container images built from an AWS base image for Python and stored in Amazon ECR. Both kinds bundle third-party libraries installed from a requirements file. A security auditor asks which patching work the company itself must perform under the shared responsibility model. Which THREE tasks belong to the company? (Select THREE)
A: Updating the third-party libraries bundled with the routing functions whenever one of those libraries receives a security fix.
B: Rebuilding the billing functions' container images from the latest base image and redeploying them so they pick up runtime patches.
C: Applying security patches to the Python runtime and operating system that the routing functions use on the managed runtime.
D: Updating the third-party libraries bundled inside the billing functions' container images whenever one of them receives a security fix.
E: Patching the physical hosts and the virtualization layer that run both the routing functions and the billing functions in the Region.
F: Publishing new versions of the AWS base images for Python whenever the language runtime or the operating system receives a patch.
K: A,B,D
G: ## Lambda's shared responsibility depends on how a function is packaged
Lambda curates and publishes security updates for all supported **managed runtimes** and **container base images**. What happens next depends on the deployment type:
- **.zip functions on a managed runtime**: by default, Lambda **applies** runtime and OS updates automatically. The customer does nothing.
- **Container image functions**: Lambda publishes updated base images, but the customer must **rebuild the image from the latest base image and redeploy** it. A running image never changes by itself.
- **Deprecated runtimes**: Lambda stops updating them, and upgrading to a supported runtime becomes the customer's job.
## Always the customer's
In every case the customer is responsible for its **function code and dependencies**, such as libraries installed from a requirements file. Lambda cannot know which library version the code needs.
## Always AWS's
The physical hosts, the network and the virtualization layer are security "of" the cloud.
W: A=Libraries in the routing functions: function code and its dependencies are always the customer's responsibility, even on a managed runtime.
B=Rebuilding the billing images: for container image functions, Lambda only publishes new base images; the customer must rebuild from the latest base image and redeploy to receive the patches.
D=Libraries in the billing images: bundled dependencies are the customer's in every deployment type, so they must be updated inside the image as well.
X: C=Patching the routing functions' runtime: on a managed runtime with the default automatic update setting, Lambda applies runtime and OS security updates itself.
E=Patching hosts and virtualization: the physical infrastructure and hypervisor that run Lambda are AWS's responsibility under security "of" the cloud.
F=Publishing base images: AWS curates and publishes the Lambda base images. The customer consumes them; it does not produce new versions.
T: For Lambda, AWS patches managed runtimes for you, but container image functions must be rebuilt from new base images, and function dependencies are always yours.
V: AWS Lambda, Amazon ECR

Which control does the shared responsibility model assign to the bank rather than to AWS for this deployment?

D=Monitoring hardware health: once installed, the Outpost is visible in the console and AWS monitors it as part of the Region, including instance health and capacity alerts.

@ CLF-C02 D2 T2.3 expert troubleshooting
S: A small travel agency has one standalone AWS account that is not part of AWS Organizations. Its administrator signs in through an IAM Identity Center user with full administrator permissions and wants to lock the root user away, using it only when nothing else can do the job. Before locking it away she reviews the year's plans. One problem already exists: finance staff whose IAM policies allow billing actions still cannot open any billing pages. Which THREE of the following tasks will require her to sign in as the root user? (Select THREE)
A: Activating IAM access to the Billing and Cost Management console so that the finance staff's billing permissions take effect.
B: Changing the root user's email address and password after the founder who opened the account leaves the agency next month.
C: Registering as a seller in the Reserved Instance Marketplace to sell the unused Reserved Instances from a cancelled project.
D: Updating the account's alternate billing and security contacts after the finance manager and the security lead change jobs.
E: Upgrading the account's AWS Support plan from Basic Support to Business Support+ before the agency launches its new booking site.
F: Creating IAM roles with permissions boundaries for the developers who will start building the new booking site next spring.
K: A,B,C
G: ## The root user is for a short list of tasks
AWS recommends doing daily work through an administrative user in IAM Identity Center and keeping the root user for the tasks that **only** it can do. For a **standalone account** (not in AWS Organizations), these include:
- Changing the **root user's email address, password and access keys**, and closing or reopening the account.
- Restoring IAM permissions when the only administrator has locked themselves out.
- **Activating IAM access to the Billing and Cost Management console**. Until this is done, IAM policies that allow billing actions have no effect.
- **Registering as a seller in the Reserved Instance Marketplace**.
- Enabling MFA delete on an S3 bucket, and editing an S3 bucket or SQS queue policy that denies all principals.
## Not root-only
Other account settings, such as the **account name, contact information and alternate contacts**, can be changed by IAM principals with permissions. Changing the **AWS Support plan** requires IAM permissions, not the root user. In AWS Organizations, the management account can also perform several root tasks for member accounts centrally, which is why the stem says the account is standalone.

Every account has **Basic Support** (account and billing help, documentation, re:Post, core Trusted Advisor checks, AWS Health). The paid plans are:
- **Business Support+**: the minimum plan AWS recommends for production. AI-powered contextual help, 24/7 phone, web and chat access to Cloud Support Engineers, **under 30 minutes** for business-critical system down, the full set of Trusted Advisor checks. Minimum $29 per month per account. **No designated TAM.**
- **Enterprise Support**: everything in Business Support+, plus a **designated Technical Account Manager**, **up to 15-minute** critical response, **AWS Security Incident Response** included, strategic business reviews and AWS Countdown. Minimum $5,000 (reduced from $15,000).
- **AWS Unified Operations**: for mission-critical workloads. Designated TAM **and Domain Specialist Engineers**, **under 5 minutes** from Incident Management Engineers, critical workload reviews.
## Plans being retired
Developer Support, Business Support and **Enterprise On-Ramp end on January 1, 2027** (they remain in AWS GovCloud (US)). During 2026, Enterprise On-Ramp customers are upgraded automatically to Enterprise Support.
W: Enterprise Support: it is the lowest tier with a designated TAM, a response of up to 15 minutes for business-critical outages and AWS Security Incident Response at no additional cost, which is also the plan AWS moves Enterprise On-Ramp customers to.
