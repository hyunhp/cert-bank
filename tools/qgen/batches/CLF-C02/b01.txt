@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: An online education startup based in Canada signs a contract with a university in Japan and must serve students there with low latency within two weeks. The startup has no staff or offices in Asia. Which benefit of the AWS Cloud makes this practical?
A: Massive economies of scale, because AWS buys hardware in bulk and passes the lower unit cost on to every customer through lower prices.
B: Go global in minutes, because the startup can deploy its application in an AWS Region near Japan without building any facilities there.
C: Trade fixed expense for variable expense, because the startup pays for servers in Japan only during the hours that students are online.
D: Stop guessing capacity, because Auto Scaling in the startup's current Region adds servers automatically when Japanese students log in.
K: B
G: ## The six advantages of cloud computing
AWS describes six advantages that appear throughout the Cloud Practitioner exam:
- **Trade fixed expense for variable expense**: pay for what you use instead of buying hardware up front.
- **Benefit from massive economies of scale**: AWS's huge combined usage lowers its costs, and prices drop for everyone.
- **Stop guessing capacity**: scale up and down with real demand instead of forecasting years ahead.
- **Increase speed and agility**: new resources are a few clicks or API calls away.
- **Stop spending money running and maintaining data centers**: focus on customers, not racking servers.
- **Go global in minutes**: deploy into AWS Regions around the world without building anything there.
## Matching a scenario to one advantage
Most questions describe one pain point. Here the pain point is geography: users are far away, there is no local presence, and the deadline is short. AWS already runs Regions in Asia Pacific, such as Tokyo (`ap-northeast-1`), so the startup can run the same application there with no new buildings, contracts or staff.
W: Go global in minutes: the startup deploys its existing application into a Region close to Japan, such as Tokyo, and students get low latency. No data center, local hardware purchase or local team is needed, which is exactly what "go global in minutes" means.
X: A=Economies of scale: lower prices are a real benefit, but cheaper unit costs do nothing about the distance between Canada and Japan or the two-week deadline.
C=Fixed to variable expense: paying by the hour is true of AWS, but it describes how the startup pays, not how it gets servers close to students on another continent.
D=Stop guessing capacity: scaling in the current Region adds capacity, yet every request from Japan still crosses the Pacific, so latency stays high. The problem is location, not capacity.
T: A need to reach users in another part of the world quickly, without building facilities there, points to "go global in minutes" by deploying in a nearby AWS Region.
V: AWS Global Infrastructure

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: A ticketing company runs its website in its own data center. Traffic is ten times higher during the few days a year when concert sales open, so the company buys servers for that peak, and they sit mostly idle the rest of the year. Which AWS Cloud benefit addresses this problem most directly?
A: Increase speed and agility, because developers can launch new test environments in minutes instead of waiting weeks for new hardware orders.
B: Go global in minutes, because the company can copy its website to Regions on several continents and spread the concert traffic among them.
C: Stop guessing capacity, because the company can add servers when sales open and remove them afterward, paying only while they run.
D: Benefit from massive economies of scale, because AWS buys hardware in bulk and lowers prices for all of its customers over time.
K: C
G: ## Capacity planning on premises
In a data center, capacity is bought months in advance. The company must guess the peak and buy for it. Guess too low and the site crashes on the busiest day; guess too high and expensive servers sit idle most of the year. Either way the guess is locked in.
## Elasticity in the cloud
On AWS, capacity follows demand. Services such as Amazon EC2 Auto Scaling add instances when load rises and remove them when it falls. Because compute is billed while it runs, the idle capacity disappears from the bill.
Example: a site that needs 10 servers normally and 100 for three days pays for 100 servers only for those three days, not for 365.
AWS calls this advantage **stop guessing capacity**, and the ability behind it is **elasticity**.
W: Stop guessing capacity: the company no longer has to size its fleet for the yearly peak. It scales out when sales open and scales back in afterward, so it stops paying for servers that sit idle for most of the year.
X: A=Speed and agility: faster provisioning helps developers experiment, but the problem here is paying all year for peak capacity, not slow access to test environments.
B=Go global in minutes: copying the site to more Regions adds more always-on servers in more places. It does not remove the idle capacity, and the buyers are not described as spread across the world.
D=Economies of scale: lower unit prices make every server cheaper, yet the company would still be paying for a fleet sized for a peak that lasts only a few days.
T: Spiky or unpredictable demand with idle hardware the rest of the time points to elasticity: stop guessing capacity and scale with real demand.
V: Amazon EC2 Auto Scaling

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.1 applied use-case
S: A company's IT team spends most of its week replacing failed disks and patching hypervisors, and new projects wait eight weeks for servers to be ordered and installed. Which TWO AWS Cloud benefits address these problems? (Select TWO)
A: Increase speed and agility, because teams can provision new resources in minutes through the console or API instead of waiting for hardware.
B: Stop spending money running and maintaining data centers, because AWS handles the physical hardware so staff can focus on applications.
C: Shift all security responsibility to AWS, because AWS patches customer applications and configures user permissions in each account.
D: Guaranteed capacity in every Availability Zone, because AWS reserves spare instances for each account without any commitment or fee.
E: Free licensing for all commercial software, because every instance price already includes licenses for any database the team installs.
K: A,B
G: ## Two separate pain points
The scenario lists two problems, and each maps to one of the six cloud advantages.
- **Slow access to resources**: on premises, a new project needs a purchase order, delivery, racking and cabling, which takes weeks. On AWS, a server, database or storage bucket is created in minutes with a console click or an API call. AWS calls this **increase speed and agility**: experiments become cheap and fast.
- **Time spent on undifferentiated work**: replacing disks, maintaining hypervisors, power and cooling do not set the company apart from its competitors. On AWS, the physical infrastructure is AWS's job. AWS calls this **stop spending money running and maintaining data centers**.
## What does not move to AWS
Under the shared responsibility model, AWS secures the cloud itself (facilities, hardware, the virtualization layer). The customer still secures what it puts in the cloud: data, identities and permissions, and for EC2 the guest operating system and applications.
W: A=Speed and agility: resources that used to take eight weeks to order become available in minutes, so new projects start right away.
B=No data center upkeep: AWS replaces failed disks and maintains the hardware and virtualization layer, so the IT team can spend its time on work that matters to the business.
X: C=All security to AWS: the shared responsibility model keeps customer data, IAM permissions, and the guest OS and applications on EC2 with the customer. AWS secures the underlying infrastructure only.
D=Guaranteed spare capacity: AWS does not hold reserved instances for every account for free. Guaranteed capacity in an Availability Zone needs a paid option such as an On-Demand Capacity Reservation.
E=Free software licenses: some instance prices include specific licenses (for example Windows Server), but third-party software licensing is not universally included; many products are bring-your-own-license or bought separately.
T: Waiting weeks for servers points to speed and agility; staff busy maintaining hardware points to letting AWS run the data centers.
V: Amazon EC2

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.2 applied use-case
S: An architect reviews a workload against the AWS Well-Architected Framework. The design should recover automatically when a component fails, and the team should regularly test its recovery procedures. Which pillar do these design principles belong to?
A: Operational excellence, which focuses on running workloads effectively, gaining insight into operations and improving processes over time.
B: Performance efficiency, which focuses on using computing resources efficiently and keeping that efficiency as demand and technology change.
C: Reliability, which focuses on a workload performing its intended function correctly and consistently, and recovering quickly from failures.
D: Sustainability, which focuses on reducing the environmental impact of workloads by maximizing utilization and choosing efficient resources.
K: C
G: ## The six pillars of the Well-Architected Framework
The AWS Well-Architected Framework groups cloud best practices into six pillars:
- **Operational excellence**: run and monitor systems, and improve processes (for example, operations as code, small reversible changes).
- **Security**: protect data, systems and assets (identity, traceability, encryption).
- **Reliability**: do the intended job correctly and consistently, and recover from failures.
- **Performance efficiency**: use resources efficiently as demand changes (for example, serverless, go global in minutes, experiment often).
- **Cost optimization**: deliver business value at the lowest price point.
- **Sustainability**: minimize environmental impact.
## Reliability design principles
- Automatically recover from failure (monitor KPIs and trigger automated recovery).
- Test recovery procedures (simulate failures instead of waiting for them).
- Scale horizontally to increase aggregate workload availability.
- Stop guessing capacity.
- Manage change through automation.
W: Reliability: "automatically recover from failure" and "test recovery procedures" are two of the named design principles of the reliability pillar, whose goal is a workload that keeps doing its job and comes back quickly when something breaks.
X: A=Operational excellence: this pillar covers how teams run and improve operations, such as operations as code and learning from events. Automatic recovery and recovery testing are listed under reliability.
B=Performance efficiency: this pillar is about choosing the right resource types and sizes and adapting as demand changes, not about surviving component failures.
D=Sustainability: this pillar targets energy and resource use, such as maximizing utilization. It does not define how a workload recovers from failures.
T: Recover automatically, test recovery, scale horizontally to survive failures: these are reliability pillar design principles.
V: AWS Well-Architected Framework

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.2 applied use-case
S: A development team ships one large release every quarter. Each release changes hundreds of components at once, and when something breaks it is hard to find the cause and roll back. Which Well-Architected design principle should the team adopt?
A: Make frequent, small, reversible changes, so each deployment touches few components and can be rolled back easily if it fails.
B: Scale horizontally to increase aggregate availability, so that the failure of one large server no longer affects the entire workload.
C: Use serverless architectures, so the team no longer has to run servers and AWS manages capacity for each function automatically.
D: Analyze and attribute expenditure, so the team can see which components and projects are responsible for the workload's costs.
K: A
G: ## Why big releases hurt
When hundreds of changes go out together, a failure could come from any of them. Finding the cause takes long, and rolling back means undoing every change at once, including the good ones.
## The operational excellence answer
The operational excellence pillar of the Well-Architected Framework lists **make frequent, small, reversible changes** as a design principle. Small changes:
- touch few components, so the cause of a failure is easy to find;
- can be reversed quickly without undoing unrelated work;
- go out often, so customers get improvements sooner.
Other operational excellence principles include implementing observability, safely automating where possible (operations as code), refining operations procedures frequently, anticipating failure, and learning from operational events.
Tools such as AWS CodePipeline and AWS CodeDeploy help teams release this way, but the exam question is about the principle.
W: Small, reversible changes: each deployment is small enough that a failure points to a short list of changes and can be rolled back alone. This is a named operational excellence design principle and directly fixes the quarterly big-bang problem.
X: B=Scale horizontally: this reliability principle replaces one big resource with many small ones so a single failure has less impact. It is about infrastructure, not about how code changes are released.
C=Serverless architectures: serverless is a performance efficiency principle that removes server management. Large, risky quarterly releases would still be large and risky on Lambda.
D=Analyze and attribute expenditure: this cost optimization principle explains where money goes. It does not make releases safer or easier to roll back.
T: Big, risky releases that are hard to roll back call for frequent, small, reversible changes (operational excellence).
V: AWS Well-Architected Framework

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.2 applied comparison
S: A team wants to answer a structured set of questions about one of its workloads, compare the answers with AWS best practices across all six pillars, and get a list of high-risk issues with improvement plans. Which AWS resource should the team use?
A: AWS Trusted Advisor, which runs automated checks on the resources in the account and recommends fixes for cost, security, and service quotas.
B: AWS Compute Optimizer, which analyzes resource utilization metrics and recommends better instance types and sizes for running workloads.
C: AWS Config conformance packs, which evaluate resource configurations against a collection of rules and report which resources are noncompliant.
D: AWS Well-Architected Tool, which guides a workload review against the framework's pillars and records the risks and improvement plans found.
K: D
G: ## Reviewing a workload versus checking resources
Two kinds of tools are easy to mix up.
- A **workload review** looks at architecture and practices: how the team deploys, how it handles failures, how it controls cost. Many of these cannot be seen by scanning resources; someone has to answer questions about them.
- **Resource checks** scan what is deployed in an account (instances, buckets, security groups) and flag specific findings.
## AWS Well-Architected Tool
The AWS Well-Architected Tool is a free service in the AWS Management Console. You define a workload, answer the questions for each pillar (and optional lenses, such as the Serverless Lens), and the tool:
- marks answers that reveal **high-risk issues (HRIs)** and medium-risk issues;
- links to guidance and builds an **improvement plan**;
- saves milestones so you can track progress over time.
W: Well-Architected Tool: it is built for exactly this question-based review of one workload against the six pillars and produces a list of high and medium risks with an improvement plan, at no extra charge.
X: A=Trusted Advisor: it scans the account's resources and flags items such as idle instances or open ports. It does not walk a team through questions about its architecture and processes for a workload.
B=Compute Optimizer: it looks at utilization metrics and recommends rightsizing for compute resources. It covers one narrow area, not a review across all six pillars.
C=Config conformance packs: they evaluate resource settings against rules for compliance. They do not ask about practices or produce Well-Architected high-risk issues and improvement plans.
T: A question-based review of a workload against the six pillars, with high-risk issues and improvement plans, is the AWS Well-Architected Tool.
V: AWS Well-Architected Tool, AWS Trusted Advisor, AWS Compute Optimizer, AWS Config

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.3 applied use-case
S: A company must leave its data center in four months when the lease ends. It runs 60 applications on virtual machines, and there is no time to change application code. Which migration strategy fits most of these applications?
A: Refactor, rebuilding the applications as cloud-native services to gain scalability and agility from managed and serverless features.
B: Rehost, moving each server as it is to Amazon EC2 so the applications run on AWS quickly without changing their code.
C: Repurchase, replacing each application with a software as a service product and moving the existing data into the new product.
D: Replatform, moving each application while swapping parts such as its database for a managed service with a few small optimizations.
K: B
G: ## The 7 Rs of migration
AWS describes seven strategies for moving an application:
- **Retire**: switch off what is no longer needed.
- **Retain**: keep it where it is for now.
- **Rehost** ("lift and shift"): move servers as they are to Amazon EC2.
- **Relocate**: move a whole virtualized environment at the hypervisor level without buying new hardware.
- **Repurchase** ("drop and shop"): replace the application with a different product, often SaaS.
- **Replatform** ("lift, tinker and shift"): move with a few optimizations, such as a managed database, without changing the core architecture.
- **Refactor** (re-architect): redesign the application to be cloud-native.
## Speed versus benefit
Rehost is the fastest and needs no code changes, so it suits tight deadlines such as a data center exit. Many companies rehost first and optimize later. AWS Application Migration Service automates rehosting by replicating servers into AWS and launching them as EC2 instances at cutover.
W: Rehost: lift and shift moves each VM to EC2 without touching code, which is the fastest strategy and the only one that fits a four-month deadline with no time for code changes. The company can optimize later.
X: A=Refactor: re-architecting gives the most long-term benefit but needs the most time and code changes, which the deadline rules out.
C=Repurchase: replacing 60 applications with SaaS products means selecting vendors, migrating data and retraining users for each one, which is far from a no-change move in four months.
D=Replatform: swapping components for managed services is a modest change, but it still requires changes and testing for each application. With no time for any changes, a pure rehost is the better fit.
T: Tight deadline and no code changes point to rehost (lift and shift); optimize after the move.
V: AWS Application Migration Service, Amazon EC2

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.3 applied use-case
S: A company plans to migrate 40 on-premises application servers to Amazon EC2 and its Oracle database to Amazon Aurora PostgreSQL, keeping downtime short during cutover. Which TWO AWS services should it use? (Select TWO)
A: AWS Application Migration Service, which continuously replicates the source servers and launches them as Amazon EC2 instances at cutover.
B: AWS Database Migration Service, which copies the data and keeps it in sync, with DMS Schema Conversion to convert the Oracle schema.
C: AWS DataSync, which copies the virtual machine disk images into Amazon S3 so they can be started as instances in a later step.
D: AWS Transfer Family, which provides SFTP endpoints so the database administrators can upload nightly export files into the cloud.
E: AWS Storage Gateway, which presents cloud storage as local volumes so the existing servers can keep running from the data center.
K: A,B
G: ## Two kinds of migration in one project
The project has two parts, and AWS has a dedicated service for each.
- **Servers**: AWS Application Migration Service (AWS MGN) installs a replication agent on each source server and continuously copies its disks at block level into AWS. When the company is ready, it launches test or cutover instances on EC2. Because replication is continuous, the cutover window is short. It is the main AWS service for rehosting.
- **Databases**: AWS Database Migration Service (AWS DMS) copies the data from a source database to a target and, with change data capture (CDC), keeps replicating ongoing changes so the source stays in use until cutover. Moving between engines, such as Oracle to PostgreSQL, is a **heterogeneous** migration and also needs the schema (tables, code objects) converted, which DMS Schema Conversion or the AWS Schema Conversion Tool does.
## Other transfer services
DataSync moves files and objects between storage systems, Transfer Family offers managed SFTP/FTPS/FTP endpoints, and Storage Gateway connects on-premises applications to cloud storage. None of them migrates running servers or a live database.
W: A=Application Migration Service: continuous block-level replication of the 40 servers lets them be launched on EC2 with only a short cutover window and no code changes.
B=Database Migration Service: DMS loads the data and keeps it in sync with change data capture until cutover, and DMS Schema Conversion handles the Oracle-to-PostgreSQL schema.
X: C=DataSync: it copies files and objects between storage systems. It does not convert running servers into bootable EC2 instances or keep them in sync until cutover.
D=Transfer Family: nightly export files over SFTP would mean long downtime and manual reloads, and nothing converts the Oracle schema or keeps the target in sync.
E=Storage Gateway: it extends on-premises applications to cloud storage while they keep running in the data center, which is a hybrid setup, not a migration of the servers.
T: Rehost servers with AWS Application Migration Service; migrate databases (including engine changes) with AWS DMS plus schema conversion.
V: AWS Application Migration Service, AWS Database Migration Service, AWS DataSync

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.4 applied definition
S: A finance director asks how moving workloads from the company's data center to AWS will change the way IT costs appear in the budget. Which statement best describes the change?
A: Large up-front capital expenses for hardware are replaced by variable operating expenses that follow actual usage of AWS resources.
B: Variable operating expenses are replaced by a single large capital expense, because AWS requires payment for three years of service up front.
C: Capital and operating expenses both stay the same, because AWS bills customers for a fixed number of servers on a yearly contract.
D: Operating expenses disappear, because AWS includes all compute, storage, and data transfer in one flat monthly fee for each account.
K: A
G: ## CapEx and OpEx
- **Capital expenditure (CapEx)**: money spent up front on long-lived assets, such as servers, storage arrays and data center buildings. The cost is paid before any value is delivered and is depreciated over several years.
- **Operational expenditure (OpEx)**: ongoing costs for running the business, paid as you go, such as electricity, rent or a cloud bill.
## What changes on AWS
On premises, a new application often starts with a hardware purchase sized for the next few years. On AWS, the company pays for compute, storage and other resources as it uses them, usually by the second, hour or GB. AWS calls this **trade fixed expense for variable expense**.
Example: instead of spending a large sum up front on servers that might be half used, the company pays a monthly bill that rises and falls with real usage.
Commitments such as Savings Plans or Reserved Instances are optional ways to get a lower rate; they are not required to use AWS.
W: CapEx to variable OpEx: AWS replaces up-front hardware purchases with pay-as-you-go charges that follow actual usage, which is the "trade fixed expense for variable expense" advantage.
X: B=Three years up front: AWS has no such requirement. Up-front payment exists only as an optional choice for commitment discounts, such as an all-upfront Reserved Instance.
C=Fixed yearly contract: On-Demand pricing has no fixed server count and no yearly contract. Costs change with the resources actually running.
D=One flat monthly fee: AWS charges per service by usage, such as instance hours, GB stored and GB transferred out. There is no flat all-inclusive fee, and running costs still exist as operating expenses.
T: Moving to AWS turns large up-front capital purchases into variable, pay-as-you-go operating expenses.

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.4 applied use-case
S: A company is building a total cost of ownership (TCO) comparison between its on-premises environment and AWS. The draft lists only the purchase price of its servers on the on-premises side. Which additional costs should be added to make the comparison fair?
A: The AWS Support plan fees, AWS Marketplace subscriptions, and training costs, which apply only after the move and belong only on the AWS side.
B: The costs of application licenses and developer salaries only, because these are the only costs that differ between the two environments.
C: The power, cooling, floor space, network hardware, and staff time needed to install, maintain, and replace the on-premises hardware.
D: The price of the AWS Free Tier offers and of the services AWS includes at no charge, valued at their normal On-Demand rates.
K: C
G: ## What TCO means
Total cost of ownership (TCO) is the full cost of running a workload over its life, not only the sticker price of the equipment. A fair cloud comparison puts all costs side by side.
## Costs that are easy to forget on premises
- **Facilities**: power, cooling, floor space or rent, physical security.
- **Hardware around the servers**: storage, network switches, firewalls, spare parts, refresh every few years.
- **People**: time to rack, cable, patch, monitor and replace hardware, plus procurement.
- **Over-provisioning**: capacity bought for a peak that is idle most of the time.
On AWS, most of these are part of the service price, so leaving them out makes on-premises look cheaper than it is.
## Costs on the AWS side
The AWS side includes the resources used, data transfer out, a paid Support plan if chosen, and migration and training costs. The AWS Pricing Calculator helps estimate it.
W: Facilities, network hardware and staff time: these are real costs of owning servers that are built into AWS prices, so they must be added to the on-premises side for a like-for-like comparison.
X: A=AWS-only costs: Support fees and training are valid entries on the AWS side, but the question is what is missing from the on-premises side. Adding them there would double-count or misplace costs.
B=Licenses and salaries only: licensing matters, but many costs differ between the environments, such as facilities, hardware refresh and the operations staff time spent on hardware.
D=Free Tier at On-Demand rates: free offers cost nothing, so valuing them at list price would invent costs that the company never pays.
T: A fair TCO comparison includes facilities, supporting hardware and staff time on premises, not only the server purchase price.

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.4 applied tradeoff
S: A company moving to AWS owns licenses for database software that are priced per physical CPU socket and core, and it wants to reuse them instead of buying new licenses. Which Amazon EC2 option supports this bring-your-own-license (BYOL) approach?
A: Dedicated Instances, which run on hardware dedicated to one customer but do not show which physical sockets and cores the instances use.
B: Dedicated Hosts, which give the company a physical server and visibility into its sockets and cores for license counting.
C: Spot Instances, which use spare EC2 capacity at a steep discount and include the database licenses in the hourly price.
D: On-Demand Instances with license-included pricing, which charges for the database license as part of each running hour.
K: B
G: ## Two ways to license software on AWS
- **License included**: AWS includes the license in the price, for example Windows Server on EC2 or some engines on Amazon RDS. Simple, but the company pays for licenses again even if it already owns them.
- **Bring your own license (BYOL)**: the company reuses licenses it already owns, which can lower costs, but it must follow the vendor's licensing terms.
## Why per-socket and per-core licenses need Dedicated Hosts
Some licenses are counted per physical socket or core, or are tied to a physical server. On shared hardware the company cannot prove which physical server it used.
An **EC2 Dedicated Host** is a physical server fully dedicated to one customer. It shows the number of sockets and physical cores, lets the company control instance placement on the host, and works with AWS License Manager to track license use.
**Dedicated Instances** also run on hardware not shared with other customers, but AWS chooses the physical server and does not expose socket or core details.
W: Dedicated Hosts: the company gets a whole physical server with visible sockets and cores, so it can count and reuse its per-socket or per-core licenses and stay within the vendor's terms.
X: A=Dedicated Instances: they give hardware isolation, but without visibility into sockets, cores or host placement the company cannot meet socket- or core-based license terms.
C=Spot Instances: Spot lowers the price of compute, not of third-party licenses, and it can be interrupted with a two-minute notice, which suits few database workloads.
D=License-included On-Demand: this charges for a new license every hour, which is the opposite of reusing the licenses the company already owns.
T: Reusing licenses counted per socket, per core or per physical server points to EC2 Dedicated Hosts (BYOL).
V: Amazon EC2 Dedicated Hosts, AWS License Manager

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.1 applied use-case
S: A media company runs a nightly video transcoding job that can be stopped and restarted at any time without losing work, and the deadline is flexible. The company wants the lowest possible compute cost. Which EC2 purchasing option should it use?
A: Spot Instances, which use spare EC2 capacity at a large discount and can be interrupted by AWS with a two-minute warning.
B: Reserved Instances, which give a discount in exchange for a one-year or three-year commitment to a specific instance configuration.
C: Dedicated Hosts, which give the company an entire physical server for its own use and visibility into the server's sockets and cores.
D: On-Demand Instances, which charge by the second with no commitment and are never interrupted by AWS while they are running.
K: A
G: ## EC2 purchasing options at a glance
- **On-Demand**: pay by the second (Linux) or hour, no commitment. Good for short, unpredictable workloads.
- **Savings Plans / Reserved Instances**: commit to one or three years of usage for a lower rate. Good for steady workloads.
- **Spot Instances**: use spare EC2 capacity for up to 90% off On-Demand prices. AWS can reclaim the capacity with a **two-minute interruption notice**.
- **Dedicated Hosts / Dedicated Instances**: hardware not shared with other customers, for compliance or licensing needs, at a higher price.
## When Spot is the right choice
Spot fits work that is **fault-tolerant and flexible**: batch processing, rendering and transcoding, CI builds, big data jobs. If an instance is reclaimed, the job restarts or another instance picks up the work.
## Data transfer is priced separately
Whatever the purchasing option, data transfer has its own prices: data coming into AWS from the internet is free, while data going out to the internet is charged per GB.
W: Spot Instances: the job tolerates interruptions and has a flexible deadline, so it can use spare capacity at the deepest discount; if AWS reclaims an instance, the job simply restarts later.
X: B=Reserved Instances: a one- or three-year commitment suits steady, always-on workloads. A nightly job that can wait gets a bigger discount from Spot without any commitment.
C=Dedicated Hosts: they cost more than shared capacity and exist for licensing and compliance needs, neither of which the job has.
D=On-Demand Instances: they are never interrupted, but the company pays the full price for a guarantee the job does not need.
T: Interruptible, flexible, fault-tolerant work at the lowest cost points to Spot Instances.
V: Amazon EC2 Spot Instances

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.1 applied tradeoff
S: A company has steady compute usage that it expects to keep for three years. Over that time it plans to change EC2 instance families, move between Regions, and run part of the workload on AWS Fargate and AWS Lambda. Which pricing model gives a discount while allowing all of these changes?
A: EC2 Instance Savings Plans, which offer one of the largest discounts in exchange for committing to one instance family in one chosen Region.
B: Standard Reserved Instances, which offer a large discount for a specific instance type in a Region and cannot be exchanged for a different family.
C: Compute Savings Plans, which apply a lower rate to EC2 usage in any family, size, or Region and to Fargate and Lambda usage as well.
D: Spot Instances, which offer the deepest discount on spare capacity and apply automatically to Fargate tasks and Lambda functions too.
K: C
G: ## How Savings Plans work
A Savings Plan is a commitment to spend a fixed amount per hour on compute (for example, `$10/hour`) for one or three years. Usage up to that amount is billed at the lower Savings Plans rate; usage above it is billed On-Demand.
## The two compute plan types
- **Compute Savings Plans**: up to 66% off. Apply to EC2 in **any instance family, size, Region, operating system or tenancy**, and also to **AWS Fargate** and **AWS Lambda**. Most flexible.
- **EC2 Instance Savings Plans**: up to 72% off. Apply to **one instance family in one Region** (size, OS and tenancy can still change). Bigger discount, less flexibility.
## Reserved Instances for comparison
Standard Reserved Instances give a similar discount for a fixed instance configuration in a Region. Convertible Reserved Instances can be exchanged for other EC2 configurations but still cover only EC2, not Fargate or Lambda.
W: Compute Savings Plans: the discount follows the company's compute spend across instance families, Regions, Fargate and Lambda, so every planned change stays covered by the commitment.
X: A=EC2 Instance Savings Plans: the deeper discount is tied to one instance family in one Region, so changing families, moving Regions or shifting to Fargate and Lambda would fall outside the plan.
B=Standard Reserved Instances: they are tied to an EC2 configuration in a Region and do not cover Fargate or Lambda, so the planned changes would leave usage at On-Demand rates.
D=Spot Instances: Spot is spare EC2 capacity that can be interrupted, which does not suit steady usage, and it is not a discount model for Lambda. (Fargate Spot exists but is a separate, interruptible capacity option.)
T: Steady usage that will change families, Regions or move to Fargate and Lambda points to Compute Savings Plans.
V: Savings Plans, Amazon EC2, AWS Fargate, AWS Lambda

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.2 applied use-case
S: Several project teams share one AWS account. Finance wants to see monthly costs broken down by project in AWS Cost Explorer. Every resource already carries a `Project` tag, but Cost Explorer cannot group costs by it. What should the company do?
A: Create an AWS Budgets cost budget for each project, which automatically adds a project grouping column to every report in Cost Explorer.
B: Move each project's resources into its own Availability Zone, so Cost Explorer can group the costs of each project by Availability Zone.
C: Run AWS Pricing Calculator estimates for each project and upload them into Cost Explorer as the monthly cost breakdown.
D: Activate `Project` as a user-defined cost allocation tag in the Billing and Cost Management console, then group by it in Cost Explorer.
K: D
G: ## Resource tags versus cost allocation tags
A tag is a key-value label on a resource, such as `Project=checkout`. Tagging resources alone does not make the tag appear in billing tools. A tag key must also be **activated as a cost allocation tag** in the AWS Billing and Cost Management console (by the management account in an organization).
- **User-defined cost allocation tags**: tags you create, such as `Project` or `CostCenter`.
- **AWS-generated cost allocation tags**: tags AWS adds, such as `aws:createdBy`.
After activation, the tag appears in AWS Cost Explorer as a filter and group-by dimension and as a column in cost and usage data exports. Activation can take up to 24 hours to show up.
## Which tool does what
- **Cost Explorer**: view, filter and forecast past and current costs.
- **AWS Budgets**: set cost or usage thresholds and get alerts.
- **AWS Pricing Calculator**: estimate costs before building.
- **Cost and Usage Report (Data Exports)**: the most detailed line-item billing data.
W: Activate the cost allocation tag: once `Project` is activated as a user-defined cost allocation tag, Cost Explorer can filter and group costs by project, which is exactly the breakdown finance wants.
X: A=Budgets per project: a budget alerts when spend crosses a threshold, and a budget can be filtered by an active tag, but creating one does not activate the tag or add a dimension to Cost Explorer.
B=One Availability Zone per project: it would distort the architecture for billing reasons, many costs (such as S3 or data transfer) are not tied to one zone, and Cost Explorer can already group by tag once it is activated.
C=Pricing Calculator: it produces estimates for planned resources. It cannot report actual monthly charges, and its estimates cannot be uploaded into Cost Explorer.
T: To break down costs by a tag in Cost Explorer, activate the tag key as a cost allocation tag in the Billing and Cost Management console.
V: AWS Cost Explorer, AWS Billing and Cost Management

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.2 applied use-case
S: A company with 15 AWS accounts, each paying its own bill, plans to bring them into AWS Organizations and turn on consolidated billing. Which TWO benefits will it gain? (Select TWO)
A: One bill paid by the management account, with the charges of each member account still shown separately in the billing data.
B: Usage from all accounts is combined for tiered volume pricing, and Reserved Instance and Savings Plans discounts can be shared.
C: Every member account receives the features of a paid AWS Support plan at no extra cost once it joins the organization.
D: Member accounts are blocked automatically from spending more than their share of the monthly bill set by the management account.
E: The IAM users of all member accounts are merged into the management account so they sign in with a single set of credentials.
K: A,B
G: ## What consolidated billing is
Consolidated billing is a free feature of AWS Organizations. One account, the **management account**, pays the charges of all **member accounts** in the organization.
## What it gives you
- **One bill**: a single payment method and invoice, while Cost Explorer and the billing data still break costs down by account.
- **Combined usage for volume discounts**: services with tiered pricing (for example, Amazon S3 storage or data transfer) count usage across all accounts, so the organization reaches cheaper tiers sooner.
- **Shared commitment discounts**: Reserved Instance and Savings Plans discounts bought in one account can apply to matching usage in other accounts. Sharing is on by default and can be turned off for specific accounts.
## What it does not do
It does not include a Support plan (Support is per account and paid separately), does not cap spending (use AWS Budgets and budget actions or service control policies for guardrails), and does not merge IAM identities (use IAM Identity Center for single sign-on across accounts).
W: A=One bill, per-account detail: the management account receives a single invoice for all accounts, while charges stay attributable to each member account.
B=Combined usage and shared discounts: usage is aggregated across accounts for volume pricing tiers, and unused Reserved Instance or Savings Plans discounts can cover usage in other accounts.
X: C=Free Support plan: Support plans are not included in consolidated billing. Each account's Support plan is purchased separately.
D=Automatic spend caps: consolidated billing only combines payment. Spending alerts or limits need AWS Budgets, budget actions or other controls.
E=Merged IAM users: IAM users stay in their own accounts. Single sign-on across accounts is done with IAM Identity Center, not with consolidated billing.
T: Consolidated billing gives one bill, combined volume pricing and shared RI/Savings Plans discounts, at no extra cost.
V: AWS Organizations, AWS Billing and Cost Management

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.3 applied comparison
S: An operations team wants to be notified when AWS plans scheduled maintenance that will affect the specific EC2 instances in its account, and wants to see which of its resources are affected by an ongoing AWS service issue. Which AWS resource provides this?
A: AWS Trusted Advisor, which checks the account against best practices and flags issues such as idle resources and open security groups.
B: AWS Health Dashboard, which shows AWS events affecting the account's resources, such as scheduled maintenance and service issues.
C: AWS CloudTrail, which records the API calls made in the account, including who made each call, when it was made, and from where.
D: AWS Config, which records configuration changes to resources over time and evaluates them against rules that the team defines.
K: B
G: ## AWS Health Dashboard
The AWS Health Dashboard shows the health of AWS services from two angles:
- **Service health**: the public status of AWS services in every Region, visible to anyone.
- **Your account health**: events that affect **your** account and resources, such as scheduled maintenance on specific EC2 instances, required updates, and ongoing service issues, listed with the affected resources.
It is available with every account (including Basic Support). Events can also be sent to Amazon EventBridge to trigger notifications or automation.
## Easily confused tools
- **AWS Trusted Advisor** looks at how you have configured your account (cost, security, fault tolerance, performance, service quotas) and recommends improvements.
- **AWS CloudTrail** records who did what (API activity).
- **AWS Config** records what your resources look like over time and checks them against rules.
W: Health Dashboard: its account view lists AWS events, such as scheduled maintenance and service issues, together with the specific resources in the account they affect, and can notify through EventBridge.
X: A=Trusted Advisor: it recommends improvements to the team's own configuration. It does not report AWS maintenance events or AWS service issues.
C=CloudTrail: it logs actions taken through the API by users and services. AWS's maintenance plans and service events are not API calls in the account.
D=Config: it tracks how the team's resources are configured and whether they follow rules. It has no knowledge of AWS-side maintenance or outages.
T: AWS events that affect your resources (maintenance, service issues) appear in the AWS Health Dashboard; best-practice checks are Trusted Advisor.
V: AWS Health Dashboard, AWS Trusted Advisor, Amazon EventBridge

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.3 applied use-case
S: A company wants to use a third-party log analytics product on AWS. It wants to subscribe to it, deploy it into its own account, and have the software charges appear on its existing AWS bill instead of signing a separate contract with the vendor. Where should it look?
A: AWS Marketplace, a curated catalog where customers find, subscribe to, and deploy third-party software with charges billed through AWS.
B: AWS re:Post, a community question-and-answer site where AWS customers, partners, and AWS experts answer technical questions.
C: AWS Service Catalog, which lets administrators publish a set of approved products that users in their organization can launch.
D: AWS Partner Solutions Finder, which helps customers search for AWS Partners that offer consulting services and solutions.
K: A
G: ## Where to get help and products from outside your team
- **AWS Marketplace**: a digital catalog of third-party software, data and services. You can subscribe with a few clicks, deploy into your account (for example as an AMI, container or SaaS product), and pay through your AWS bill. Many listings offer free trials and pay-as-you-go or contract pricing; some offer private offers negotiated with the seller.
- **AWS Partner Network (APN)**: companies that build on AWS or help customers use it. The **AWS Partner Solutions Finder** helps you find a partner, for example for migration consulting.
- **AWS Professional Services**: AWS's own consultants for large engagements.
- **AWS re:Post**: a community Q&A site with answers from customers, partners and AWS experts, plus Knowledge Center articles.
- **AWS Service Catalog**: your own internal catalog of approved products (often CloudFormation templates) that users can launch.

X: B=re:Post: it answers questions about using AWS. It does not sell software or put third-party charges on the AWS bill.
C=Service Catalog: it distributes products that the company's own administrators have already approved. It is not a place to buy software from outside vendors.
D=Partner Solutions Finder: it helps locate partners to work with, but engaging a consulting partner usually means a separate agreement, not a software subscription billed through AWS.
T: Find, subscribe to and pay for third-party software through your AWS bill with AWS Marketplace.
V: AWS Marketplace, AWS re:Post, AWS Service Catalog
