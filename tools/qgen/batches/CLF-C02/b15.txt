@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.1 advanced use-case
S: A tax preparation firm runs its client portal on eight on-premises servers sized for the three-month filing season; for the rest of the year average utilization stays below 15 percent. Leadership has frozen all capital purchases, and the two-person operations team cannot add or remove servers by hand during the peak. After the move to AWS, which approach gives the MOST cost-effective match between capacity and demand?
A: Run the portal in an EC2 Auto Scaling group whose target tracking policy adds instances in season and removes them afterward
B: Buy three-year Standard Reserved Instances for eight large instances so peak capacity is always billed at a discounted hourly rate
C: Move the portal to one very large EC2 instance sized for peak demand and stop it by hand on weekends outside the season
D: Keep the eight servers on premises and rent Dedicated Hosts on AWS that absorb overflow traffic only during the filing season
K: A
G: ## Elasticity: capacity that follows demand
On premises, a company buys servers for its **peak** and pays for them all year, even when they sit idle. In the cloud, **elasticity** means capacity is added when demand rises and released when it falls, and you pay only for what runs.
## How Auto Scaling delivers it
An EC2 Auto Scaling group keeps a fleet between a minimum and a maximum size. A **target tracking** policy watches a metric, such as average CPU at `50%`, and launches or terminates instances to stay near the target. No one has to add servers by hand.
## Applying it here
- Peak: three months at full size.
- Rest of the year: under 15 percent use, so most instances can be removed.
- No capital purchase: On-Demand instances are an operating expense billed per second or hour.
W: Auto Scaling with target tracking: the group grows for the filing season and shrinks afterward without manual work, so the firm pays for peak capacity only while the peak lasts and makes no capital purchase.
X: B=Peak-sized Reserved Instances: a three-year commitment for peak capacity bills all eight instances for nine quiet months as well, which repeats the on-premises waste at a discount.
C=One large instance: it still pays for peak capacity most of the year, manual stopping needs the very staff time the team lacks, and a single instance is a single point of failure.
D=On-premises plus Dedicated Hosts: the firm keeps paying for its own servers, and Dedicated Hosts are whole physical servers billed per host, an expensive way to absorb short overflow.
T: Elasticity through Auto Scaling turns peak-sized fixed capacity into capacity that grows and shrinks with demand.
V: Amazon EC2 Auto Scaling, Amazon EC2

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.1 advanced use-case
S: A mobile game studio in Canada will launch its game in Brazil next month. Its real-time match servers run on Amazon EC2 and are fully defined in AWS CloudFormation templates. Players in Brazil need low round-trip latency to those match servers, and the studio has no staff, contracts, or facilities in South America. Which approach BEST uses the global reach of AWS to meet these constraints?
A: Deploy the same CloudFormation templates in the South America (Sao Paulo) Region so the match servers run close to the players
B: Put Amazon CloudFront in front of the Canadian match servers so edge locations in Brazil cache the real-time game traffic for players
C: Order an AWS Outposts rack for a colocation facility in Sao Paulo and run the match servers on it as an extension of Canada
D: Add AWS Global Accelerator in front of the Canadian match servers so Brazilian players enter the AWS network at a nearby edge
K: A
G: ## Going global in minutes
AWS runs Regions on several continents. Because the infrastructure already exists, a company can deploy in another part of the world without buying hardware, signing leases, or hiring local staff. With infrastructure as code, the same template that built the stack in one Region builds an identical stack in another.
## Latency depends on where compute runs
Real-time game traffic is interactive: every move goes to the server and back. Caching cannot help with it, and network optimizations shorten only part of the path. The largest gain comes from running the servers **near the players**.
## Example
`sa-east-1` (South America, Sao Paulo) is a full Region. Launching the CloudFormation stack there puts the match servers a short network distance from players in Brazil.
W: Sao Paulo Region deployment: the existing templates recreate the match servers in a Region in Brazil within hours, so players get local round trips and the studio needs no facilities or local contracts.
X: B=CloudFront: it caches static and cacheable content at edge locations; real-time match traffic must still reach servers in Canada, so each move keeps the long round trip.
C=Outposts in colocation: Outposts are installed in a site the customer provides and runs, which needs exactly the facility contracts and local presence the studio does not have.
D=Global Accelerator: it improves the path by entering the AWS backbone early, but the servers stay in Canada, so the distance between Brazil and Canada still dominates the latency.
T: When players are far away, the global reach of AWS means running the workload in a Region near them, reusing the same templates.
V: AWS CloudFormation, Amazon EC2, AWS Global Infrastructure

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.2 advanced definition
S: An online bank's architecture review board asks each team to justify its design against the reliability pillar of the AWS Well-Architected Framework. One team's proposal replaces a single large payment server with many small instances, rehearses failover with injected faults every quarter, and adds capacity from live demand metrics instead of a yearly forecast. Which THREE reliability design principles does this proposal apply? (Select THREE)
A: Scale horizontally to increase aggregate workload availability, replacing one large resource with several smaller independent ones
B: Test recovery procedures, using automation to simulate failures and validate that the workload recovers before a real outage happens
C: Stop guessing capacity, monitoring demand and utilization so that resources are added or removed automatically to match the actual load
D: Implement a strong identity foundation, centralizing identity management and applying least privilege to every interaction with the workload
E: Adopt a consumption model, paying only for the computing resources the workload needs and raising or lowering usage with business requirements over time
F: Make frequent, small, reversible changes, deploying updates in small increments so that failed changes are easy to roll back quickly
K: A,B,C
G: ## The reliability pillar's design principles
The Well-Architected reliability pillar lists five design principles:
- **Automatically recover from failure**: monitor business KPIs and trigger automated recovery.
- **Test recovery procedures**: simulate failures in the cloud and prove recovery works.
- **Scale horizontally**: many small resources instead of one big one, so one failure has a small impact.
- **Stop guessing capacity**: watch demand and add or remove resources automatically.
- **Manage change through automation**: infrastructure changes are made and reviewed as automation.
## Matching the proposal
Many small instances = scale horizontally. Quarterly fault injection = test recovery procedures. Capacity from live metrics = stop guessing capacity.
## Other pillars use similar wording
Identity foundation belongs to security, consumption model to cost optimization, and small reversible changes to operational excellence. Knowing which pillar owns a principle is part of the exam.
W: A=Scale horizontally: replacing one large payment server with many small instances removes the single point of failure, which is exactly this principle.
B=Test recovery procedures: quarterly injected faults validate failover before a real outage, which is how the pillar describes testing recovery.
C=Stop guessing capacity: adding capacity from live demand metrics instead of a yearly forecast avoids saturation, the cause the pillar names.
X: D=Strong identity foundation: a real Well-Architected principle, but it belongs to the security pillar, and nothing in the proposal concerns identities or privileges.
E=Consumption model: this is a cost optimization principle about paying for what you use; the proposal's goals are availability and recovery.
F=Small reversible changes: this operational excellence principle concerns how deployments are made; the proposal does not change its release process.
T: Reliability principles: recover automatically, test recovery, scale horizontally, stop guessing capacity, and manage change through automation.
V: AWS Well-Architected Framework

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.1 advanced use-case
S: A nonprofit hosts its fundraising campaign site, which is only static HTML, images, and JavaScript, on a single EC2 instance in one Availability Zone. The site went offline twice during major campaigns when the instance failed. The nonprofit has no operations staff and a tight budget, and it wants the site to stay highly available without managing any servers. Which approach meets these requirements BEST?
A: Host the files in Amazon S3, which stores objects across multiple Availability Zones, and serve them through Amazon CloudFront
B: Move the site to a larger EC2 instance type with more memory and CPU so that it can absorb the heavy traffic peaks of each campaign
C: Keep the single EC2 instance and take hourly Amazon EBS snapshots so that a failed server can be rebuilt quickly by hand
D: Add a second EC2 instance in the same Availability Zone and switch between the two servers by updating DNS records by hand
K: A
G: ## High availability as a cloud benefit
High availability means a system keeps serving users when a component fails. On premises it requires duplicate hardware, sites, and staff. On AWS, many managed services are **highly available by design**, so a small team gets redundancy without building it.
## Static sites need no servers
A site made only of HTML, images, and JavaScript needs no application server. Amazon S3 can host it:
- S3 Standard stores objects redundantly across **multiple Availability Zones**, so the loss of one zone does not take the content offline.
- **Amazon CloudFront** caches the files at edge locations near visitors, absorbs traffic peaks, and adds HTTPS.
- There is no instance to patch, resize, or replace.
## The trap
Bigger servers, snapshots, or a second server in the same zone keep a single point of failure or need manual recovery.
W: S3 with CloudFront: the content is stored across several Availability Zones and served from edge caches, so it stays available through instance or zone failures with no servers to run.
X: B=Larger instance: a bigger single instance in one zone still fails as one unit, so the outages during campaigns would continue.
C=Hourly EBS snapshots: snapshots help rebuild after a failure, but recovery is manual and the site is down until someone rebuilds it, and the nonprofit has no operations staff.
D=Second instance in the same zone: both servers share the zone's fate, and switching DNS by hand is slow manual work rather than automatic recovery.
T: For a static site that must stay available with no operations staff, use Amazon S3 with CloudFront instead of a single server.
V: Amazon S3, Amazon CloudFront

@ CLF-C02 D1 T1.4 advanced use-case
S: A media company plans to move 200 virtual machines to Amazon EC2. Its first cost estimate copied each VM's on-premises CPU and memory allocation, which the IT team set years ago for worst-case peaks; monitoring shows most VMs average under 20 percent CPU. The CFO wants an estimate that reflects real need without risking performance after go-live. Which approach should the team take?
A: Size instances from measured utilization, then use AWS Compute Optimizer after migration to adjust any remaining mismatches
B: Keep the like-for-like sizes and buy three-year Standard Reserved Instances for them so the larger instances cost less every hour
C: Choose the largest instance in each family for every VM so performance after go-live is guaranteed and resizing is never needed
D: Map every VM to the smallest burstable instance and rely on unlimited mode to absorb every peak, whatever the sustained load is
K: A
G: ## Rightsizing as a cost lever
On premises, servers are sized for the worst case because adding capacity later is slow. In the cloud, instance size can change in minutes, so the economical approach is to size for **measured** need and adjust as data arrives.
## Before and after migration
- **Before**: use utilization data (CPU, memory, I/O over weeks) to pick instance types, not the allocation someone chose years ago.
- **After**: AWS Compute Optimizer analyzes configuration and utilization metrics of running resources and recommends rightsizing or flags idle resources.
## Example
A VM allocated 16 vCPUs that averages 15 percent CPU may run well on an instance with 4 vCPUs. Multiplied across 200 VMs, like-for-like sizing would roughly quadruple compute cost for no benefit.
W: Measured sizing plus Compute Optimizer: the estimate follows actual utilization, and after go-live Compute Optimizer's rightsizing recommendations catch instances that are still too large or too small.
X: B=Like-for-like with RIs: a discount on oversized instances still pays for capacity that sits idle, and three-year commitments lock that waste in.
C=Largest instance in each family: this inflates cost even further and ignores the monitoring data that shows the VMs are mostly idle.
D=Smallest burstable everywhere: unlimited mode charges for sustained bursting, and VMs with real sustained load would perform poorly or cost more than a proper size.
T: Size cloud instances from measured utilization and keep adjusting with rightsizing recommendations instead of copying on-premises allocations.
V: AWS Compute Optimizer, Amazon EC2

The compliance lead must name who performs the destruction and where to obtain independent proof for the auditor. Which answer is correct?

S: A 12-person software startup is launching its first production workload on AWS and uses only Basic Support today. K: A
G: ## What Basic Support covers
Every account has Basic Support: customer service for account and billing questions, documentation, AWS re:Post, AWS Health, and **core** Trusted Advisor checks. It includes no technical support cases with engineers.
## Business Support+
AWS calls Business Support+ the minimum recommended plan for production workloads. It adds:
- AI-powered contextual assistance and **24/7 phone, web, and chat** access to Cloud Support Engineers.
- Under 30 minutes for business-critical system down cases.
- **Third-party software support** for EC2 operating systems and popular software components.
- The **full set of Trusted Advisor checks** and the AWS Support API.
## Higher and retiring plans
Enterprise Support adds a designated TAM, which this startup does not need. Developer Support ends on January 1, 2027, and AWS directs its customers to Business Support+.
W: Business Support+: it is the lowest-cost plan with 24/7 engineer access by phone, web, and chat, third-party software help, and all Trusted Advisor checks.
X: B=Developer Support: AWS is retiring it on January 1, 2027 and points its customers to Business Support+; it also never offered 24/7 phone and chat access.
C=Basic Support: it gives only core Trusted Advisor checks and no technical cases with engineers, so most requirements are missed.
D=Enterprise Support: it meets the needs but adds a designated TAM and a much higher minimum price that the startup does not need.
T: For 24/7 engineer access, third-party software help, and full Trusted Advisor checks at the lowest cost, choose Business Support+.
