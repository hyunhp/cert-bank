@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.1 applied comparison
S: A logistics company runs a steady fleet of Amazon EC2 instances and wants a three-year Reserved Instance discount. Its engineers expect to move the fleet to a newer instance family and possibly a different operating system during the term. Which Reserved Instance type fits best?
A: Standard Reserved Instances, which can be listed for sale in the Reserved Instance Marketplace and changed to a different instance family at any time.
B: Convertible Reserved Instances, which can be exchanged during the term for others with a different instance family, operating system, or tenancy.
C: Zonal Standard Reserved Instances, which reserve capacity in one Availability Zone and follow the fleet automatically to any new instance family.
D: Scheduled Reserved Instances, which reserve capacity for a recurring daily or weekly time window and convert to any family at the end of the window.
K: B
G: ## Two kinds of Reserved Instances
A Reserved Instance (RI) is a billing discount for committing to an EC2 configuration for one or three years. It is not a separate kind of server: a running On-Demand instance that matches the RI's attributes simply gets the lower rate.
- **Standard RIs**: the larger discount. You can **modify** some attributes (for example the Availability Zone, the scope, or the size within the same family for Linux), and you can sell unused Standard RIs in the **Reserved Instance Marketplace**. You cannot exchange them for a different instance family.
- **Convertible RIs**: a smaller discount, but you can **exchange** them during the term for Convertible RIs with a different **instance family, operating system, tenancy or payment option**, as long as the new reservation is of equal or greater value. They cannot be sold in the Marketplace.
## Choosing between them
Example: a company commits to `m5` instances for three years. If it later wants `m7g`, a Standard RI would stop matching, while a Convertible RI can be exchanged for the new family.
Rule of thumb: stable, well-known configuration means Standard; expected changes in family or OS mean Convertible (or a Savings Plan if the company wants the flexibility applied automatically).
W: Convertible Reserved Instances: the exchange feature exists exactly for this case. The company keeps a three-year discount and can swap the reservation for a newer family or another operating system when the migration happens.
X: A=Standard Reserved Instances: they can be sold in the Reserved Instance Marketplace and modified within limits, but they cannot be exchanged for a different instance family, which is the change the company plans.
C=Zonal Standard RIs: a zonal RI does reserve capacity in one Availability Zone, but its discount only applies to the instance type it was bought for. It does not follow the fleet to a new family.
D=Scheduled RIs: they were for recurring part-time windows and are no longer offered for purchase. They never converted to other families, and the fleet here runs steadily, not in windows.
T: A Reserved Instance discount that must survive a change of instance family or operating system calls for Convertible Reserved Instances.
V: Amazon EC2 Reserved Instances

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.1 applied definition
S: A retailer is buying three-year Reserved Instances for the same EC2 instance type and wants the largest possible discount on the total cost. Cash flow is not a concern. Which payment option should it choose?
A: No Upfront, which spreads the whole cost of the reservation over monthly payments and gives the largest discount because no money is paid in advance.
B: Partial Upfront, which splits the cost between an upfront payment and a reduced hourly rate and always gives the largest discount of the three options.
C: All Upfront, which pays for the entire term at the start and gives the largest discount of the three payment options for the same term and type.
D: Monthly Upfront, which charges the full hourly rate at the start of each month and gives the largest discount once the reservation is fully used.
K: C
G: ## Reserved Instance payment options
For the same instance type, Region and term, a Reserved Instance can be paid in three ways:
- **All Upfront**: pay the whole term at purchase; no hourly charges afterward. **Largest discount.**
- **Partial Upfront**: pay part at purchase and the rest as a discounted hourly rate over the term. Middle discount.
- **No Upfront**: pay nothing at purchase and a discounted hourly rate over the term. Smallest of the three discounts, but no cash outlay at the start.
## Term matters too
A three-year term gives a larger discount than a one-year term for the same option. So "three years, All Upfront" is the cheapest overall way to reserve a given configuration, while "one year, No Upfront" is the most flexible on cash.
Savings Plans use the same three payment options and the same principle: the more you pay in advance and the longer you commit, the bigger the discount.
W: All Upfront: paying for the whole three-year term at the start gives AWS the most certainty and the customer the deepest discount of the three payment options. Since cash flow is not a concern, it is the cheapest choice.
X: A=No Upfront: it avoids any payment at purchase, but in exchange the discount is the smallest of the three options, so the total cost over three years is the highest.
B=Partial Upfront: it sits between the other two options. It gives a better discount than No Upfront but a smaller one than All Upfront, so it is never the largest.
D=Monthly Upfront: there is no payment option with this name. Reserved Instances offer only All Upfront, Partial Upfront and No Upfront.
T: For the same term and instance type, All Upfront gives the largest Reserved Instance discount and No Upfront the smallest.
V: Amazon EC2 Reserved Instances

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.1 applied use-case
S: A sports streaming service must be sure that 40 specific EC2 instances can launch in one Availability Zone during a two-week tournament. It does not want any one-year or three-year commitment. Which option meets this need?
A: A Compute Savings Plan for the two weeks, which lowers the hourly rate of the 40 instances and guarantees that they can launch in the chosen zone.
B: Regional Reserved Instances for the instance type, which apply a discount anywhere in the Region and hold the capacity in each of its zones.
C: An On-Demand Capacity Reservation for the zone, which holds the capacity for 40 instances for as long as it is active and can be canceled anytime.
D: Spot Instances requested two weeks in advance, which lock the capacity for the whole tournament at a fixed discounted price set at request time.
K: C
G: ## Discounts and capacity are different things
Pricing options answer "how much will I pay?". Capacity options answer "will the instances be available when I launch them?". Most discount models do not reserve capacity.
## On-Demand Capacity Reservations
An **On-Demand Capacity Reservation** reserves EC2 capacity for a chosen instance type in a **specific Availability Zone**:
- No term commitment: create it when needed and cancel it when done.
- Billed at the **On-Demand rate whether or not instances run** in it, so unused reservations still cost money.
- Can be combined with Savings Plans or regional Reserved Instances to get a discount on the reserved capacity.
## How other options compare
- **Savings Plans** and **regional RIs**: discount only, no capacity guarantee.
- **Zonal RIs**: discount plus capacity in one zone, but they require a one- or three-year term.
- **Spot**: spare capacity that AWS can reclaim with a two-minute warning.
W: On-Demand Capacity Reservation: it guarantees the 40 instances in the chosen Availability Zone for exactly as long as the company keeps it, with no long-term commitment, and is canceled after the tournament.
X: A=Compute Savings Plan: Savings Plans only lower the rate, never reserve capacity, and the shortest term is one year, so they fail both requirements.
B=Regional Reserved Instances: they need a one- or three-year term and give a discount across the Region, but regional RIs do not reserve capacity in any zone.
D=Spot Instances: Spot uses spare capacity that AWS can reclaim with a two-minute notice, and the price varies with supply and demand. It guarantees neither capacity nor a fixed price.
T: Guaranteed EC2 capacity in one Availability Zone without a term commitment is an On-Demand Capacity Reservation.
V: Amazon EC2 On-Demand Capacity Reservations

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.1 applied comparison
S: A government contractor's compliance rules require that its EC2 instances never run on physical hardware shared with other AWS customers. It has no licenses tied to sockets or cores and does not want to allocate or manage individual physical servers. Which option fits best?
A: Dedicated Hosts, which give the contractor full physical servers to allocate, track and place instances on, with visibility into their sockets and cores.
B: Spot Instances, which run on spare capacity that AWS reserves for each customer and therefore never share hardware with another account.
C: On-Demand Capacity Reservations, which hold capacity in one Availability Zone on hardware that AWS sets aside for the contractor alone.
D: Dedicated Instances, which run on hardware dedicated to one customer while AWS decides placement, with no physical servers to allocate or track.
K: D
G: ## Tenancy options on EC2
By default, EC2 instances use **shared tenancy**: instances from different customers can run on the same physical server, isolated by the hypervisor. Two options give single-customer hardware:
- **Dedicated Instances**: instances run on hardware dedicated to one AWS account. AWS chooses the host. You get isolation, but no visibility into or control over the physical server.
- **Dedicated Hosts**: you allocate whole physical servers and place instances on them. You see sockets and physical cores and can keep instances on the same host over time, which supports **bring-your-own-license** software priced per socket or core. You also manage the host capacity.
## Choosing
Need isolation only: Dedicated Instances. Need isolation plus host-level visibility or control (licensing, host affinity): Dedicated Hosts. Both cost more than shared tenancy.
W: Dedicated Instances: they meet the "no hardware shared with other customers" rule while AWS handles placement, so the contractor gets isolation without allocating or tracking physical servers it does not need.
X: A=Dedicated Hosts: they also give isolation, but they add host allocation and capacity management that the contractor explicitly wants to avoid, and their main extra benefit, socket and core visibility for licensing, is not needed here.
B=Spot Instances: Spot is a pricing model for spare capacity in shared tenancy by default. It does not reserve hardware for one customer and can be interrupted.
C=Capacity Reservations: they guarantee that capacity is available in a zone, but with default tenancy the instances still run on shared hardware, so they do not satisfy the isolation rule.
T: Single-customer hardware without managing hosts is Dedicated Instances; add host-level visibility and BYOL needs and it becomes Dedicated Hosts.
V: Amazon EC2 Dedicated Instances, Amazon EC2 Dedicated Hosts

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.1 applied definition
S: A data science team is reviewing how AWS charges for data transfer for Amazon EC2. Which TWO types of data transfer are generally free of charge? (Select TWO)
A: Data transferred into AWS from the internet, such as files uploaded to an EC2 instance from a user's laptop.
B: Data transferred out from an EC2 instance to users on the internet, such as downloads served by a web server.
C: Data transferred between EC2 instances in two different AWS Regions, such as copying a database for disaster recovery.
D: Data transferred between EC2 instances in the same Availability Zone over their private IP addresses within a VPC.
E: Data transferred between EC2 instances in two Availability Zones of one Region, such as replication between zones.
K: A,D
G: ## The general rules of AWS data transfer pricing
Data transfer is billed per GB, and the price depends on **where the data goes**:
- **Into AWS from the internet**: free.
- **Out of AWS to the internet**: charged per GB, with lower rates at higher volumes. This is usually the biggest data transfer line on a bill.
- **Between Regions**: charged per GB, because the data crosses AWS's global network between Regions.
- **Between Availability Zones in the same Region**: charged per GB (for EC2, in each direction), but at a lower rate than internet or inter-Region traffic.
- **Within the same Availability Zone over private IP addresses**: free.
## Why it matters
Architecture choices drive this bill. Example: an application tier in one zone that sends large amounts of data to a cache in another zone pays inter-AZ charges on every request, while the same traffic inside one zone over private IPs costs nothing. High availability across zones is still usually worth the small charge; the point is to know where the costs come from.
W: A=Inbound from the internet: AWS does not charge for data coming into its network from the internet, so uploads to the instance cost nothing for transfer.
D=Same AZ, private IP: traffic between instances in the same Availability Zone using private IP addresses is free, which is why chatty components are sometimes placed close together.
X: B=Outbound to the internet: data leaving AWS to the internet is charged per GB and is often the largest data transfer cost for web workloads.
C=Between Regions: inter-Region transfer is charged per GB, so cross-Region copies for disaster recovery add a transfer cost on top of storage.
E=Between Availability Zones: traffic between zones in one Region is charged per GB in each direction, although at a lower rate than internet or inter-Region transfer.
T: Data in from the internet and traffic within one Availability Zone over private IPs are free; out to the internet, between Regions and between zones are charged.
V: Amazon EC2, Amazon VPC

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.1 applied use-case
S: A law firm turns on Amazon S3 Cross-Region Replication to copy its document bucket from one AWS Region to another for disaster recovery. Besides storage costs in the second Region, which new charge should the firm expect?
A: A data transfer charge per GB for the data copied from the source Region to the destination Region, plus charges for the replication requests.
B: A data transfer charge per GB for the data arriving in the destination Region, because AWS charges for all inbound traffic between Regions.
C: A fixed monthly fee for each Region that holds a copy of the bucket, charged whether or not any objects are replicated during the month.
D: A per-GB charge for the data moved between Availability Zones inside the source Region, because replication always crosses zones first.
K: A
G: ## What cross-Region copies cost
Copying data between Regions is a common disaster recovery pattern. It adds three kinds of cost:
- **Storage** in the destination Region (the second copy).
- **Inter-Region data transfer**: each GB copied out of the source Region to another Region is billed per GB.
- **Requests**: replication writes objects into the destination bucket, and those requests are billed like other S3 requests.
## Common misconceptions
- AWS does not charge a fee simply for using a Region; you pay for the resources and traffic you create there.
- S3 already stores data redundantly across multiple Availability Zones in a Region for most storage classes, and you are not billed separately for that internal redundancy.
- Charges for transfer between Regions are applied on the sending side, not as an "inbound" fee in the receiving Region.
Example: replicating 2 TB of new documents per month means paying inter-Region transfer on 2 TB, the replication requests, and storage for the 2 TB copy.
W: Inter-Region transfer and requests: every byte replicated leaves the source Region for another Region, which is billed per GB, and each replicated object generates write requests in the destination bucket.
X: B=Inbound charge at destination: the transfer charge is applied to data leaving the source Region, not as a fee for data arriving. AWS does not bill inbound traffic in the destination Region for replication.
C=Monthly fee per Region: AWS does not charge for using a Region itself. The firm pays only for storage, requests and transfer that it actually uses there.
D=Inter-AZ charge in the source: S3's built-in multi-zone redundancy is part of the storage price. Replication is billed as inter-Region transfer, not as zone-to-zone traffic.
T: Copying data to another Region adds per-GB inter-Region data transfer (and request) charges on top of storage in the second Region.
V: Amazon S3

G: ## Kinds of AWS Free Tier offers
AWS has offered free usage in several forms, and exam questions test whether you can tell them apart:
- **Always free**: a monthly free amount of selected services that does not expire, for example a number of AWS Lambda requests and compute time each month, or a small amount of Amazon DynamoDB storage. Usage above the free amount is billed normally.
- **Short-term free trials**: some services offer a trial that starts when you first use the service and lasts a limited time.

Free Tier amounts are limits, not caps: going beyond them is charged. AWS Budgets and Free Tier usage alerts in the Billing and Cost Management console help a learner notice before that happens.

C=Promotional credits: credits are a fixed balance that is used up or expires. They are how new accounts start, but they are not the recurring always free allowance.
D=First-year discounts: there is no first-year discount on every service. The former "12 months free" tier gave limited free usage of selected services, not discounts on all of them.
T: "Always free" means a recurring monthly free allowance of selected services that does not expire; trials and credits are time-limited.

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.1 applied comparison
S: A museum builds an audio guide API that receives a few thousand requests on some days and almost none on others. It is comparing an always-running EC2 instance with AWS Lambda. How is the Lambda cost calculated?
A: By the number of hours each function stays deployed in the account, at a fixed hourly rate that applies whether or not the function receives requests.
B: By a flat monthly price per function that includes unlimited requests, with the price set by the memory size chosen when the function is created.
C: By the number of vCPUs reserved for each function every hour, at a rate that applies even when the function is idle and receives no requests.
D: By the number of requests plus the compute duration of each invocation, which is measured in milliseconds and priced by the memory configured.
K: D
G: ## Pay-per-use pricing in serverless services
Serverless services charge for **what you actually use**, not for capacity that sits idle.
- **AWS Lambda**: a charge **per request** plus a charge for **duration**, measured in milliseconds and priced according to the **memory** you configure (often expressed in GB-seconds). No requests means no request or duration charges.
- **Amazon DynamoDB on-demand**: charged per read and write request plus storage.
- **AWS Fargate**: charged for the vCPU and memory a task requests, for the time it runs.
- **Amazon S3**: charged for GB stored per month, requests and data transfer out.
## Comparing with EC2
An EC2 instance is billed for every second it runs, whether it serves one request or thousands. For spiky or low traffic, Lambda's per-request and per-millisecond pricing usually costs less. For constant heavy load, an always-on instance can be cheaper, so the comparison depends on the usage pattern.
W: Requests plus duration: Lambda bills each invocation and the time it runs in milliseconds at a rate set by the memory size, so on the quiet days the museum pays almost nothing.
X: A=Hours deployed: deploying a function costs nothing by itself. Lambda has no hourly charge for a function that is waiting for requests.
B=Flat monthly price: Lambda has no per-function subscription. The memory setting affects the price per millisecond of execution, not a monthly fee.
C=Reserved vCPUs per hour: Lambda does not bill for idle vCPU capacity. That model resembles an always-on instance, which is what the museum wants to compare against.
T: AWS Lambda is billed per request and per millisecond of execution, scaled by configured memory; idle functions cost nothing.
V: AWS Lambda, Amazon EC2

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.1 applied definition
S: A finance analyst is listing the AWS pricing models that require the company to commit to a one-year or three-year term in exchange for a lower price. Which TWO models belong on the list? (Select TWO)

B: On-Demand Capacity Reservations, which reserve EC2 capacity in a chosen Availability Zone and can be canceled at any time.

G: ## Commitment-based versus no-commitment pricing
AWS compute pricing falls into two groups:
- **Commitment-based (one or three years)**:

  - **On-Demand**: full price, maximum flexibility.

A commitment lets AWS plan its capacity, so it passes some of the savings back. The trade-off is that the customer pays for the commitment whether or not it uses it.

D=Reserved Instances: they require a one- or three-year term for a specific instance configuration in exchange for a discount compared to On-Demand.

B=Capacity Reservations: they have no term commitment. They are billed at On-Demand rates while active and can be canceled at any time.
E=On-Demand Instances: the defining feature of On-Demand is the absence of a commitment; the company pays only while instances run.

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.1 applied troubleshooting
S: A software company bought a Compute Savings Plan with a commitment of $20 per hour. During a product launch, its eligible compute usage reached $35 per hour at Savings Plans rates. How is the extra usage above the commitment billed?
A: It is billed at regular On-Demand rates, because a Savings Plan only discounts eligible usage up to the hourly commitment amount.
B: It is blocked by AWS until the next hour, because a Savings Plan sets a hard limit on how much compute the account can use per hour.
C: It is billed at Spot prices, because usage beyond a Savings Plan commitment is automatically moved to spare capacity in the Region.
D: It is added to the commitment for the rest of the term, because the plan raises the hourly amount to match the account's peak usage.
K: A
G: ## How a Savings Plan is applied each hour
A Savings Plan is a commitment to spend a fixed amount per hour, measured at Savings Plans rates, for one or three years.
Every hour, AWS applies the plan to eligible usage:
- Usage **up to the commitment** gets the Savings Plans rate.
- Usage **above the commitment** is billed at normal **On-Demand rates**.
- If usage is **below** the commitment, the company still pays the full commitment for that hour; the unused part is lost.
## Example
Commitment: `$20/hour`. Usage worth `$35/hour` at Savings Plans rates. The first `$20` is covered by the plan; the remaining usage is charged On-Demand. Nothing is blocked, and the commitment does not change.
## Sizing a plan
Because unused commitment is wasted and extra usage is simply charged On-Demand, companies usually commit to their steady baseline and leave peaks On-Demand. Cost Explorer and the Savings Plans recommendations in the Billing and Cost Management console suggest a commitment from past usage.
W: On-Demand for the excess: the plan covers eligible usage up to $20 per hour at the discounted rate, and anything above that is billed at normal On-Demand prices, with no interruption to the workload.
X: B=Hard limit: a Savings Plan is a billing discount, not a usage cap. AWS does not stop or throttle compute when the commitment is exceeded.
C=Moved to Spot: Savings Plans never change how instances run. Spot is a separate purchasing option chosen by the customer, not an overflow for extra usage.
D=Commitment grows: the hourly commitment is fixed for the whole term when the plan is bought. Buying more coverage requires purchasing an additional plan.
T: Usage beyond a Savings Plan's hourly commitment is billed at On-Demand rates; usage below it still costs the full commitment.
V: Savings Plans

@ CLF-C02 D4 T4.2 applied use-case
S: A nonprofit has a monthly AWS budget of $2,000. Its treasurer wants an email warning when AWS projects that the month will end above $2,000, before the money is actually spent. Which solution meets this requirement?
A: An AWS Budgets cost budget of $2,000 with an alert on forecasted spend, which emails the treasurer when the projected month-end cost exceeds the limit.
B: An AWS Cost Explorer report saved with a $2,000 filter, which emails the treasurer automatically whenever the current spending passes the limit.
C: An AWS Trusted Advisor cost check set to $2,000, which emails the treasurer when it predicts that idle resources will push spending over the limit.
D: An AWS Pricing Calculator estimate with a $2,000 ceiling, which emails the treasurer when actual usage in the account grows beyond the estimate.
K: A
G: ## AWS Budgets
AWS Budgets lets you set a planned amount and get alerted when you approach or exceed it.
- **Budget types**: cost, usage, Reserved Instance and Savings Plans utilization or coverage.
- **Alert on actual or forecasted amounts**: an **actual** alert fires after spending crosses the threshold; a **forecasted** alert fires when AWS projects that the period will end above it, giving earlier warning.
- **Notifications**: email recipients or an Amazon SNS topic (which can fan out to chat or other systems).
- **Budget actions**: optionally apply a policy or stop instances when a threshold is reached.
## Example

G: ## Reporting abuse of AWS resources
Sometimes the problem is not in your account: someone else's AWS resources are used for spam, phishing, malware hosting, denial of service attacks or other abuse.
## The AWS Trust & Safety team
- AWS Trust & Safety handles reports that **AWS resources are being used for abusive or illegal purposes**.
- Anyone can report, including people without an AWS account, using the abuse reporting form or the abuse contact email listed by AWS.
