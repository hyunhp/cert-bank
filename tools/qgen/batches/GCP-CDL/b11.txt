@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.1 applied use-case
S: A manufacturer closes two data centers and moves its applications to Google Cloud. At the board meeting the CFO asks why the line "equipment purchases" has almost disappeared from the budget while a recurring "cloud services" line has grown. Which explanation is correct?
A: Spending moved from CapEx to OpEx, because the company no longer buys and depreciates hardware up front and instead pays for the cloud resources it consumes as it uses them
B: Spending moved from OpEx to CapEx, because the company now pays Google a large up-front amount for the cloud services and then depreciates that payment over several years of use
C: Spending was eliminated entirely, because the cloud provider absorbs the full cost of running the applications and the company only pays a fee when something fails
D: Spending stayed in CapEx, because the company still has to buy and own the network equipment and servers that the cloud applications run on in Google's data centers
K: A
G: ## CapEx and OpEx
- **Capital expenditure (CapEx)** is money spent up front to acquire assets such as servers, storage arrays and network gear. The cost is usually spread over the asset's useful life through depreciation.
- **Operational expenditure (OpEx)** is money spent as the business operates, for example power, support contracts or services consumed each month.
Google's Well-Architected Framework describes the difference: on-premises IT includes both CapEx and OpEx, with acquired hardware and software depreciated over its operating life, while in the cloud the cost of most resources is treated as OpEx and is incurred as the resources are consumed. (The CapEx/OpEx definitions are generally accepted accounting terms; the cloud-side statement comes from the Well-Architected cost pillar.)
## Why a CFO cares
- No large purchase has to be approved years before demand is known.
- Cost follows usage, so a quiet quarter costs less than a busy one.
- The trade-off is that spend is variable, so it needs visibility and governance, which the rest of this topic covers.
W: CapEx to OpEx shift: the manufacturer stops buying and depreciating hardware and pays for cloud resources as it consumes them, which is exactly why the equipment line shrank and the services line is recurring.
X: B=OpEx to CapEx: this reverses the shift. Cloud resources are generally billed as they are consumed, not paid up front and depreciated.
C=Cost eliminated: the company still pays for what it uses. The change is in how and when it pays, not that the cost disappears.
D=Still CapEx: Google owns and runs the underlying servers and network, so the manufacturer has no hardware purchase to capitalize for these workloads.
T: Moving to the cloud turns up-front hardware CapEx into consumption-based OpEx.

@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.1 applied comparison
S: A retailer compares keeping its servers on premises with moving to Google Cloud. The finance team proposes comparing only the purchase price of the servers against the first-year cloud invoice. The CTO objects that this misses the real total cost of ownership (TCO). Which TWO cost items belong in an on-premises TCO that the proposal leaves out? (Select TWO)
A: Data center space, power and cooling needed to keep the servers running
B: The marketing budget the retailer already spends on its seasonal campaigns
C: Staff time for patching, hardware replacement and routine maintenance of the servers
D: The revenue the retailer expects from online orders over the next year
E: The licence fee for the retailer's point-of-sale application, which is the same in both options
K: A,C
G: ## What TCO means
Total cost of ownership (TCO) is the full cost of owning and running an asset or service over its life, not only its purchase price. TCO is a generally accepted finance concept; no Google page defines it differently.
## What belongs in a fair comparison
- **Direct acquisition**: hardware, software and refresh cycles.
- **Running costs**: facilities, power, cooling, network, and the people who operate and maintain the environment.
- **Indirect costs**: idle capacity bought for peak demand, downtime and the time to provision new capacity.
Items that are the same in both options, or that are not costs of the infrastructure, do not change the comparison and are left out.
W: A=Facilities, power and cooling: these recurring costs come with owning servers and are missing from a purchase-price-only comparison, so they belong in the on-premises TCO.
C=Staff maintenance time: patching and replacing hardware is operating cost of ownership that the proposal ignores, so it must be counted.
X: B=Marketing budget: this spend is unrelated to where the servers run and would be identical under either option, so it adds nothing to the comparison.
D=Expected revenue: revenue is a benefit, not a cost of ownership. TCO compares costs, and revenue would be weighed separately in a business case.
E=Identical licence fee: a cost that is the same under both options does not distinguish them, so it does not change the TCO difference between the options.
T: TCO counts every cost of owning and operating the infrastructure, not only the purchase price.

@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.1 applied comparison
S: A company's engineering teams launch cloud resources freely, while finance only sees one combined invoice at month end and cannot tell which team caused a rise in spend. A consultant recommends adopting Google's FinOps approach. Which description of who manages cloud costs under that approach is correct?
A: Finance alone manages cloud costs, reviewing the invoice each month and ordering engineering to switch off any resources that the finance team considers wasteful
B: Engineering, finance and business teams share accountability for cloud spend, using real-time cost visibility so each team owns the cost of what it runs
C: A single cloud administrator makes all cost decisions on behalf of the whole company, so that no other team ever needs access to detailed billing data
D: Google Cloud sets each customer's spending limits automatically and enforces them, so no team inside the company needs to take ownership of cost at all
K: B
G: ## FinOps in Google's words
Google Cloud describes cloud FinOps (financial operations) as an operational framework and cultural practice that brings engineering, finance and business teams together to manage cloud spending and maximize business value. Because cloud consumption is variable, FinOps sets up shared financial accountability, supported by real-time cost visibility, resource optimization, accurate forecasting and trade-offs between speed, cost and performance.
## Cultural principles
- **Accountability**: teams are responsible for their own cost optimization.
- **Collaboration**: finance and engineering work as a single unit.
- **Cost transparency**: billing data is available to people at every level.
## Reading the scenario
The problem is that finance cannot see which team drives spend and engineering does not see the cost. Cost ownership has to be shared and transparent.
W: Shared accountability with visibility: FinOps brings engineering, finance and business together, so each team sees and owns the cost of its own resources, which fixes the one-combined-invoice problem.
X: A=Finance alone: this keeps engineering, who make the spending decisions, out of the cost conversation. FinOps asks finance and engineering to partner, not to police each other.
C=One administrator: concentrating cost decisions and hiding billing data contradicts the cost-transparency and team-accountability principles.
D=Google sets the limits: Google provides budgets, alerts and quotas as tools, but the customer decides its limits and owns its spend.
T: FinOps makes engineering, finance and business jointly accountable for cloud cost, backed by transparency.

@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.1 applied definition
S: A company wants to improve cloud cost control and sorts its planned actions into people, process and technology. Which action is an example of the process element?
A: Running a monthly review in which finance and engineering compare spend with forecasts and agree on follow-up actions
B: Training product teams to read their own cost reports and encouraging a culture of cost ownership
C: Turning on dashboards and automated budget alert emails that show spend per project to every team lead
D: Hiring a team of engineers to write a new in-house billing tool to replace the reports Google Cloud already offers
K: A
G: ## People, process and technology
Controlling cloud costs needs three things to work together, as Google's FinOps guidance and cost pillar suggest:
- **People**: skills, roles and a culture of cost awareness. Teams must know what their decisions cost and who is accountable.
- **Process**: repeatable routines such as budgeting, forecasting, regular cost reviews and approval or governance standards.
- **Technology**: the tools that provide visibility and automation, for example billing reports, budgets with alerts, and dashboards.
(The three-way grouping is a common change-management framing; the cost pillar's core principles of aligning spend with value, a culture of cost awareness and continuous optimization map onto it.)
## Sorting the examples
A recurring review with agreed actions is a routine, so it is process. Training and culture are people. Dashboards and alerts are tools, so technology.
W: Monthly review with forecasts and follow-ups: a repeatable routine that links spending to plans is the process element.
X: B=Training and culture: building skills and a sense of ownership concerns people, not a routine or a tool.
C=Dashboards and alert emails: these are tools that deliver visibility, so they belong to technology.
D=Building a custom billing tool: this is also technology, and it duplicates cost reports that Google Cloud already provides, so it is not an example of process.
T: People bring skills and ownership, process brings repeatable routines, technology brings visibility and automation.

@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.1 applied use-case
S: A finance director wants a single place where cost and usage trends are shown by service and by project, so that team leads can see where spend is rising and act before month end. The director does not want to build anything custom. Which approach fits?
A: Ask each team to estimate its own cloud spend by hand in a shared spreadsheet at the end of each quarter, then send the totals to the finance department
B: Use Google Cloud's built-in cost management tools, such as Cloud Billing reports and the FinOps hub, to view spend and usage trends
C: Wait for the monthly invoice to arrive and then ask the monitoring team to work out the costs of each team from application error rates
D: Move every workload into one single project so that the invoice shows one clear total for the whole company without any breakdown by team

Google Cloud provides tools that give cost visibility without custom building:
- **Cloud Billing reports** show costs and usage trends, which can be filtered and grouped, for example by project or service.
- **Budgets and alerts** (covered below) warn when spend crosses planned thresholds.
- **FinOps hub** is, in Google's words, a centralized space in the Google Cloud console to help start and manage FinOps practices.
Google's FinOps page also lists reporting dashboards and cost insights in tools such as Cloud Hub Optimize and Gemini Cloud Assist, which can answer cost questions such as which services cost the most.

Real-time transparency is one of the cultural principles of FinOps: teams cannot act on costs they cannot see until the invoice arrives.
W: Built-in cost tools: Cloud Billing reports and the FinOps hub show spend and usage trends by service and project in one place, with no custom build, so leads can act before month end.

@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.1 applied use-case
S: A bank wants to roll out one security and access policy for all 40 projects of its retail division, and have any project created later pick it up automatically. The central IT team does not want to configure each project by hand. Which part of the Google Cloud resource hierarchy should it use?
A: The individual resources inside each project, such as every virtual machine and every storage bucket, configuring the policy on each of them separately
B: A separate billing account for every project, since a billing account carries the security and access rules of the projects that are linked to it
C: A folder for the retail division that holds the projects, with the policy set at the folder so it flows down to them
D: A new parent project that contains the 40 existing projects as its children, with the policy applied once at that parent project
K: C
G: ## The resource hierarchy
Google Cloud organizes resources in a tree:
- **Resources** such as VMs, buckets and datasets are the things you use.
- **Projects** are the base container for resources. Every resource belongs to a project.
- **Folders** group projects (and other folders), for example by department or environment.
- **Organization node** is the root, representing the company.
## Why the hierarchy helps
IAM access policies and organization policies are attached at a level of the tree and flow down, so everything below inherits them. Granting or restricting something at a folder applies to all current and future projects and folders inside it.
## Reading the scenario
One policy for many projects, including new ones, is the job of a folder. Resources are too low, billing accounts do not hold access rules, and a project cannot contain other projects.
W: Folder for the division: a policy applied at the folder is inherited by every project in it, now and later, so central IT configures it once.
X: A=Individual resources: configuring each VM and bucket separately is the manual work the team wants to avoid and does not cover projects created later.
B=A billing account per project: a billing account handles who pays, not the access and security policy of the resources, and a separate one per project multiplies administration.
D=Project as a parent: projects hold resources and cannot contain other projects. Only folders and the organization node are parents of projects.
T: Attach policies at a folder or the organization so they are inherited by everything below.
V: Resource Manager, Cloud IAM

@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.1 applied use-case
S: A multinational has hundreds of projects owned by many teams and wants the new Google Cloud resource hierarchy to help with governance. Which TWO benefits does the hierarchy provide? (Select TWO)
A: Access control and policies set high in the hierarchy are inherited by the resources below, which keeps security and compliance consistent
B: Every project automatically receives identical built-in spending limits from its parent, so the company never needs to create budgets or quotas
C: A single organization-level view lets auditors and administrators see and review the projects, folders and resources in the company
D: Resources in different projects of the organization are blocked from communicating with each other under any circumstances once the hierarchy exists
E: Policies set on a folder are permanently locked once saved and cannot be changed by anyone, including the people who are responsible for them
K: A,C
G: ## What the hierarchy gives a business
- **Access control and inheritance**: IAM allow policies and organization policies attach to organization, folder or project, and flow down. Granting a role to a team at a folder gives access to all projects in it, so there is less manual setup.
- **Security and compliance**: central teams can set guardrails once at the top, for example restrictions that apply to every project, and children inherit them.
- **Visibility and auditing**: because everything hangs under one organization node, administrators and auditors have one structure in which to see what exists and who has access.
## What it does not do
The hierarchy does not set spending limits by itself (budgets and quotas do that) and does not isolate networks automatically.
W: A=Inheritance for consistent security: policies set high in the tree flow down, so security and compliance rules are applied consistently without per-project work.
C=Organization-wide visibility: a single root gives administrators and auditors one structure in which to review projects, folders, resources and their access policies.
X: B=Automatic cost limits: the hierarchy organizes resources and policy, but spending limits come from budgets and quotas that must be set up.
D=No communication between projects: projects are not network walls, and resources in different projects can communicate when allowed.
E=Unchangeable folder policies: policies can be changed by users who hold the right permissions, so inheritance is not a lock.
T: The hierarchy gives inherited access and policy plus organization-wide visibility for auditing.
V: Resource Manager

@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.1 applied troubleshooting
S: A team configures a budget for its project with alert threshold rules at 50, 90 and 100 percent. After the project passes 100 percent of the budget, the team is surprised that its applications keep running and no services have been shut off. What is the most likely explanation?
A: The budget was misconfigured, because a budget always disables the whole project and all its services once the final threshold is reached
B: Google Cloud ignores budgets for any project that uses more than one service, so the threshold alerts never applied to this project
C: Threshold rules only start working after an administrator manually approves each alert, so the project was not yet being watched by the budget
D: A budget with threshold rules is alerts-only: it notifies recipients as spend crosses percentages and does not itself stop resources
K: D
G: ## What budgets do
A Cloud Billing budget lets you track actual spend against a planned amount. You pick the scope (a billing account, projects, folders, services or labelled resources), the period and the amount, and then set **threshold rules**: when actual or forecasted cost crosses a percentage, alert emails go to the recipients you choose.
## What they do not do
Google's documentation describes alerts-only budgets: they send notifications to keep people informed and do not by default stop usage. (The documentation also mentions spend cap budgets for some services; for this exam, think of budgets as the alerting tool.) If a business wants action, a person or an automated process has to respond to the alert.
## Related tools
- **Quotas** restrict how much of a resource a project can use.
- **Reports** show where the money went.
Alerts warn, quotas limit, reports explain.
W: Alerts-only behaviour: threshold rules notify people as spend crosses a percentage, so nothing is shut off unless someone or an automated process acts on the alert.
X: A=Always disables the project: budgets do not stop usage by default, so continued running is expected behaviour, not a misconfiguration.
B=Ignored for multi-service projects: budgets can be scoped to a project, several services or the whole billing account, so nothing is ignored.
C=Approval needed: threshold alerts fire automatically when spend or forecast crosses the percentage, with no approval step.
T: Budget thresholds notify; they do not stop spending unless someone acts.
V: Cloud Billing budgets

@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.1 applied use-case
S: A startup is worried that a coding mistake, or stolen credentials, could create thousands of virtual machines overnight and produce a very large bill. It wants a built-in safeguard that limits how many resources a project can consume, not just a notification afterwards. Which control fits?
A: A weekly Cloud Billing report that the finance manager reads on Monday morning to look for anything unusual in the past week of spending
B: A resource quota on the project, which restricts how much of a resource it can use and blocks consumption beyond that
C: A longer approval workflow in which finance signs every invoice before the payment is made to Google
D: A larger billing account, since bigger accounts automatically refuse any request for more than the average level of resource use
K: B
G: ## Quotas as a guardrail
Google Cloud uses quotas to maintain fairness and reduce spikes in resource use. A quota restricts how much of a Google Cloud resource a project can use, such as the number of VMs or the rate of API requests. In most cases, when you try to consume more than the quota allows, the system blocks access to the resource and the task fails. Quotas generally apply at project level.
## Business use
- They limit the damage of a mistake or compromised credentials, because consumption stops at the cap.
- Teams can ask for higher quotas through the Cloud Quotas system when there is a real need.
## Compare with other controls
- **Budget alerts** tell you after spend rises.
- **Billing reports** explain spend after it has happened.
- **Quotas** limit consumption beforehand.
W: Resource quota: it caps what a project can consume and blocks requests beyond that, so a runaway script or stolen credentials cannot create unlimited resources.
X: A=Weekly report: a report is reviewed after the spend has occurred, and a Monday review would miss an overnight surge.
C=Invoice sign-off: approving payment happens after the usage is already billed, so it does not stop consumption.
D=Larger billing account: account size does not set limits; quotas are applied to projects and resources.
T: Quotas limit consumption in advance; budgets and reports only inform.
V: Cloud Quotas

@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.1 applied tradeoff
S: A media company runs a large video-rendering job every night. The job is split into many independent pieces that can be retried, and the deadline is the next morning, so a few interrupted pieces only cause a delay. The company wants to reduce compute cost. Which approach best balances cost and risk?
A: Run the job on Spot VMs, accepting that capacity may be reclaimed, because the work is fault-tolerant and restartable
B: Run the job on Spot VMs and also host the company's always-on customer payment service on them to save more
C: Buy a permanent fleet of standard VMs sized for the nightly peak, and leave them running all day and night to avoid any interruption
D: Cancel the nightly job and render each video only when a customer asks for it, regardless of how long that customer must then wait
K: A
G: ## Spot VMs
Spot VMs are virtual machines that use excess Compute Engine capacity. They have significant discounts compared with on-demand prices, but Compute Engine might stop or delete (preempt) them at any time to reclaim the capacity. Google recommends them for fault-tolerant workloads such as batch processing.
## The trade-off
- **Gain**: much lower cost for compute that would otherwise cost full price.
- **Risk**: interruption with little warning.
That trade is acceptable when the work can be split, retried and resumed, and a delay is harmless. It is not acceptable for services that must always be up.
## Reading the scenario
Independent, retryable pieces with a morning deadline are the textbook match for Spot VMs.
W: Spot VMs for restartable batch work: interrupted pieces are simply retried, so the company gains the discount and the risk is only a short delay.
X: B=Payment service on Spot: an always-on customer-facing service cannot tolerate sudden reclaim, so the saving is not worth the outages.
C=Always-on peak fleet: paying for peak capacity around the clock keeps the cost high and contradicts the aim of paying only for what is needed.
D=Cancel the nightly job: this hurts the business to avoid a risk that the job design already tolerates.
T: Spot VMs cut cost for fault-tolerant, restartable work and are a poor fit for always-on services.
V: Compute Engine, Spot VMs

@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.1 applied tradeoff
S: An AI research team needs accelerators (GPUs) for a training run that can start whenever capacity becomes available. Capacity for these high-demand resources is scarce, and the finance lead does not want to pay for reserved hardware that sits idle while the team waits. Which Google Cloud approach fits?
A: Buy GPUs outright, install them in the company's own server room and depreciate the purchase over several years
B: Ask for a permanent standard reservation of the maximum GPU capacity the team could ever need and keep it for all months of the year
C: Use the Dynamic Workload Scheduler, which provisions scarce GPUs and TPUs when available, so the team need not hold idle hardware
D: Run the training on Spot VMs only, since they are the cheapest option
K: C
G: ## Obtaining scarce capacity cost-effectively
Compute Engine offers provisioning models that trade immediacy, commitment and price:
- **Standard** VMs are created immediately and run until you stop them.
- **Spot** VMs use spare capacity at a discount but can be preempted at any time.
- **Flex-start** VMs, and future reservations in calendar mode, are provisioned through the **Dynamic Workload Scheduler (DWS)**. Google describes DWS as scheduling capacity based on resource availability and helping you obtain high-demand resources such as GPUs and TPUs.
## Why it suits this case
The team can start whenever capacity becomes available, so it can queue a request for a run window instead of paying for capacity held around the clock. That keeps the cost aligned with use.
W: Dynamic Workload Scheduler: it provisions requested capacity when it is available and helps obtain scarce GPUs and TPUs, so the team avoids paying for hardware held idle while it waits.
X: A=Buying GPUs: this is the CapEx model the team is avoiding, and it leaves the company with idle hardware and the burden of refreshing it.
B=Permanent maximum reservation: it guarantees capacity but pays for it whether or not it is used, which is the idle spend the finance lead wants to avoid.
D=Spot only: Spot VMs can be preempted at any time, so the claim that capacity is never reclaimed is false, and a long training run could be interrupted.
T: Dynamic Workload Scheduler lets teams obtain scarce accelerators when available without paying for idle reserved capacity.
V: Compute Engine, Dynamic Workload Scheduler

@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.2 applied use-case
S: A software company runs its application in production. Developers see that the application is slow and want to find which functions and lines of code use the most CPU and memory while real users are on the service. Which Google Cloud Observability tool should they use?
A: Cloud Profiler, which gathers CPU and memory use from production and ties it to the code that uses it
B: Error Reporting, which groups application crashes so the team can see how many different problems exist and whether each is new
C: Cloud Logging, which stores the text messages produced by the application and the platform
D: Cloud Trace, which follows a single request as it passes through several services
K: A
G: ## The Google Cloud Observability suite
Google Cloud Observability (formerly Cloud Operations suite, and earlier Stackdriver) groups tools that let teams understand how their systems behave:
- **Cloud Monitoring**: metrics, dashboards and alerts about performance and health.
- **Cloud Logging**: collects, stores, searches and analyzes log data.
- **Cloud Trace**: shows how a request flows through services and where latency accumulates.
- **Cloud Profiler**: a low-overhead profiler that continuously gathers CPU usage and memory allocation from production applications and attributes them to the source code.
- **Error Reporting**: aggregates application error events into groups based on root cause.
## Reading the scenario
The question is where resources are consumed in the code. Profiling answers that.
W: Cloud Profiler: it samples production applications with low overhead and attributes CPU and memory use to the code that causes it, which tells developers where to optimize.
X: B=Error Reporting: it groups errors and counts them, but a slow, healthy application produces no errors to group and it does not measure resource use.
C=Cloud Logging: logs record events and messages. They do not attribute CPU and memory consumption to specific functions.
D=Cloud Trace: it shows the path and timing of a request across services, which finds slow calls between services, not the resource-hungry lines of code inside one.
T: Profiler shows which code uses CPU and memory; Trace shows where a request spends time.
V: Cloud Profiler, Google Cloud Observability

@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.2 applied use-case
S: An online store built from several microservices receives complaints that checkout is slow. No service reports errors and all of them look healthy individually. The operations lead wants to see how long a single customer request spends in each service it passes through, to find the slowest step. Which tool fits best?
A: Cloud Profiler, because it shows how much CPU each service uses over the whole week
B: Cloud Trace, which follows a request across services and shows how much latency each step adds
C: Error Reporting, because slow checkout is an error that it would group by root cause
D: A weekly cost report, because slow requests always mean the services were expensive to run
K: B
G: ## Distributed tracing
In a system of microservices a single user action touches many services. When it is slow, averages per service can all look fine while the total is poor. **Cloud Trace** records the path of a request as a trace made of spans, so you can view latency trends, inspect individual traces and find the bottleneck.
## Observability tools compared
- Trace: where does a request spend its time across services?
- Profiler: which code inside a service uses CPU and memory?
- Error Reporting: what failures are occurring and how often?
- Logging: what happened, in detail?
- Monitoring: are metrics within expected ranges, and who should be alerted?
W: Cloud Trace: it follows one request through all the services involved and shows the latency of each step, which reveals the slowest hop in the checkout path.
X: A=Cloud Profiler: it shows resource use inside code over time, not the timing of one request across services.
C=Error Reporting: the stem says no errors are reported. Slowness is not an application error event, so there is nothing to group.
D=Weekly cost report: cost data does not explain request latency, and the claim that slow requests mean expensive services is not true.
T: Use Cloud Trace to find which step in a multi-service request adds the delay.
V: Cloud Trace

@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.2 applied use-case
S: After a new release, an application throws a mix of exceptions. The development manager wants a view that shows which kinds of errors are happening, how often each occurs and when a new type first appears, rather than reading thousands of raw messages. Which tool provides this?
A: Cloud Monitoring uptime checks, which only report whether the website responded to probes sent from various locations
B: Cloud Profiler, which shows where the code spends its CPU time and memory rather than listing and grouping exceptions
C: Error Reporting, which aggregates application error events into groups by root cause and lets teams triage them
D: Cloud Trace, which charts request latency but does not categorize exceptions
K: C
G: ## Error Reporting
Error Reporting helps teams identify, prioritize and triage issues across cloud services. It aggregates individual error events, each with a timestamp, context and message, into **error groups** based on root cause. Instead of thousands of similar messages, the team sees a short list of distinct problems with counts and first-seen information. It builds on Cloud Logging, because it can find errors in log entries as well as receive them directly.
## When to use which
- Raw detail of every event: Cloud Logging.
- Categorized, counted exception types: Error Reporting.
- Slow requests: Cloud Trace.
- CPU and memory by code: Cloud Profiler.
W: Error Reporting: it groups many error events into a few root-cause groups with counts, so the manager sees which problems exist and how often they occur.
X: A=Uptime checks: they report whether a site responds, not the types and counts of exceptions inside the application.
B=Cloud Profiler: it profiles resource use, so it does not list or categorize exceptions.
D=Cloud Trace: it measures latency across a request path and does not group exception types.
T: Error Reporting turns a flood of error events into grouped, counted problems to triage.
V: Error Reporting, Google Cloud Observability

@ GCP-CDL D6 T6.2 applied comparison
S: An operations director wants two capabilities: a chart plus an automatic notification to the on-call team whenever the average response time rises above an agreed level, and a searchable record of exactly what each application and the platform did around the time of an incident. Which combination of tools matches these needs?

G: ## SLI, SLO and SLA
These SRE terms are explained in Google Cloud's SRE fundamentals article:
- **Service level indicator (SLI)**: a direct measurement of a service's behavior, for example the frequency of successful requests or probes.
- **Service level objective (SLO)**: a precise numerical target for the SLI, such as availability. Discussions about whether the service is reliable enough are framed against the SLO.

## Why the SLA is often looser
The availability level in an SLA is normally looser than the internal SLO (the article gives 99.9 percent against an internal 99.95 percent) so that the team has room to react before it breaches the contract.
## DevOps and SRE link
SRE ties reliability to business objectives. Every service should have an SLO, because without one nobody can decide whether to invest in more reliability or in faster feature delivery.
