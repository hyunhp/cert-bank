@ GCP-CDL D1 T1.1 advanced use-case
S: A humanitarian nonprofit must launch a registration app for displaced families within days after each disaster, in whichever country is affected. Demand is impossible to predict: it may serve a few hundred people or several hundred thousand. Donors insist that their money is not spent on equipment that sits idle between emergencies. Which TWO cloud benefits address these constraints MOST directly? (Select TWO)
A: Speed and agility, because resources can be provisioned in minutes in a suitable region instead of ordering and installing hardware
B: Elastic scalability with pay-as-you-go pricing, because capacity follows actual demand and is released when the emergency ends
C: Guaranteed lower total cost than any on-premises option, because cloud providers price every single workload below the cost of owned hardware
D: Automatic legal compliance in every country, because a cloud provider takes over the nonprofit's data protection obligations
E: Unlimited free capacity for charities, because cloud providers waive usage charges for any humanitarian workload by default
K: A,B
G: ## Matching cloud benefits to business constraints
The exam guide lists the main benefits of cloud: scalability, cost-effectiveness, agility, speed, flexibility, security, global reach and high availability, data-driven insights and strategic focus. A good answer names the benefit that removes the constraint actually stated.
- **Speed and agility**: infrastructure is available on demand. A team can create servers, databases or a serverless service in minutes in a region close to the users, instead of buying, shipping and installing hardware.
- **Elastic scalability and pay-as-you-go**: capacity grows when demand grows and shrinks when it falls, and you pay for what you use. Nothing has to be sized and paid for in advance for the worst case.
## What cloud does not promise
Cloud is not automatically cheaper for every workload, it does not transfer legal obligations to the provider, and usage is billed unless a specific program says otherwise.
## This scenario
Two constraints: launch within days anywhere (speed, global regions) and no idle spend between emergencies with unknown demand (elastic, pay per use).
W: A=Speed and agility: on-demand provisioning in an existing region near the disaster lets the app go live in days, which hardware purchases could never do.
B=Elastic pay-as-you-go: capacity scales with the real number of registrations and is released afterwards, so donors do not pay for idle equipment.
X: C=Guaranteed lower cost: cloud cost depends on how resources are used; no provider guarantees that every workload is cheaper than owned hardware.
D=Automatic compliance: the customer stays responsible for meeting each country's data rules; the provider supplies tools and certified infrastructure, not the obligation itself.
E=Free capacity for charities: usage is billed; any nonprofit program is a separate offer, not a default waiver of charges.
T: Speed (on-demand provisioning) and elastic pay-as-you-go are the benefits that fit unpredictable, short-notice workloads with no budget for idle capacity.
V: Google Cloud

@ GCP-CDL D1 T1.1 advanced tradeoff
S: A distribution company runs its warehouse system on the same number of servers 24 hours a day, every day, with almost no change in load. The CFO approved the cloud move expecting the bill to fall simply because cloud is pay-as-you-go. The CIO must set realistic expectations before the business case is signed. Which statement about cost-effectiveness is the MOST accurate guidance for this workload?
A: Savings will come automatically from elasticity, because cloud providers scale every steady workload down at night without any planning effort
B: Savings will come from rightsizing, managed services and commitment-based discounts, because a constant load gains little from elasticity alone
C: Savings are impossible for steady workloads, so the company should keep this system on premises and move only its seasonal applications
D: Savings will come from moving the bill to operating expense, because operating expense is always smaller than the yearly depreciation of owned servers
K: B
G: ## Where cloud savings actually come from
Pay-as-you-go saves most when demand varies: you stop paying when resources are not needed. A workload that runs at the same size all the time gets little from that effect, but it can still become cheaper.
- **Rightsizing**: on premises, servers are often bought larger than needed. In the cloud you can choose the size that matches measured use and change it later.
- **Managed services**: a managed database or platform removes patching, backup and hardware work, which lowers the people and facility costs in the total cost of ownership (TCO).
- **Commitment-based discounts**: Compute Engine committed use discounts give deeply discounted prices in return for a one-year or three-year commitment; they are designed for predictable, steady-state usage.
## OpEx is a change of accounting, not a discount
Moving from capital expense (buying servers) to operating expense (paying monthly) changes when and how costs appear; it does not make them smaller by itself.
W: Rightsizing, managed services and commitments: a steady workload saves through better-sized resources, less operational work and discounts made for predictable usage, not through elasticity.
X: A=Automatic elasticity: nothing scales a constant workload down by itself, and a load that never changes leaves nothing to scale away at night.
C=Savings impossible: steady workloads can still save through rightsizing, managed services and committed use discounts; keeping them on premises is not required.
D=OpEx always smaller: shifting from capital to operating expense changes the timing and visibility of spend, not its size; a monthly bill can exceed depreciation.
T: Steady workloads save through rightsizing, managed services and committed use discounts, not through pay-as-you-go elasticity.
V: Compute Engine

@ GCP-CDL D1 T1.1 advanced use-case
S: A three-person fintech startup must show investors and a partner bank that customer data in its new cloud application is protected. It cannot hire a security team this year, and the partner bank asks specifically which protections apply without any extra work by the startup. Which Google Cloud characteristic is the BEST answer to the partner bank's question?
A: Customer content stored in Google Cloud is encrypted at rest by default, on infrastructure that Google designs, hardens and monitors
B: Each customer receives a dedicated physical data center, so the startup's hardware is isolated from every other Google Cloud customer
C: Google manages the startup's user accounts and access rights, so no one at the startup has to decide who can see which data
D: Every application deployed on Google Cloud is automatically certified as compliant with the partner bank's own audit requirements
K: A
G: ## Security as a cloud benefit
One reason organizations move to cloud is security at a scale that small teams cannot build themselves. On Google Cloud, several protections are **on by default**, so they apply before the customer configures anything:
- Data stored in Google Cloud is encrypted at rest by default.
- The infrastructure (purpose-built servers, data centers, network) is designed, hardened and monitored by Google.
This is part of "secure by default" and of the shared responsibility model: Google is always responsible for the underlying infrastructure and physical security.
## What stays with the customer
The customer always remains responsible for its access policies and its data: who gets which role, which data is stored and how the application is configured. Certifications held by Google Cloud are evidence for the customer's auditors, not an automatic certificate for the customer's own application.
W: Default encryption and hardened infrastructure: these protections are inherited without effort from the startup, which is exactly what the partner bank asked about.
X: B=Dedicated data center: Google Cloud is a multi-tenant public cloud; customers are isolated logically, not by receiving their own physical data center.
C=Google manages access rights: deciding who can access which data is always the customer's responsibility, even on fully managed services.
D=Automatic certification: Google's certifications cover its own infrastructure and services; the startup must still show that its application meets the bank's requirements.
T: Default encryption at rest and Google-hardened infrastructure are protections a small team inherits without extra work; access control stays with the customer.
V: Google Cloud

@ GCP-CDL D1 T1.1 advanced tradeoff
S: A 90-year-old insurer wants to transform how it serves customers. Its most valuable policy data lives in a mainframe that cannot be switched off for at least three years, and most of its IT staff have never worked with cloud services. The board wants visible customer benefits within a year. Which approach BEST balances these constraints?
A: Replace the mainframe in one large project first, and start building any customer-facing services only after the new core system is complete
B: Wait until every IT employee holds a cloud certification, and only then begin moving any workload or data out of the existing environment
C: Connect the mainframe to the cloud in a hybrid setup, build new customer services there in phases, and train staff alongside each phase
D: Buy a ready-made software package for every business process, so that the company no longer needs its own data or its own IT staff
K: C
G: ## Digital transformation challenges
Transformation is not only a technology project. Common obstacles are **legacy systems** that cannot move at once, **skills gaps** in the workforce, and **culture** that resists change. Successful programs deliver value step by step instead of waiting for everything to be ready.
## Hybrid and phased delivery
- A hybrid setup connects existing on-premises systems to the cloud, so new services can use legacy data while the old system keeps running.
- Delivering in phases gives the business early, visible results and lowers risk.
- Training people alongside real projects closes the skills gap faster than training in isolation.
## This scenario
The mainframe must stay for years and staff lack cloud skills, yet the board wants benefits within a year. Only a phased, hybrid approach with learning built in meets all three.
W: Hybrid, phased, with training: new customer services in the cloud can use mainframe data now, deliver value within a year and let staff learn on real work.
X: A=Replace the mainframe first: the mainframe cannot be switched off for three years, so customers would see nothing within the board's one-year window.
B=Wait for certifications: training without delivery postpones every customer benefit and does not match the board's timeline.
D=Buy packages for everything: off-the-shelf software can help, but an insurer's differentiation depends on its own data and people; this ignores the mainframe constraint.
T: When legacy systems and skills gaps slow transformation, a hybrid, phased approach with training delivers early value without a risky big-bang replacement.
V: Google Cloud

@ GCP-CDL D1 T1.2 advanced use-case
S: A national tax agency runs its own private cloud for citizen records, which law requires to stay in agency data centers. Every spring the filing portal's web traffic jumps for six weeks, and buying hardware for that peak leaves it idle the rest of the year. The agency wants to keep records on premises while handling the peak at the LOWEST capital cost. Which model fits BEST?
A: Move all records and the filing portal to one public cloud region, so the agency stops operating its own data centers
B: Buy enough private-cloud servers for the spring peak, and lend the spare capacity to other agencies during the remaining months
C: Run the portal on two public cloud providers at once, so each provider absorbs half of the spring traffic while records move between them
D: Use a hybrid cloud that keeps records in the private cloud and adds public cloud capacity for the portal's web tier during the peak
K: D
G: ## Private, hybrid and multicloud
- **Private cloud**: cloud-style infrastructure run for one organization, often in its own data centers. It gives control but the organization still buys capacity for the peak.
- **Public cloud**: shared provider infrastructure, paid for by use.
- **Hybrid cloud**: private (or on-premises) and public cloud used together and connected, so each workload runs where it fits best.
- **Multicloud**: more than one public cloud provider.
## Cloud bursting
A classic hybrid use case: steady or regulated parts stay on premises, while extra capacity for a temporary peak comes from the public cloud and is released afterwards.
## This scenario
Records must stay in agency data centers (rules out a full move), and buying for the peak is the cost problem (rules out more hardware). Hybrid with public capacity for the web tier solves both.
W: Hybrid with burst capacity: records stay where the law requires, while the web tier uses public cloud capacity only during the six-week peak, with no peak hardware purchase.
X: A=Move everything: this breaks the legal requirement that citizen records stay in agency data centers.
B=Buy for the peak: this is exactly the capital spend the agency wants to avoid, and lending capacity to others adds complexity without removing the purchase.
C=Two public providers: multicloud does not solve the residency rule, and moving records between providers conflicts with keeping them on premises.
T: Hybrid cloud keeps regulated data on premises while public cloud absorbs temporary peaks without buying peak hardware.
V: Google Cloud

@ GCP-CDL D1 T1.2 advanced use-case
S: An Australian online pharmacy must keep all customer prescriptions stored in Australia under national health privacy rules. Its checkout must keep working if a single data center area fails, but the board has accepted that a rare outage of a whole region may interrupt sales for a while. Leadership wants the SIMPLEST deployment that satisfies both stated requirements. Which deployment fits BEST?
A: Deploy in one Australian region and spread the application and database across several zones of that region
B: Deploy in a single zone of an Australian region and rely on edge locations worldwide to keep checkout available
C: Deploy in one Australian region plus a second region in Asia, so that a full regional outage never affects checkout
D: Deploy in one zone of an Asian region close to Australia, and copy nightly backups of prescriptions into Australia
K: A
G: ## Regions, zones and edge locations
- A **region** is an independent geographic area, such as a location in Australia. Choosing the region decides where data is stored, which matters for data residency.
- A **zone** is a deployment area inside a region. Zones are isolated from each other's failures, so spreading an application across several zones of one region protects it when one zone fails.
- **Edge locations** (points of presence) bring Google's network and caching close to users; they speed up delivery but do not run the application or store its records.
## Choosing the simplest fit
A multi-region design protects against a whole-region outage, but it adds cost and complexity and, here, may move data outside Australia. The requirement is only a single data center area failure, which a multi-zone deployment in one region already covers.
W: One Australian region, several zones: data stays in Australia and the loss of one zone does not stop checkout, with no extra regions to operate.
X: B=Single zone plus edge: edge locations cache content but do not run checkout or the database, so a zone failure would still stop sales.
C=Second region in Asia: protects against a regional outage that was not required, adds complexity, and stores data outside Australia.
D=Asian region with backups: primary prescriptions would be stored outside Australia, and one zone gives no protection against a zone failure.
T: Use several zones in one in-country region to survive a zone failure while keeping data resident; add regions only when regional outages must be covered.
V: Google Cloud

@ GCP-CDL D1 T1.2 advanced comparison
S: A packaging manufacturer moves a 15-year-old quality-control application whose vendor requires a specific operating system version and a licensed agent installed on the server. The manufacturer wants to stop buying and maintaining hardware, but its IT team must keep full control of the operating system and installed software. Which service model gives the needed control with the LEAST loss of flexibility?
A: Software as a service, because the provider runs the whole application and the manufacturer only manages user accounts and its own data
B: Platform as a service, because the provider manages the operating system while the team deploys only its application code to the runtime
C: Infrastructure as a service, because the provider supplies virtual machines while the team controls the operating system and software
D: Function as a service, because small event-driven functions replace the vendor application and the provider patches every runtime for the team
K: C
G: ## Service models and control
- **IaaS** (for example Compute Engine): the provider runs the physical hardware, data centers and network; the customer chooses and manages the operating system, middleware and applications. Most control, most customer responsibility.
- **PaaS** (for example App Engine or GKE): the provider also manages the runtime environment; the customer focuses on the application and its configuration.
- **SaaS** (for example Google Workspace): the provider runs the whole application; the customer manages users and its data.
- **FaaS / serverless functions** (Cloud Run functions): small pieces of code run in response to events.
## Trade-off
Moving up from IaaS removes operational work but also removes control over the operating system. An application that needs a specific OS version and an installed agent needs that control.
W: IaaS: virtual machines remove hardware purchases while the IT team keeps full control of the operating system version and the licensed agent.
X: A=SaaS: the provider runs the application, so the manufacturer could not install the vendor's software or choose the operating system.
B=PaaS: the provider manages the operating system, so the team could not pin the required version or install the vendor's agent.
D=FaaS: functions are short event-driven code, not a home for a packaged vendor application that needs its own operating system.
T: Choose IaaS when an application needs control of the operating system and installed software; higher models trade that control for less operational work.
V: Compute Engine

@ GCP-CDL D2 T2.1 advanced use-case
S: A sportswear brand keeps its sales history as files in Cloud Storage. The analytics team queries it with BigQuery, while the data science team uses Apache Spark. Today each team keeps its own copy, and the copies disagree. Leadership wants one copy of the data, readable by both teams' engines, with the LEAST risk of being tied to a single vendor's format. Which approach fits BEST?
A: Load the files into each team's tool separately every night, and reconcile the two copies with a detailed weekly comparison report for leadership
B: Convert all files into a proprietary format that only BigQuery can read, and ask the data science team to stop using Spark
C: Move the files into a spreadsheet that both teams share, so that every analyst and data scientist edits the same document
D: Keep one copy in an open table format such as Apache Iceberg in Cloud Storage, and expose it to both engines through BigLake tables
K: D
G: ## Openness and interoperability
The exam guide asks why openness matters for data: open formats and open standards let different tools read the same data, which removes silos and reduces vendor lock-in.
- **Open table formats** such as Apache Iceberg store data in files that many engines understand.
- **BigLake tables** let BigQuery query structured data in Cloud Storage (and in other clouds through BigQuery Omni), and BigQuery connectors let open-source engines such as Apache Spark read the same tables with the same access controls.

Two copies that disagree are a data silo problem. One copy in an open format, readable by both BigQuery and Spark, gives a single source of truth without tying the data to one engine.

B=Proprietary format: increases lock-in, which leadership wants to avoid, and forces a team to abandon a tool it needs.
C=Shared spreadsheet: unsuitable for large sales history and for programmatic analysis by either engine, and it lacks governance.
T: Keep one copy of data in an open format that several engines can read; this removes silos and reduces lock-in.

@ GCP-CDL D2 T2.1 advanced use-case
S: An online home-goods retailer's ad targeting relied on audience lists bought from data brokers, but browsers now limit cross-site tracking and regulators require clear customer consent for any personal data used in marketing. The marketing director wants to keep personalizing offers to existing and returning shoppers while staying compliant. Which data source should the retailer invest in FIRST?
A: Third-party data, by buying larger audience lists from more brokers so that each campaign reaches more people than before
B: Second-party data, by forming a sharing agreement with a complementary brand and targeting that brand's customer list in campaigns
C: First-party data such as purchases, loyalty activity and site behavior that customers share directly with the retailer under consent
D: Contextual targeting only, placing ads beside related content without using any information about the retailer's own shoppers

- **First-party data**: collected directly by the organization from its own customers, for example purchases, loyalty-program activity and website behavior. The organization knows how it was collected and can obtain consent.

D=Contextual targeting only: privacy-friendly, but it uses no data about the retailer's shoppers, so it cannot personalize offers to existing and returning customers.
T: When tracking and consent rules tighten, first-party data collected with consent becomes the most valuable and compliant source for personalization.
V: Google Cloud

@ GCP-CDL D2 T2.2 advanced use-case
S: A law firm must keep signed case files in Cloud Storage for ten years after each case closes. Files are almost never opened again; fewer than one in a thousand is requested in a typical year. When a court does request one, a paralegal must download it within minutes. The firm wants the LOWEST storage cost that still meets this retrieval need. Which storage class fits BEST?
A: Standard storage, because only the class for frequently accessed data can return a file within minutes of a request
B: Nearline storage, because files that are read about once a month are the cheapest class that can be downloaded quickly
C: Archive storage, because it has the lowest storage cost and still returns data within milliseconds rather than hours
D: Coldline storage, because Archive storage keeps data offline and needs several hours to restore before it can be read
K: C
G: ## Cloud Storage classes for infrequent access
Four location-flexible classes trade storage price against access cost:
- **Standard**: frequently accessed ("hot") data, no minimum storage duration.
- **Nearline**: read about once a month or less; 30-day minimum storage duration; retrieval fees.
- **Coldline**: read at most once a quarter; 90-day minimum; retrieval fees.
- **Archive**: read less than once a year; 365-day minimum; the lowest storage cost; higher access cost.
(A fifth class, Rapid, is only for zonal buckets and high-performance workloads.)
## Archive is still online
Unlike the coldest storage services of some other providers, Google Cloud's Archive storage returns data **within milliseconds, not hours or days**. The trade-off is cost per access and the minimum storage duration, not waiting time.
## This scenario
Ten-year retention and very rare reads point to Archive; the "within minutes" requirement does not rule it out.
W: Archive storage: lowest at-rest cost for data kept for years and rarely read, and files are available within milliseconds when a court asks.
X: A=Standard only for fast access: every class returns data quickly; Standard is the most expensive choice for files that are almost never read.
B=Nearline: fits monthly access; for files read less than once a year it costs more to store than Archive.
D=Coldline because Archive is offline: Archive in Cloud Storage is not offline; it serves data in milliseconds, so this reasoning is wrong and Coldline costs more to store.
T: Cloud Storage Archive is the cheapest class for long retention and still serves data in milliseconds; its cost is in access fees and the 365-day minimum.
V: Cloud Storage

@ GCP-CDL D2 T2.2 advanced comparison
S: A media company is planning three new data stores. Store 1 holds billions of listening events per day and must answer single-key lookups for a user's recent history in milliseconds. Store 2 is a mobile app's user content that must sync in real time and work offline. Store 3 is a booking ledger written from several continents that needs strong consistency everywhere. Which THREE matches are correct? (Select THREE)
A: Store 1 on Bigtable, a NoSQL wide-column database built for very large single-keyed data at low latency
B: Store 2 on Firestore, a serverless document database with real-time synchronization and offline support for mobile apps
C: Store 3 on Spanner, a relational database that scales horizontally across regions with strong consistency
D: Store 1 on Cloud SQL, a managed relational database that scales up best for billions of writes each day
E: Store 2 on BigQuery, a serverless data warehouse that pushes changed rows to offline phones as soon as they reconnect to the network
F: Store 3 on Bigtable, a key-value store that provides multi-table relational transactions with SQL joins
K: A,B,C
G: ## Matching database products to workloads
- **Bigtable**: NoSQL wide-column, key-value store. Scales to billions of rows and petabytes, with high read and write throughput at low latency for single-keyed data. Typical uses: time series, event and IoT data, personalization.
- **Firestore**: serverless document database for web and mobile apps, with built-in real-time synchronization and offline support.
- **Spanner**: relational database with horizontal read and write scalability, strong consistency, and single-region, dual-region or multi-region configurations (up to 99.999% availability).
- **Cloud SQL**: managed MySQL, PostgreSQL and SQL Server, ideal for familiar relational workloads of moderate scale.
- **BigQuery**: serverless data warehouse for analytics, not an app back end.
## Reading the clues
"Billions of events, single-key lookups in milliseconds" points to Bigtable. "Real-time sync and offline" points to Firestore. "Written from several continents, strong consistency" points to Spanner.
W: A=Bigtable for events: built for huge volumes of single-keyed data with low-latency reads and high write throughput.
B=Firestore for mobile content: real-time sync and offline support for mobile apps are core Firestore capabilities.
C=Spanner for the ledger: relational transactions with strong consistency across regions are what Spanner is designed for.
X: D=Cloud SQL for billions of events: Cloud SQL suits moderate relational workloads; billions of daily writes with key lookups fit Bigtable far better.
E=BigQuery for offline sync: BigQuery is an analytics warehouse; it does not sync data to mobile devices.
F=Bigtable for the ledger: Bigtable is not a traditional relational database and does not offer the multi-row relational transactions a booking ledger needs.
T: Bigtable for massive key-value event data, Firestore for real-time mobile documents, Spanner for globally consistent relational transactions.
V: Bigtable, Firestore, Spanner

@ GCP-CDL D2 T2.2 advanced use-case
S: An insurer runs its policy system on a licensed Oracle database and wants to move to a PostgreSQL-compatible managed database on Google Cloud to cut licensing cost. The database is several terabytes and agents update policies around the clock, so the system can be offline for at most one hour during the switch. Which approach meets the downtime limit with the LEAST custom migration work?
A: Export the whole Oracle database to files over a weekend, then import them into the new database and restart every application afterwards
B: Use Database Migration Service to convert the schema and continuously replicate changes to AlloyDB, then switch over when both are in sync
C: Move the Oracle database unchanged to a Compute Engine virtual machine, keeping the same license and planning to convert it in a later year
D: Rewrite the policy application to use Bigtable instead, so the company can stop using relational databases and their licenses altogether
K: B
G: ## Database Migration Service
Database Migration Service moves databases to Google Cloud with minimal downtime.
- **Homogeneous** migrations keep the same engine (MySQL to Cloud SQL for MySQL).
- **Heterogeneous** migrations change the engine (for example Oracle to a PostgreSQL destination). Database Migration Service converts the schema and objects to the destination's format, with Gemini-assisted conversion features.
- **Continuous migration**: after an initial snapshot, change data capture (CDC) keeps copying inserts, updates and deletes. When source and destination are in sync, you switch over, so downtime is short.
Destinations include Cloud SQL and AlloyDB for PostgreSQL, a fully managed PostgreSQL-compatible database.
## This scenario
Goal: leave Oracle licensing for a managed PostgreSQL-compatible service. Constraint: at most one hour offline. Continuous replication with conversion meets both with a managed tool rather than custom scripts.
W: Database Migration Service to AlloyDB: schema conversion plus continuous CDC replication lets the insurer switch over in a short window and lands on a managed PostgreSQL-compatible database.
