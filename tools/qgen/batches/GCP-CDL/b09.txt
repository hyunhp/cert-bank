@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied use-case
S: An online ticket seller's website became unreachable for two hours during a major sale. Logs show millions of requests per second arriving from thousands of compromised devices, and no data was copied or changed. Which threat and which security property does this incident describe?
A: A DDoS attack, which floods a service with traffic from many sources and harms availability rather than confidentiality
B: A phishing campaign, which tricks employees into revealing passwords and mainly harms the confidentiality of accounts
C: A ransomware infection, which encrypts the company's files and mainly harms the integrity of its stored records
D: A cryptomining intrusion, which hijacks computing power for mining and mainly harms the compliance posture
K: A
G: ## Distributed denial of service (DDoS)
A **DDoS attack** tries to make a service unusable by overwhelming it with traffic or requests from many sources at once, often thousands of compromised devices called a botnet. The goal is rarely to steal data. The goal is to stop legitimate customers from getting through.
This definition is the generic industry meaning; Google Cloud's security pages simply list DDoS among the standard risks that cloud security has to address.
## Business implication
- Lost sales and a damaged reputation while the site is down.
- Contractual penalties if an availability commitment is missed.
- The loss is of **availability**, one of the three properties of the confidentiality, integrity, availability model.
## Reading the scenario
Millions of requests from many devices, an outage, and nothing copied or changed: that is a flood against availability.
W: DDoS attack: traffic from many compromised sources swamped the site so real buyers could not reach it, which is a loss of availability with no data touched.
X: B=Phishing: phishing deceives people into giving away credentials, and there is no sign of any employee being tricked or any account being accessed.
C=Ransomware: ransomware locks or encrypts files and demands payment, but here nothing was encrypted and the site simply could not handle the flood of requests.
D=Cryptomining: mining abuses compute capacity quietly for profit, whereas this event was a visible, short outage caused by incoming traffic.
T: DDoS = a flood of traffic from many sources that attacks availability, not data.
V: Cloud Armor

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied use-case
S: A manufacturer's finance chief asks what a ransomware attack would actually mean for the business. The security lead says production files and backups on the company's own network could be locked and a payment demanded. Which business implication does the CFO most need to plan for?
A: Operations may halt and data may leak while the company decides on payment, so recovery must be planned in advance
B: The attack only affects the attacker's own systems, so the company's main exposure is a small rise in network traffic and some extra log volume
C: The company will lose access to its public website for a short time, but its files and records remain fully readable
D: The attack changes how the company's bills are calculated, so the main exposure is an unexpected cloud invoice
K: A
G: ## Ransomware
**Ransomware** is malware that encrypts or locks an organization's data or systems and demands payment for restoring access. Many attackers also steal a copy of the data first and threaten to publish it.
This is the generic industry definition.
## Business implications
- **Downtime**: production, ordering or patient care may stop.
- **Data exposure**: stolen data creates regulatory and reputational risk.
- **Hard decisions**: paying does not guarantee recovery and may encourage more attacks.
- **Preparation matters**: tested, isolated backups and a recovery plan reduce the leverage an attacker has. This is the idea behind cyber resilience.
## Reading the scenario
A CFO needs the money and continuity view: stopped operations, possible exposure and the value of being able to recover without paying.
W: Halted operations and possible exposure: locked files stop work and stolen copies create further risk, so the recovery capability has to exist before the incident.
X: B=Attacker's own systems: ransomware runs on the victim's systems, so the exposure is to the company itself and not a traffic increase.
C=Website only: a public web outage describes a DDoS pattern, while ransomware makes the company's own files unreadable.
D=Unexpected invoice: surprise bills are typical of cryptomining abuse, not of ransomware, whose cost is downtime and ransom.
T: Ransomware locks data and demands payment, so the business impact is downtime and exposure unless recovery is planned.
V: Backup and DR

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied troubleshooting
S: A start-up's monthly cloud bill is far higher than usual. Review shows dozens of large virtual machines running at full CPU in a project nobody remembers creating, started with a developer's leaked access key. Which threat is the most likely cause?
A: Phishing, because the key was typed into a fake login page and the bill reflects the stolen password
B: Cryptomining, because attackers use stolen credentials to run costly compute that mines currency for them
C: A DDoS attack, because high CPU is the usual sign of customers flooding the start-up's website
D: A hardware failure, because a damaged server in the provider's data center causes extra machines to start by themselves
K: B
G: ## Cryptomining abuse
In a **cryptomining** attack, someone gains access to cloud resources, often through leaked keys or weak credentials, and starts compute-heavy machines that mine cryptocurrency. The victim pays for the capacity and the attacker keeps the proceeds.
This is the generic definition; Google Cloud treats unexpected compute use as a common sign of compromised credentials.
## Business implications
- Surprise cost.
- Evidence that credentials or permissions were not well protected.
- A prompt to rotate keys, restrict who can create resources and monitor unusual activity.
## Reading the scenario
Unknown, fully loaded large machines in an unknown project, started with a leaked key, show someone is using the start-up's compute for their own gain.
W: Cryptomining: the leaked key let the attacker launch heavy compute for mining, which explains both the full CPU and the unexpected bill.
X: A=Phishing: phishing is a way to steal credentials, but the scenario names a leaked developer key, and the bill comes from the machines the attacker later started.
C=DDoS: a flood would target the start-up's own site and cause an outage, not create new machines in an unknown project.
D=Hardware failure: provider hardware problems do not launch new customer machines, and the customer is not billed for machines it did not request because of them.
T: Cryptomining = stolen access used to run compute for the attacker, shown by an unexpected bill.
V: Cloud Billing

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied definition
S: A security awareness trainer explains threats to new employees. Which description correctly matches the terms phishing and malware?
A: Phishing is a flood of traffic against a website, and malware is a policy that limits what staff may install
B: Phishing is encryption of a company's files, and malware is the key an attacker emails to restore them
C: Phishing is a deceptive message that tricks a person into giving up information, and malware is harmful software
D: Phishing is code that copies itself to other machines, and malware is a message that asks for a password
K: C
G: ## Two different attack types
- **Phishing** targets people. A message, often an email, text or fake web page, pretends to come from a trusted party and persuades the recipient to share credentials, click a link or open an attachment.
- **Malware** is a broad term for malicious software: viruses, worms, ransomware, spyware and others. A **virus** is one kind that attaches itself to other files or programs and spreads when they run.
## Why they connect
Phishing is often the delivery method. A user clicks a link in a fake message and malware is installed, or enters a password into a fake login page.
These definitions are the generic industry meanings; Google's cloud security page lists phishing and malware among the common risks.
## Defences at business level
Training, multi-factor authentication and email filtering reduce phishing; updated software and endpoint protection reduce malware.
W: Deceptive message and harmful software: phishing manipulates a person, while malware is a program designed to damage or misuse systems, and the two are often linked.
X: A=Traffic flood and install policy: a traffic flood is a DDoS attack, and malware is software, not a policy.
B=File encryption and key: encrypting files for payment is ransomware, a kind of malware, and attackers do not send keys by email as a definition of malware.
D=Self-copying code and password request: the descriptions are swapped, because self-replicating code is a virus (one kind of malware) and the password-asking message is phishing.
T: Phishing tricks people; malware (including viruses and ransomware) is harmful software.

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied use-case
S: A retailer stored customer exports in a cloud storage bucket that was accidentally left open to the whole internet, and a researcher found it. No attacker broke any system. Which TWO statements about this incident are correct? (Select TWO)
A: It is a misconfiguration, which cloud security guidance names as one of the leading causes of cloud data breaches
B: It was caused by a flaw in the provider's physical data center security, which the provider alone must correct
C: It shows that customers are responsible for configuring access settings correctly for the services they use
D: It proves that data stored in the cloud cannot be protected, so the data should be moved back on premises
E: It was a zero-day exploit, which means attackers found a software bug that had no available fix
K: A,C
G: ## Misconfiguration
A **misconfiguration** is a setting that leaves a resource less protected than intended, such as a public bucket, a default password, encryption left off, or permissions that are too broad. Google's cloud security guidance describes misconfiguration as one of the leading causes of data breaches in cloud environments.
## Who is responsible
Under the shared responsibility model, Google secures the underlying infrastructure, and the customer decides how its own resources are configured and who can access its data. Cloud services make sharing easy, so a careless choice has wide effect.
## Business implication
Reduce the risk with guardrails (organization policies, automated posture checks), least-privilege access, and review of settings before launch.
W: A=Misconfiguration: the open bucket is a setting error with no break-in needed, and this is the failure type Google names among leading causes of cloud breaches.
C=Customer responsibility: configuration and access for the customer's own resources belong to the customer, so it must set and check them.
X: B=Provider data center: the bucket's access setting is the customer's choice, and physical security of the facility was not involved.
D=Cannot be protected: the cloud offers strong controls, and this incident was caused by a setting, not an unavoidable limit of the cloud.
E=Zero-day exploit: nothing was exploited, because the data was simply reachable by anyone due to its access setting.
T: Misconfiguration (an open bucket, a default password) is a customer-side error and a leading cause of cloud breaches.

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied use-case
S: A bank's own systems are well protected, but attackers stole customer records by first breaking into a small marketing agency whose staff had a trusted connection to the bank's network. What type of threat does this illustrate, and what is the sensible response?
A: A physical-damage threat, so the bank should move its servers to a second data center building
B: A phishing threat, so the bank should replace its email system with a different vendor's product and retrain every employee
C: A DDoS threat, so the bank should buy additional network bandwidth to absorb the extra traffic
D: An unsecured third-party threat, so the bank should limit partner access and review partners' security
K: D
G: ## Third-party risk
Attackers often go after the weakest link: a supplier, agency or partner that has trusted access to a target. If that partner is poorly secured, the attacker can arrive through the approved connection. Unsecured third-party systems are a named risk in modern cloud security.
## Business implications
- The company is accountable for its customers' data even when the weakness is in a partner.
- Contracts and audits must cover partner security.
## Sensible responses
- Give partners only the access they need (least privilege), and for a limited time.
- Do not treat a partner connection as automatically trusted, which is the zero-trust idea.
- Review partner security as part of supplier management.
## Reading the scenario
Nothing was wrong at the bank. The entry point was a partner with a trusted link.
W: Unsecured third-party system: the breach came through a partner, so the bank should restrict what partners can reach and check how well they protect their own systems.
X: A=Physical damage: the loss came from a stolen-data intrusion through a partner, and a second building would not reduce that exposure.
B=Phishing: phishing may have helped the attacker at the agency, but the business lesson is the unsecured partner link, and a different email vendor does not address it.
C=DDoS: this was not a flood of traffic and no outage occurred, so more bandwidth would not have stopped data theft.
T: A partner with weak security and trusted access is a third-party risk; limit and review that access.

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied tradeoff
S: A logistics firm runs its only data center in a flood-prone area and worries about physical damage from fire or flooding taking its systems offline. Which cloud approach most directly lowers this particular risk?
A: Enable multi-factor authentication for all administrators so that a flood cannot reach the accounts
B: Run workloads and backups across separate zones or regions, so one site's damage does not stop the service
C: Add a firewall at the edge of the data center so that water damage is blocked before it reaches the servers inside
D: Encrypt all data at rest in the data center so that flooding cannot damage the encrypted disks
K: B
G: ## Physical damage as a security and resilience risk
Fire, flood, power loss or theft can destroy hardware and interrupt service. This affects **availability** and, if there are no copies, the data itself.
## How the cloud helps
Google Cloud is organised into regions and zones, which are separate physical locations. Running a workload across several zones, or keeping backups and a recovery copy in another region, means one damaged building does not end the service. Google also operates and secures the facilities themselves under the shared responsibility model, so the customer no longer maintains a building.
## Software controls do not fix hardware loss
Authentication, firewalls and encryption protect against attackers, not against water or fire.
W: Separate locations: spreading workloads and backups over zones or regions means one site's damage leaves another running, which directly answers the physical-damage risk.
X: A=Multi-factor authentication: this protects accounts from misuse and has no effect on whether a building floods.
C=Edge firewall: a firewall filters network traffic and cannot stop physical damage to equipment.
D=Encryption at rest: encryption keeps stolen data unreadable, but an encrypted disk destroyed by water is still destroyed.
T: Reduce physical-damage risk with geographic redundancy, using multiple zones, regions and backups.

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied tradeoff
S: A company moves a database from its own data center to virtual machines in Google Cloud (IaaS), and moves its email to a fully managed SaaS product. How does the division of security responsibility differ between the two?
A: With the VMs, Google manages the guest operating system and the database; with SaaS, the company manages the servers
B: With the VMs, the company secures the operating system, database and data; with SaaS, Google carries most of it
C: With both, Google is fully responsible for the data, so the company's only task is to pay for the service
D: With both, the company is fully responsible for everything, including the provider's buildings and hardware
K: B
G: ## Shared responsibility model
In the shared responsibility model, the cloud provider and the customer each own part of security. The split depends on the service model.
- **IaaS** (such as Compute Engine VMs): the provider is responsible for the underlying infrastructure and physical security. The customer is responsible for most of the rest: guest operating system, applications, data and access.
- **PaaS**: the provider takes on more, and responsibility for application-level controls and IAM is shared. The customer remains responsible for data security.
- **SaaS**: the provider owns most of the security work. The customer remains responsible for access controls and the data it chooses to store in the application.
## The pattern
The more managed the service, the less the customer has to secure, but the data and who can access it always stay the customer's concern.
W: Customer secures more in IaaS: with virtual machines the company runs the operating system, database and data protection, while with SaaS Google carries most of the load and the company handles access and its data.
X: A=Reversed roles: in IaaS the customer manages the guest operating system and database, and in SaaS the provider operates the servers.
C=Google fully responsible: the customer always keeps responsibility for its data and access, whatever the service model.
D=Customer responsible for everything: the provider is always responsible for the physical facilities and underlying infrastructure of the cloud.
T: The more managed the service, the more the provider secures; the customer always owns its data and access.
V: Compute Engine

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied definition
S: A customer asks what Google means by "shared fate" and how it differs from the shared responsibility model. Which answer matches Google's description?
A: Shared fate means the customer and Google are treated as one legal entity, so Google accepts all liability for any security failure
B: Shared fate means that the customer takes over all security tasks, so the provider no longer needs to give guidance
C: Shared fate is a model in which the customer secures data in the cloud and the provider has no duty beyond hardware
D: Shared fate builds on shared responsibility, with Google actively helping customers through secure blueprints and guidance
K: D
G: ## From shared responsibility to shared fate
The shared responsibility model says which security tasks belong to the provider and which to the customer. Google believes this stops short of helping customers reach better security outcomes, because the model can be hard to apply and customers can be left to work out the right configuration alone.
**Shared fate** builds on it and treats the relationship as an ongoing partnership. Google builds and runs a trusted platform and also:
- gives best-practice guidance and secured, attested infrastructure code, such as a secured landing zone;
- is clear and opinionated about recommended security settings;
- releases solutions that combine services to solve hard security problems;
- helps customers measure and manage risk, including through its Risk Protection Program (cyber insurance options).
## Business value
Customers who are not security experts start from a secure design instead of building one from scratch.
W: Partnership beyond the boundary: shared fate keeps the responsibility split but adds Google's active help, such as blueprints, recommended settings and risk tools.
X: A=One legal entity: shared fate is a security partnership approach and does not merge the companies or transfer all liability.
B=Customer takes over all tasks: the opposite of the idea, since shared fate adds more provider involvement.
C=No duty beyond hardware: shared responsibility already gives Google more than hardware, and shared fate adds guidance on top.
T: Shared fate = shared responsibility plus Google actively helping customers secure their workloads.

A: Cloud is no more or less secure by nature, and top providers build secure-by-design layered security into the platform

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied use-case
S: An insurance firm discovers that a billing script silently changed policy amounts in its database for several weeks. No data was leaked and the database stayed online. Which security property was violated?
A: Confidentiality, because unauthorized people were able to read private policy records
B: Availability, because the database could not be reached when staff needed it
C: Integrity, because the data was altered so it could no longer be relied on as accurate

G: ## The confidentiality, integrity, availability triad
Cloud security aims to protect three properties of data and systems.
- **Confidentiality**: only authorized people and systems can read data. Encryption and access control support it.

- **Availability**: systems and data can be used when needed. Redundancy, backups and DDoS protection support it.
## Reading the scenario
Nothing leaked (confidentiality is intact) and the database stayed online (availability is intact). The problem is that the values were changed, so the records can no longer be trusted: integrity.
## Business implication
Wrong billing amounts cause financial loss, disputes and regulatory trouble.
W: Integrity: policy amounts were modified without proper authority, so the data is no longer trustworthy even though it was neither exposed nor offline.
X: A=Confidentiality: the scenario says no data leaked, so unauthorized reading did not occur.
B=Availability: the database stayed online, so people could still reach it.
D=Compliance: compliance means meeting laws or standards, and the scenario does not describe any provider losing certifications, only altered data.
T: Integrity = data stays accurate and unaltered by unauthorized changes.

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied use-case

A: Give every employee the same broad administrator role so that nobody is ever blocked by missing permissions

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied tradeoff
S: A company with a traditional network gives employees full access to internal systems once they connect to the office VPN. After a breach, it considers a zero-trust architecture instead. Which statement best describes the zero-trust approach?
A: Every request is untrusted until verified, so access depends on identity and context, even inside the network
B: Users inside the corporate network are trusted automatically, and only connections from the internet are checked
C: Trust is decided once at the perimeter, and a successful login then lasts for the entire time the user stays on the network
D: Security checks are removed from internal systems to speed up access, and a firewall handles all risk at the edge
K: A
G: ## Zero-trust architecture
Google defines zero trust as a security model based on the idea that no person or device should be trusted by default, even if already inside the organization's network. It removes implicit trust by enforcing strict authentication and authorization throughout the network, not just at a perimeter. Every request is treated as if it comes from an untrusted network until it has been inspected, authenticated and verified.
## Why it matters
In a perimeter model, an attacker who gets in can move freely (lateral movement). With zero trust:
- policies depend on identity, device, location and context, and are re-assessed regularly;
- resources are inaccessible by default;
- the network is divided into small segments (micro-segmentation);
- traffic is logged and analyzed for signs of compromise.
Google's own implementation of this idea is called BeyondCorp.
W: Untrusted until verified: access is granted per request based on identity and context, which stops a stolen connection from giving free movement.
X: B=Trust inside, check outside: this is the traditional perimeter model that zero trust replaces.
C=One-time perimeter check: zero trust continuously validates, and a single login does not give lasting trust.
D=Remove internal checks: zero trust adds checks at every access point, so removing internal controls is the opposite.
T: Zero trust = never grant implicit trust, and verify every request by identity and context.
V: BeyondCorp

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied definition
S: A new CISO wants to explain "security by default" and "security posture" to the executive team. Which pair of explanations is correct?
A: Security by default means customers must switch on protections themselves; posture is the cloud provider's list of certifications
B: Security by default means protections are built in; posture is the overall state of an organization's security
C: Security by default means data is deleted after use; posture is the speed at which employees report incidents
D: Security by default means only the firewall is on; posture is the number of staff in the security team
K: B
G: ## Two terms
- **Security by default** (secure by default or by design): protections are part of the platform from the start and do not depend on the customer remembering to enable them. For example, Google Cloud encrypts customer data at rest by default, and Google describes its infrastructure as secure by design with layered security built in.
- **Security posture**: the overall strength and state of an organization's security: its policies, controls, configurations, visibility into assets and ability to detect and respond to threats. A posture can be assessed, scored and improved over time.
This definition of posture is the generic industry meaning.
## Why executives care
Defaults reduce the chance of human error such as leaving encryption off, and posture assessments show where to invest.
W: Built-in protection and overall state: defaults mean the safe choice is already in place, and posture describes how well protected the organization currently is.
X: A=Customer must switch on: that is the opposite of by default, and certifications are evidence for compliance, not the posture itself.
C=Deleting data and report speed: data deletion is a retention practice, and reporting speed is one small part of response, not the whole posture.
D=Only the firewall and staff count: a single control is not a default security model and team size does not measure posture.
T: Security by default = protection built in; security posture = the overall state of security.

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied tradeoff
S: A regional bank wants to keep serving customers and recover quickly even when an attack succeeds. Its CISO says prevention alone is not enough. Which concept covers this goal, and what does it include?
A: Data loss prevention, which classifies files and so ensures that an attack can never succeed
B: Cyber resilience, which combines preparation, detection, response and recovery so the business can bounce back
C: Zero trust, which makes the network a single trusted zone so that every attack is stopped on entry
D: Security by default, which is limited to the provider's data center door locks and cameras and does not involve the bank at all
K: B
G: ## Cyber resilience
**Cyber resilience** is the ability of an organization to prepare for, withstand, respond to and recover from cyber incidents while continuing to deliver its critical services. It accepts that some attacks will get through and asks how fast and how well the business recovers.
This is the generic industry definition.
## What it includes
- **Prevent**: good configuration, access control and training.
- **Detect**: monitoring and threat intelligence.
- **Respond**: a rehearsed incident plan.
- **Recover**: tested, protected backups and redundancy to restore operations.
## Business view
Resilience protects revenue and reputation. For ransomware, strong recovery means the organization does not have to rely on the attacker to get its data back.
W: Cyber resilience: it plans for success of an attack as well as prevention, with detection, response and recovery so service continues.
X: A=Data loss prevention: DLP helps protect sensitive data but does not guarantee that attacks fail or provide recovery.
C=Zero trust: zero trust removes implicit trust everywhere, which is the opposite of a single trusted zone, and no approach stops every attack.
D=Security by default: it is about built-in protection of platforms, and it is not limited to locks nor does it exclude customers.
T: Cyber resilience = prepare, detect, respond and recover so the business keeps running through attacks.

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied use-case
S: A bank wants analysts to run queries on sensitive customer data in the cloud. The CISO is satisfied that stored data is encrypted and that connections to the service are encrypted. Which encryption state is still left unprotected while the query is actually being processed in memory?
A: Data at rest, because stored data is encrypted only while a query is running
B: Data in transit, because connections are protected only while a query is waiting
C: Data in use, because processing in memory needs readable data, so protecting it takes confidential computing
D: Data in backup, because encryption of backups is a separate state that Google Cloud does not support at all today
K: C
G: ## The three states of data
Encryption protects data in different states.
- **At rest**: stored on disks, databases or object storage. Google Cloud encrypts customer data at rest by default.
- **In transit**: moving between locations, for example from a user to a service or between services. Encryption on the network keeps eavesdroppers from reading it.
- **In use**: being processed in memory by a CPU. Data usually has to be decrypted to be computed on, so this is the gap that remains.
## Protecting data in use
Google's encryption page says confidential computing encrypts data in use with Confidential VMs and Confidential GKE Nodes. This helps organizations that process highly sensitive data, such as in regulated industries or shared environments.
## Reading the scenario
Stored and transmitted data are already encrypted, so the remaining state is data in use.
W: Data in use: the query processes data in memory, where it is normally readable, so confidential computing is the control that covers this state.
X: A=At rest: the CISO already encrypted stored data, and encryption at rest does not depend on queries running.
B=In transit: the stem states the connections are encrypted, so that state is covered.
D=Backups: backups are data at rest and are covered by encryption at rest, and the claim about Google not supporting it is wrong.
T: At rest and in transit are common; data in use is the one confidential computing protects.
V: Confidential Computing

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied use-case
S: An employee signs in with a password and a code from a phone app, then opens a finance report. Later the security team reviews a record of who opened which reports and when. Which pair of terms matches these two steps?
A: Authorization verifies the user's identity, and authentication records who opened which reports
B: Authentication verifies identity, and authorization decides what the user may access, with the review being auditing
C: Auditing verifies the user's identity at sign-in, and authentication decides which reports the user is allowed to open
D: Encryption verifies the user's identity, and decryption decides which reports the user is allowed to open

G: ## Three separate questions
- **Authentication**: who are you? Proof of identity with something you know (password), have (phone or security key) or are (biometrics).

**Multi-factor authentication (MFA)**, including 2-step verification, requires two or more different kinds of proof. A stolen password alone is then not enough, which blunts phishing.
## Reading the scenario
Password plus phone code is authentication; the access that is granted is authorization; the later record of who opened the reports is auditing.
W: Authentication, authorization and auditing: the sign-in proves identity, permissions decide what may be opened, and the log review is auditing.
X: A=Swapped: authorization decides permissions, not identity, and authentication does not record activity.
C=Auditing and authentication: auditing looks back at records and does not verify sign-ins, and authentication does not set permissions.
D=Encryption and decryption: these protect data confidentiality and are not identity or permission steps.
T: Authentication = who you are; authorization = what you may do; auditing = what you did.

A: Threat intelligence is evidence-based knowledge about attackers, their methods and indicators, used to anticipate and detect threats
B: Threat intelligence is the act of isolating an infected machine and restoring systems from backup after an incident
C: Threat response is the set of actions taken to contain, investigate and recover from a detected threat
D: Threat response is the annual compliance audit that proves the company follows the rules of its industry
E: Threat response is the long-term collection of data about attackers that is used only to write reports

Security operations is the work of monitoring, detecting and responding to threats. Three terms appear often.

@ GCP-CDL D5 T5.1 applied definition
S: A payroll provider sends salary files to a bank over the internet. The security team explains that the files are scrambled before sending and turned back into readable form only by the bank, while a separate network control decides which connections are allowed in. Which pair of terms names these two controls?
A: Encryption and decryption scramble and restore the data with keys, while a firewall allows or blocks connections by rules
B: A firewall scrambles and restores the data with keys, while encryption allows or blocks connections by rules
C: Decryption scrambles the data before sending, while a firewall restores it into readable form on arrival
D: Auditing scrambles and restores the data with keys, while encryption allows or blocks connections by rules
K: A
G: ## Encryption, decryption and firewalls
- **Encryption** scrambles data into a secret code that can only be unlocked with a unique digital key. Google describes it as protecting data from being stolen, changed or compromised.
- **Decryption** is the reverse step, turning scrambled data back into readable form using the right key. Whoever holds the key can read the data.
- **Firewall**: a network control that allows or blocks traffic according to rules, such as which sources, destinations and ports may connect. It protects the path to a system and does not scramble data. Google Cloud's firewall offering is Cloud Next Generation Firewall (Cloud NGFW).
## Why both are needed
A firewall limits who can connect, and encryption protects the data if it is intercepted or stored somewhere it should not be. Neither replaces the other.
The firewall definition is the generic industry meaning.
W: Encryption and decryption plus a firewall: keys scramble and restore the salary files, and rules decide which connections reach the bank.
X: B=Roles swapped: a firewall filters traffic and has no role in scrambling data, and encryption does not allow or block connections.
C=Decryption and firewall: scrambling is encryption, and a firewall does not restore readable data.
D=Auditing and encryption: auditing records activity and does not apply keys to data, and encryption does not filter connections.
T: Encryption and decryption protect the data with keys; a firewall filters connections by rules.
V: Cloud NGFW
