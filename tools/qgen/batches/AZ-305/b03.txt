@ AZ-305 D1 T1.3 applied use-case
S: A company's European business unit has 40 subscriptions, and more are created every quarter. All resources in these subscriptions must be deployed only to EU regions, and subscription owners must not be able to remove the rule. Which solution should you recommend?
A: Group the business unit's subscriptions under one management group and assign the Allowed locations policy at that management group
B: Assign the Allowed locations policy separately in each subscription and add an assignment step to the subscription creation runbook
C: Create a custom Azure role without write permissions outside EU regions and assign it to every user in the business unit subscriptions
D: Place a ReadOnly resource lock on each subscription and remove the lock only while approved deployments to EU regions are running
K: A
G: ## Management groups as a governance scope
A **management group** is a container above subscriptions. Policy and role assignments made on it are inherited by every child management group, subscription, resource group and resource, including subscriptions moved in later.
- Each management group or subscription has exactly **one parent**; the tree can be up to six levels deep (not counting the root and subscription levels).
- Learn's example is exactly this case: a policy on a management group that limits VM locations applies to every descendant subscription, and "the resource or subscription owner can't alter this security policy".
## Why the scope matters
Assigning the same policy 40 times works today but drifts: every new subscription needs a new assignment and someone with Owner on a subscription can delete an assignment made at that subscription. An assignment at the parent management group is managed in one place, cannot be removed from below, and covers new subscriptions as soon as they are moved under the group.
## Example
`mg-europe` contains the 40 subscriptions. One assignment of the built-in **Allowed locations** definition (effect deny) with `westeurope`, `northeurope` and other EU regions blocks any create or update outside them.
W: Management group assignment: inheritance pushes one Allowed locations assignment to every current and future subscription under the group, and owners of child subscriptions cannot change an assignment made at a parent scope.
X: B=Per-subscription assignments: this repeats the work 40 times, depends on a runbook never being skipped, and leaves each assignment at a scope where the subscription owner can delete it.
C=Custom role limited to EU: Azure RBAC controls which operations a user may perform, not property values such as the region of a resource. Region rules are a job for Azure Policy, which checks the resource's properties.
D=ReadOnly lock: a ReadOnly lock blocks all updates and deployments, not just non-EU ones, and removing it for each deployment opens a window in which any region can be used.
T: Rules that must apply to many current and future subscriptions belong on a management group, where inheritance applies them and subscription owners cannot override them.
V: Azure management groups, Azure Policy

@ AZ-305 D1 T1.3 advanced use-case
S: A company is designing its management group hierarchy. Developers often create subscriptions with Visual Studio benefits, and these must not land at the tenant root with no policies applied. Only the platform team may create new management groups. Which two settings should you recommend? (Select TWO)
A: Set a dedicated sandbox management group as the default management group for newly created subscriptions
B: Enable the hierarchy setting that requires Azure RBAC authorization for creating new management groups
C: Assign the Owner role at the tenant root management group to the platform team so they can move subscriptions
D: Apply a CanNotDelete resource lock at the tenant root management group to stop new child groups from being added
E: Assign an Azure Policy with the deny effect at the root that blocks the creation of Microsoft.Management resources
K: A,B
G: ## Default behaviour of the hierarchy
- Every directory has one **tenant root management group**; its ID is the Microsoft Entra tenant ID.
- **New subscriptions are placed under the root management group by default**, so they inherit only what is assigned at the root, which Learn recommends keeping to a minimum.
- **By default, any principal in the tenant can create management groups** under the root.
## The two Cloud Adoption Framework recommendations
- **Default management group**: configure a dedicated group, typically `sandbox`, that receives every new subscription. Learn calls this "especially important" when users have MSDN or Visual Studio subscriptions; the sandbox group carries the policies that isolate experiments from production.
- **Require authorization for management group creation**: a hierarchy setting that makes creating a management group require the appropriate Azure RBAC permission, so only privileged users can change the structure.
W: A=Default management group: new subscriptions go straight to the sandbox group and inherit its policies instead of sitting under the root with nothing applied.
B=Require RBAC authorization: once the hierarchy setting is on, only principals granted the right to create management groups (the platform team) can add groups; everyone else is refused.
X: C=Owner at the tenant root: this gives the platform team rights over every resource in the tenant, far beyond the need, and does nothing to stop other users from creating groups or to place new subscriptions.
D=Lock on the root: resource locks apply to Azure resources and resource groups or subscriptions; they are not the control for who may create management groups, and the root group itself can't be moved or deleted anyway.
E=Deny policy at the root: Azure Policy evaluates resources inside subscriptions; creating management groups is controlled by the hierarchy's authorization setting, not by a policy at the root.
T: Give new subscriptions a default management group (such as sandbox) and turn on RBAC authorization for creating management groups so that only privileged users shape the hierarchy.
V: Azure management groups, Cloud Adoption Framework

@ AZ-305 D1 T1.3 advanced tradeoff
S: A workload has development, test and production environments. Each environment needs its own quotas, cost reporting and access boundary, but all three must get the same security and compliance policies as the workload's other landing zones. What should you recommend?
A: Create one management group per environment under the landing zones group and place one subscription for the workload under each of them
B: Use one subscription per environment, all placed in the same landing zone management group that carries the workload's policy assignments
C: Use a single subscription for the workload and separate the environments into resource groups that have distinct tags and RBAC assignments
D: Use one subscription per environment and place each under the tenant root group so that platform policies never affect development work
K: B
G: ## What each level is for
- **Management groups** aggregate policy (and a small number of platform role) assignments for subscriptions that need the same governance.
- **Subscriptions** are the boundary for quotas and limits, billing and cost reporting, and a clean access boundary.
- **Resource groups** hold resources that share a lifecycle inside a subscription.
## Cloud Adoption Framework guidance
The management group design area says: "Don't create management groups for production, testing, and development environments. If necessary, separate these groups into different subscriptions in the same management group." Management groups are meant to reflect *types of governance* (for example `corp` or `online` landing zones, `sandbox`), not every environment or org-chart box; keeping the tree at three to four levels reduces overhead.
## Applying it
Three subscriptions (dev, test, prod) under the same landing zone group inherit identical policies, while quotas, costs and role assignments stay separated per environment.
W: Subscriptions per environment in one group: subscriptions give separate quotas, costs and access boundaries, and putting them in the same landing zone management group gives all three the same inherited policies, as Learn recommends.
X: A=Management group per environment: Learn explicitly advises against environment management groups; they deepen the hierarchy and invite policy drift between environments that should share the same rules.
C=Resource groups in one subscription: resource groups share the subscription's quotas and limits, and costs and access are harder to isolate, so the stated need for separate quotas and boundaries is not met.
D=Subscriptions under the root: subscriptions at the root inherit only root-level assignments, so they would miss the workload's landing zone policies, the opposite of the requirement.
T: Separate environments with subscriptions, not management groups; keep the subscriptions in the same management group so they inherit the same policies.
V: Azure subscriptions, Azure management groups, Cloud Adoption Framework

@ AZ-305 D1 T1.3 applied comparison
S: Three web apps share one Azure SQL logical server. Each app is deployed and retired on its own schedule, while the database server is replaced only during a yearly platform refresh. How should you organize these resources into resource groups?
A: Put all three web apps and the SQL server in one resource group so that a single deletion cleans up the entire solution at once
B: Put each web app in its own resource group and the shared SQL server in a separate resource group that follows its own lifecycle
C: Put the SQL server in the resource group of the first web app and grant the other two apps access to that resource group with RBAC
D: Put every resource in its own resource group so that each one can carry its own region, tags, locks and role assignments
K: B
G: ## The resource group rule
Azure Resource Manager guidance: **all resources in a resource group should share the same lifecycle** — you deploy, update and delete them together. If a resource needs a different deployment cycle, it belongs in another resource group.
- A resource can connect to resources in other resource groups; Learn's example is a web app in one group using a database in another.
- A resource group's location only says where its metadata is stored; resources inside can be in other regions.
## Applying it to shared components
Here the apps have three different lifecycles and the database server a fourth. Grouping by lifecycle means retiring app 2 is a single resource group deletion that cannot touch the shared server, and the platform team can lock or assign access to the server's group without affecting the apps.
W: Group per lifecycle: each app's group can be deployed and deleted independently, and the shared SQL server sits in its own group so no app retirement can remove it.
X: A=One group for everything: deleting the group to retire one app would also delete the other apps and the shared database server; the members do not share a lifecycle.
C=Server in the first app's group: when the first app is retired, its resource group can no longer be deleted cleanly without destroying the server the other apps still use.
D=One group per resource: this multiplies groups without adding control; tags, locks and roles can already be set per resource, and related resources lose a common unit of deployment.
T: Put resources that are deployed, updated and deleted together in one resource group; put shared components with a different lifecycle in their own group.
V: Azure Resource Manager, resource groups

@ AZ-305 D1 T1.3 advanced use-case
S: Finance requires a CostCenter tag on every resource. Resource groups are created by a central team that knows the cost center; workload teams then deploy resources and often forget the tag. Existing resources must also be fixed. Which two Azure Policy assignments should you recommend? (Select TWO)
A: A policy with the deny effect that blocks creating or updating resource groups that do not have the CostCenter tag
B: A policy with the modify effect that adds the resource group's CostCenter tag to resources that lack it, plus a remediation task
C: No policy for resources, because resources automatically inherit tags that are applied to their resource group or subscription
D: A policy with the audit effect on resources that copies the CostCenter tag from the resource group the next time it is evaluated
E: A policy with the append effect assigned at the tenant root that writes a default CostCenter value of Unknown to every resource
K: A,B
G: ## Tags are not inherited
Learn's tag article is explicit: "Resources don't inherit the tags you apply to a resource group or a subscription." To copy a tag down, you use Azure Policy.
## The pattern from the tag governance tutorial
The Azure Policy tutorial "Manage tag governance" uses exactly this split:
- **Deny resource groups missing CostCenter**: the cost center cannot be guessed from the group's name, so it must be supplied when the group is created. A rule with `mode: all` targeting `Microsoft.Resources/subscriptions/resourceGroups` and effect `deny` enforces that.
- **Modify resources to inherit CostCenter**: a `modify` rule with the `add` operation and value `[resourcegroup().tags['CostCenter']]` adds the tag when missing and leaves an existing value alone.
## Existing resources
`modify` acts on create and update requests. Resources that already exist are fixed with a **remediation task**, which runs the modify operations through the assignment's managed identity.
W: A=Deny untagged resource groups: the central team must supply the real cost center when the group is created, so every group becomes a reliable source for the value.
B=Modify plus remediation: the modify effect copies the group's CostCenter onto new or updated resources that lack it, and a remediation task applies the same change to resources that already exist.
X: C=Automatic inheritance: tags on resource groups and subscriptions are not inherited by resources; without a policy every resource the teams forget stays untagged.
D=Audit that copies tags: audit only reports non-compliance in the compliance results; it never changes the resource, so no tag is ever added.
E=Append Unknown at the root: a placeholder value defeats the purpose of cost allocation, and append is meant for adding fields to a request rather than copying a value from the parent group.
T: Deny resource groups without the tag, then use a modify policy (with remediation for existing resources) to copy the group's tag down, because tags are not inherited.
V: Azure Policy, resource tags, Microsoft Cost Management

@ AZ-305 D1 T1.3 applied troubleshooting
S: A cost report grouped by the Department tag shows separate rows for Finance, finance and FINANCE, splitting one department's spending across three entries. Teams tag resources by hand. What should you recommend so future reports group spending correctly?
A: Rename the tag key to DEPARTMENT in capital letters, because tag names are case-sensitive and the mixed key names cause the extra rows
B: Assign a policy that allows only a defined list of Department values, because tag values are case-sensitive in Azure and reports
C: Move the department value into the resource group name, because Microsoft Cost Management cannot group spending by tag values
D: Store the department in a JSON string tag, because each resource is limited to one tag and the extra values overflow into rows
K: B
G: ## How Azure treats tag text
- **Tag names are case-insensitive** for operations: `Department` and `department` update the same tag (the provider may keep the casing you typed, which can show up in cost reports).
- **Tag values are case-sensitive**: `Finance` and `finance` are different values, so cost analysis groups them separately.
- A resource, resource group or subscription can have up to 50 tag name-value pairs; tags are plain text, so never put secrets in them.
## Fixing it at the source
Manual tagging produces drift. An Azure Policy assignment that **denies** a `Department` value outside an allowed list (parameterized, for example `Finance`, `Sales`, `HR`) stops new variants; existing resources can be found in the compliance results and corrected, after which grouping by tag in Microsoft Cost Management shows one row per department.
W: Allowed values policy: because tag values are case-sensitive, only a constrained list of exact values keeps every resource in one group; Azure Policy enforces the list at create and update time.
X: A=Capitalized tag key: tag names are case-insensitive for operations, so the key's casing is not what splits the rows; the differing values are.
C=Department in the group name: Cost Management can group and filter costs by tag; moving the data into names loses flexibility and does not fix inconsistent input.
D=JSON string tag: resources support up to 50 tags, not one; a JSON value is only a workaround for needing more than 50, and it would carry the same case variants.
T: Tag values are case-sensitive, so enforce an allowed list of values with Azure Policy when tags drive cost reporting.
V: Resource tags, Azure Policy, Microsoft Cost Management

@ AZ-305 D1 T1.3 advanced use-case
S: Auditing must be enabled on every Azure SQL logical server across 25 subscriptions, including servers that exist today and servers created later, without relying on teams to configure it. Which two actions should you recommend? (Select TWO)
A: Assign a deployIfNotExists policy for SQL server auditing at the management group, with a managed identity that has the needed role
B: Create a remediation task for that assignment so that the auditing setting is deployed to the SQL servers that already exist
C: Assign an audit policy for SQL server auditing at the management group so that missing settings are configured during evaluation
D: Set the assignment's enforcement mode to DoNotEnforce so that the policy deploys the setting without blocking any requests
E: Apply a ReadOnly lock to each SQL server so that teams cannot turn off auditing after the setting has been configured once
K: A,B
G: ## Policies that deploy things
Most Azure Policy effects only judge a request (deny) or report (audit). Two effects can change the environment:
- **deployIfNotExists (DINE)**: after a resource is created or updated, checks for a related resource or setting (for example, SQL server auditing settings) and, if it is missing, deploys an ARM template.
- **modify**: adds, replaces or removes properties or tags on the request.
## The managed identity
When DINE deploys or modify changes something, it uses a **managed identity associated with the policy assignment** (system- or user-assigned). That identity needs the roles listed in the definition's `roleDefinitionIds`; the portal grants them automatically, SDK and template deployments must grant them explicitly.
## New versus existing resources
DINE acts on create and update. Resources that already exist are reported as non-compliant until you run a **remediation task**, which deploys the template to them in bulk.
W: A=DINE at the management group: one assignment covers all 25 subscriptions and every new server, deploying the auditing setting through the assignment's managed identity whenever it is missing.
B=Remediation task: DINE does not retroactively fix resources on its own; a remediation task applies the template to the existing non-compliant servers.
X: C=Audit effect: audit only records non-compliance; it never configures the auditing setting, so servers stay unprotected.
D=DoNotEnforce mode: this mode stops the policy effect from being applied during create and update requests, so new servers would not get auditing automatically; it is meant for testing an assignment.
E=ReadOnly lock: a ReadOnly lock blocks every management change, including legitimate ones, and does nothing to enable auditing in the first place.
T: To make a setting exist everywhere, assign a deployIfNotExists policy with a managed identity and run a remediation task for existing resources.
V: Azure Policy, Azure SQL Database

@ AZ-305 D1 T1.3 advanced comparison
S: Resources tagged tier=critical are spread across many resource groups in 30 subscriptions. Teams must be able to update them, but no one may delete them, and newly tagged resources must be protected automatically. Which solution should you recommend?
A: Create a custom Azure role without delete permissions and replace the Contributor role for every team with it in all 30 subscriptions
B: Apply a CanNotDelete lock to each resource group that contains a critical resource and to every new group that receives one
C: Assign a policy with the denyAction effect for DELETE on resources tagged tier=critical at the management group containing the subscriptions
D: Assign a policy with the deny effect on resources tagged tier=critical so that delete requests fail the compliance check and are rejected
K: C
G: ## Blocking an action instead of a state
Most Azure Policy effects evaluate the *state* of a resource in a create or update request. **denyAction** evaluates the *intended action*: the only supported action today is `DELETE`. A matching delete request is rejected with `403 Forbidden`, so accidental deletion of critical resources is prevented at scale.
- The condition can use tags, so "resources tagged tier=critical" is a single rule.
- Assigned at a management group, it covers every subscription below, and a resource that gets the tag later is protected immediately.
- Updates are still allowed, because only the delete action is denied.
## Limits to know
Learn notes that policy does not block resource removal during a **subscription** deletion, and blocks a **resource group** deletion only when the rule sets `cascadeBehaviors` to deny. Locks and policy assignments themselves are exempt to avoid lockouts.
W: denyAction on tagged resources: it blocks DELETE requests for any resource carrying the tag across all 30 subscriptions, still allows updates, and needs no per-resource setup when new resources are tagged.
X: A=Role without delete: this changes everyone's access in 30 subscriptions and removes delete rights for all resources, not just critical ones; it also cannot react to a tag.
B=CanNotDelete locks per group: locks would also protect the non-critical resources in those groups and must be placed by hand whenever a critical resource appears in a new group.
D=Deny effect: deny evaluates create and update requests against the resource's properties; it does not intercept delete operations, which is exactly why denyAction exists.
T: To stop deletions of a tagged set of resources at scale while allowing updates, assign an Azure Policy with the denyAction effect.
V: Azure Policy, resource tags

@ AZ-305 D1 T1.3 applied tradeoff
S: The security team built a new initiative with deny policies for the production management group. Before enforcement, they want to see which existing resources would be non-compliant, without blocking any deployments, and then switch the same assignment to enforcement. What should you recommend?
A: Assign the initiative at a test management group first, then delete that assignment and create a new one at the production group later
B: Assign the initiative at the production management group with its enforcement mode set to DoNotEnforce, then change it to Default
C: Assign the initiative and add a policy exemption with the waiver category for every subscription until the review has finished
D: Assign the initiative and list every production subscription in the assignment's excluded scopes until the evaluation completes
K: B
G: ## Enforcement mode
Every policy or initiative assignment has an `enforcementMode`:
- **Default**: the effects are enforced during resource create and update (deny blocks, modify changes the request).
- **DoNotEnforce**: the effects are **not** enforced, but compliance is still evaluated, so the compliance results show which resources would be non-compliant.
Learn: "Use DoNotEnforce when you want to evaluate the policy assignment before enforcement. This mode is useful for testing a new assignment, reviewing compliance impact, or validating policy logic." Remediation tasks for deployIfNotExists can still be started in this mode.
## Rolling out safely
Assign at the real scope with DoNotEnforce, review the compliance data, fix resources, then change the same assignment to Default. Nothing has to be recreated, and the compliance history belongs to the assignment that will enforce.
W: DoNotEnforce then Default: the assignment evaluates real production resources and reports would-be violations without denying anything, and flipping the property later turns on enforcement for the same assignment.
X: A=Test group first: compliance results from a test group say nothing about the production resources that need checking, and the production assignment must be built anew.
C=Waiver exemptions: exempt resources are not evaluated against the assignment, so the team would not see the very compliance results they asked for.
D=Excluded scopes: excluded scopes are removed from evaluation entirely; excluding every production subscription leaves nothing to report on.
T: Test a new policy assignment in place with enforcement mode DoNotEnforce, which reports compliance without enforcing, then switch it to Default.
V: Azure Policy

@ AZ-305 D1 T1.3 applied use-case
S: One storage account violates an assigned initiative's public network access rule. The owners accepted the risk until the app moves at quarter end; the violation must stay visible to auditors with a reason and must stop being tolerated automatically after that date. What should you recommend?
A: Add the storage account to the assignment's excluded scopes and set a reminder to remove the exclusion at quarter end
B: Create a policy exemption for the storage account in the mitigated category with a description and no expiration date
C: Create a policy exemption for the storage account in the waiver category with a description and an expiration date
D: Change the effect of the rule to audit through an assignment override until the app has moved, then set it back to deny
K: C
G: ## Exclusions versus exemptions
- **Excluded scopes (notScopes)** on an assignment simply remove a scope from evaluation. Nothing records why, and nothing ends it.
- **Policy exemptions** are separate objects that exempt a resource or hierarchy from an assignment (or from specific definitions within an initiative via `policyDefinitionReferenceId`). Exempt resources still count toward overall compliance and are visible as exempt.
## Exemption categories
- **Mitigated**: the policy's intent is met through another method (for example, a third-party control).
- **Waiver**: the non-compliance is *temporarily accepted*.
## Expiration
`expiresOn` sets when the exemption stops being honored. The exemption object is kept for record-keeping after that date, so auditors keep the history, but the resource is evaluated normally again.
W: Waiver exemption with expiry: waiver states that the risk was accepted temporarily, the description records the reason, and expiresOn makes the account non-compliant again automatically after quarter end.
X: A=Excluded scope: an exclusion leaves no categorized, reasoned record for auditors and does not end by itself; a forgotten reminder leaves the hole open.
B=Mitigated without expiry: mitigated means the intent is met by other means, which is untrue here, and without an expiration the tolerance never ends.
D=Effect override to audit: an override changes the effect for every resource in scope, not just this account, and relaxing deny for everyone is broader than the accepted risk.
T: For a time-limited, accepted violation on one resource, use a policy exemption in the waiver category with an expiration date.
V: Azure Policy

@ AZ-305 D1 T1.3 applied troubleshooting

A: Grant the team the Resource Policy Contributor role on the Corp management group, then assign the existing definition at that group

@ AZ-305 D1 T1.3 applied comparison
S: Developers in a subscription have the Contributor role and must keep creating virtual machines themselves. The company must ensure that only a short list of approved VM sizes can be deployed, whoever deploys them. Which solution should you recommend?
A: Create a custom Azure role that contains only the approved VM sizes in its actions and assign it to the developers instead of Contributor
B: Assign an Azure Policy that uses the allowed virtual machine size SKUs definition with a deny effect at the subscription scope
C: Configure a budget in Microsoft Cost Management with an action group that deletes any virtual machine above the approved sizes
D: Apply a ReadOnly lock to the subscription and let a platform engineer remove it whenever a developer needs to deploy a VM

G: ## Azure RBAC and Azure Policy answer different questions
- **Azure RBAC** decides *who* can perform *which operations* at a scope, for example `Microsoft.Compute/virtualMachines/write`. A role cannot express "only when the size is `Standard_D2s_v5`".
- **Azure Policy** decides *what state* resources may have, regardless of who makes the change. Learn: "Even if an individual has access to perform an action, if the result is a non-compliant resource, Azure Policy still blocks the create or update."
"The combination of Azure RBAC and Azure Policy provides full scope control in Azure."
## Example
Developers keep Contributor (they can create VMs), and a built-in allowed SKUs policy with the deny effect rejects any VM request whose size is not on the list, including requests from administrators.
W: Allowed SKU policy: policy checks the size property on every create or update request and denies non-approved sizes for every user, while Contributor still lets developers create the approved ones.
X: A=Custom role per size: role actions are operations, not property values, so a role cannot restrict which VM size is used.
C=Budget with deletion: budgets react to spending after the fact; deleting VMs from an alert is disruptive and does not stop the deployment in the first place.
D=ReadOnly lock: the lock blocks every change for everyone and turns each deployment into a manual request, which defeats self-service without checking the size.
T: Use RBAC to control who can act and Azure Policy to control what resources may look like; allowed sizes, regions and SKUs are policy.
V: Azure Policy, Azure RBAC, Azure Virtual Machines

@ AZ-305 D1 T1.3 advanced use-case
S: A company must report whether TLS 1.2 is enforced in the operating system of every server, including Windows servers in Azure and in its on-premises datacenter, and must see the results in one compliance view. Which solution should you recommend?
A: Onboard the on-premises servers to Azure Arc and assign Azure Policy machine configuration policies that audit the TLS settings
B: Assign the Allowed resource types policy at the management group so that servers without TLS 1.2 cannot be deployed at all
C: Use Azure Resource Graph queries against the virtual machine properties to read the TLS settings of each operating system
D: Apply a CanNotDelete lock on every server and track TLS settings in a tag that the server administrators update by hand
K: A

G: ## Two lock levels

S: A platform team deploys a shared virtual network and firewall into each landing zone subscription from Bicep. Application teams with Owner on those subscriptions must not change or delete these resources, while the platform pipeline must still update them. Which solution should you recommend?

G: ## Deployment stacks
A **deployment stack** (`Microsoft.Resources/deploymentStacks`) manages the resources defined in a Bicep file or ARM template as one unit, at resource group, subscription or management group scope.

@ AZ-305 D1 T1.3 applied tradeoff
S: For a three-month training program, a team will provision identical lab VMs and networks into 12 resource groups in one subscription from one Bicep file. G: ## Managing resources as one unit
A **deployment stack** tracks every resource its template creates, even across multiple resource groups (a stack at subscription scope can deploy to resource groups in that subscription).

- `detachAll`: leave resources and groups in place, untracked.
- `deleteResources`: delete managed resources only.
