D1 · Fundamentals of AI and ML 20%

Most items describe a plain business need and ask you to label it: which learning approach, which kind of task, whether ML fits at all. Getting the vocabulary right matters more than math. Wrong options often name a real technique that answers a different question, such as grouping similar records when the need is to predict a future value.

What to watch for

  • Look for a known outcome in the historical data. Labeled examples point toward supervised learning, while rewards and trial and error suggest reinforcement learning.
  • Compare training and validation results together: weak results everywhere and strong results only on training data are two different problems with different fixes.
  • Keep the nesting of terms straight: generative AI sits inside deep learning, which sits inside ML, which sits inside AI. Having the broader capability does not mean having the narrower ones.

D2 · Fundamentals of Generative AI 24%

Generative AI basics are tested through short scenarios about how models work, what they know and which AWS offering fits. You decide between Amazon Bedrock, Amazon Q and SageMaker JumpStart, or explain a limit such as a training cutoff or token-based pricing. Distractors tend to be neighboring features of the right service, so the exact need stated in the question decides.

What to watch for

  • Separate the two Amazon Q offerings by audience: Q Business answers employee questions from company data, while Q Developer helps people write and understand code.
  • A model knows only what it saw before its training cutoff. Recent or private facts have to be supplied with the request.
  • When deciding whether output can be used without review, weigh how much an error would hurt the people affected, not surface details like length or tooling.

D3 · Applications of Foundation Models 28%

Scenarios cover building with a foundation model: writing prompts, choosing between RAG and fine-tuning, setting inference parameters, picking a model and measuring results. The goal stated in the question (current facts, a steady style, a summary to score, less randomness) usually points to one technique. Options that change something unrelated to that goal are typically wrong.

What to watch for

  • Tie symptoms to inference settings. Output that varies too much from run to run points to temperature, and truncated output points to length limits.
  • Know the evaluation metrics by task: ROUGE for summaries, BLEU for translation, and BERTScore when meaning matters more than exact wording.
  • Judge an application in production by whether people use it and whether business results improve, not by model size or the effort spent building it.

D4 · Guidelines for Responsible AI 14%

Responsible AI items name a concern (skewed training data, an opaque model, harmful content, a feature that should not matter) and ask which principle applies or which AWS tool addresses it. The main split is between Amazon SageMaker Clarify, which measures bias and explains predictions, and Amazon Bedrock Guardrails, which filters content in generative apps. Matching the concern to the right family removes most distractors.

What to watch for

  • If one group is thin in the training data, expect weaker or less fair results for that group; the issue has to be measured and corrected.
  • Check feature importance for common sense. A top-ranked feature with no real link to the outcome hints at leakage or a spurious pattern.
  • Keep the principle names apart: telling users they are talking to AI is about transparency, while veracity concerns whether the answers are true.

D5 · Security, Compliance, and Governance 14%

Security items for AI workloads mostly come down to matching a need with one AWS service: permissions, encryption, audit history, configuration checks, data lineage or compliance reports. The question states the need in plain terms, such as who called a model or whether a setting is still correct. Wrong options are usually real governance tools that do a neighboring job.

What to watch for

  • Keep audit tools apart: CloudTrail records who did what, AWS Config tracks whether settings stay correct, and AWS Artifact supplies AWS's own compliance reports.
  • Access to models and buckets is granted by policies. Network rules, alarms, lifecycle rules and storage classes do not authorize anyone.
  • Tracing an output back to the documents behind it is a metadata question, so think about cataloging rather than logs or content filters.
